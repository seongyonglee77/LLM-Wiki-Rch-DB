## Bibliographic Metadata

- Authors: Adrian Bangerter; Eric Mayor; Simona Pekarek Doehler
- Year: 2011
- Design: Mixed-methods discourse study combining quantitative content analysis of direct reported speech sources and contexts with qualitative conversation analysis of selected nursing handover storytelling excerpts.

## One-sentence Summary

The article argues that nurses use direct reported speech in handover stories to dramatize non-routine events, display professional reasoning, convey complaints indirectly, legitimize decisions, and sustain a community of practice.

reported speech; conversation analysis; nursing handover; institutional storytelling; professional accountability

To explain how direct reported speech functions in institutional storytelling during nursing shift handover meetings and how it contributes to shared understanding, sensemaking, and professional accountability.

# Deep Summary

## Research Problem and Purpose

To explain how direct reported speech functions in institutional storytelling during nursing shift handover meetings and how it contributes to shared understanding, sensemaking, and professional accountability.

## Theory & Literature Review

The paper frames handover storytelling as a consequential form of informal institutional communication and positions direct reported speech as a powerful narrative device for depicting prior talk, organizing recipient participation, and making non-routine events intelligible to colleagues.

- Claim: Handover meetings combine formal information transfer with informal stories that help nurses make sense of ambiguous and non-routine events.
  - Interpretation: The literature review treats storytelling as more than off-task talk: it is part of how care-unit members update shared understanding during shift transitions.
  - Evidence: "Informal conversation during handover serves to make sense of ambiguous, non-routine events, thereby helping nurses construct and update a shared understanding of the current state of the care unit." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This framing justifies studying narrative detail in handovers rather than reducing handover quality to efficient information transmission.

- Claim: The study defines reported speech as talk used to report prior talk, while distinguishing direct reported speech from indirect reported speech.
  - Interpretation: This distinction underpins the paper's focus on DRS as depiction rather than mere description.
  - Evidence: "RS is using 'talk to report talk' (Clift &amp; Holt, 2007, p. 1). RS can be direct or indirect." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: The analytic claims depend on DRS being able to reenact scenes and voices for recipients, not simply summarize past interaction.

- Claim: Prior conversation-analytic research presents DRS as a device that gives recipients apparent direct access to reported events and often livens complaint stories.
  - Interpretation: The authors build from earlier work on DRS to ask what it accomplishes in a specific institutional context.
  - Evidence: "It allows recipients direct access to the quoted utterance and the circumstances of its production, enabling them to experience those circumstances for themselves." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This literature base supports treating nurses' quotations as interactional actions with evidential, evaluative, and dramatic force.

## Findings

The findings show that nurses quote a limited set of institutional actors, that DRS typically appears after a narrative buildup of situations and actions, and that qualitative excerpts reveal DRS doing technical, interpersonal, and accountability work.

- Claim: The two handover corpora contained substantial numbers of direct reported speech instances.
  - Interpretation: DRS is not presented as an occasional flourish but as a recurrent feature of the handover storytelling data.
  - Evidence: "There were 123 occurrences of DRS in the surgery unit corpus and 61 in the cardiovascular rehabilitation unit corpus." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: The frequency supports the paper's decision to analyze DRS systematically across content categories and interactional excerpts.

- Claim: Quoted sources varied by unit, with surgery nurses more often quoting themselves and patients, and rehabilitation nurses most often quoting patients.
  - Interpretation: The distribution of quoted voices reflects local care-unit activity and interactional ecology.
  - Evidence: "In the surgery unit, caregivers quoted themselves and patients most often, followed by physicians, then colleagues." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This finding links DRS patterns to institutional setting instead of treating all handovers as conversationally uniform.

- Claim: DRS is usually prepared by a buildup in which situations and actions give way to reported speech near the story climax.
  - Interpretation: The quantitative analysis converges with conversation-analytic observations about storytelling structure.
  - Evidence: "In other words, there is a buildup of context preceding DRS." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This establishes DRS as sequentially positioned and narratively consequential rather than randomly distributed in talk.

## Discussion

The discussion argues that DRS helps nurses display professional rationality, legitimize non-routine actions, convey blame while preserving professional neutrality, and sustain shared membership-category knowledge within the nursing community of practice.

- Claim: Self-quotes and reported thought let nurses publicly account for professional judgment in non-routine situations.
  - Interpretation: The paper treats these quoted inner or prior utterances as ways of making decision-making visible to colleagues.
  - Evidence: "Self-quotes and reported thought seem to account for the speaker's professional rationality." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This shows how accountability is interactionally produced in handovers rather than merely recorded in institutional documentation.

- Claim: Quotes of colleagues or doctors can authorize decisions and distribute responsibility across the care team.
  - Interpretation: Reported voices of other professionals work as warrants for actions that depart from routine procedure.
  - Evidence: "Direct quotes of other nurses (and possibly of doctors) seem to be used as public displays of the legitimacy of one's own actions or decisions." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This helps explain how nurses narratively manage risk, legitimacy, and peer accountability during handover.

- Claim: The authors conclude that DRS is central to nurses' enactment of professional identity and shared community practice.
  - Interpretation: DRS is interpreted as a resource for demonstrating appropriate conduct and consolidating professional culture.
  - Evidence: "In DRS, we observe nurses 'doing being professional'-that is, displaying the rationality and professionality of their conduct and sharing it with others." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: The conclusion extends the paper beyond DRS mechanics to workplace learning, collective memory, and institutional culture.

## Unique Contributions

The study connects reported speech research with nursing handover discourse by combining corpus-level coding of quoted sources and DRS placement with fine-grained conversation analysis of multimodal, sequentially organized storytelling.
