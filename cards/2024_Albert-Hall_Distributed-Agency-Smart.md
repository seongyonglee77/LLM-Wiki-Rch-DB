## One-sentence Summary

The paper argues that agency in smart homecare is not located in a single human or machine actor but is produced through coordinated interaction among disabled service users, care workers, virtual assistants, and home technologies.

## Theory & Literature Review

The paper reframes agency away from a human-versus-machine binary and toward the situated organization of everyday action. It critiques individualistic and impairment-focused models of agency in care and assistive technology, then uses ethnomethodology and conversation analysis to study distributed agency in real smart homecare interaction.

## Findings

The findings analyze three variations of heater control in Ted and Ann's care routine. They show that Ted uses Alexa to take an active role in joint care work, Ann times her assistance around Ted's attempts, and Alexa becomes a participant or accountable object within the routine.

- Claim: Ted's Alexa use lets him perform his part of shared care work while Ann does other tasks.
  - Interpretation: The smart speaker enables a division of labor in which Ted actively contributes to the getting-out-of-bed routine.
  - Evidence: "This illustrates the distribution of labor in Ann and Ted's care routine and shows how Ted uses the virtual assistant to accomplish 'his' tasks and take an active role in the joint care activity." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This is direct evidence that assistive technology can support agency through interdependence rather than independent replacement of care work.

- Claim: Hybrid agency is accomplished through the timing of Ted's command, Alexa's response, and Ann's embodied work.
  - Interpretation: The action of turning on the heater depends on sequential coordination across human and technical participants.
  - Evidence: "In this case, hybrid agency is accomplished by Ann timing her move of the electric heater to fit Ted and Alexa's sequence closing and the heater coming on." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This demonstrates that agency is distributed in the temporality and pragmatics of the routine, not located in Alexa alone.

- Claim: Ted's repeated request upgrades treat Alexa as potentially responsive to recipient design.
  - Interpretation: His gaze, summonses, intonation, and reformulations show that the device is oriented to as an addressee whose availability matters.
  - Evidence: "The changes in the design of Ted's six SR3 sequences in this episode probe the availability of the device in different ways, treating it - at least potentially - as responsive to variations in 'recipient design' (Tuncer et al., 2023)." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This is key evidence that agency is interactionally ascribed even when the authors avoid claiming full machine agency.

- Claim: Ann's apology treats her help or non-help as accountable within the distributed action.
  - Interpretation: By accounting for not intervening earlier, Ann displays that assistance itself is morally and interactionally consequential.
  - Evidence: "Ann's apology for not having helped also achieves a form of 'hybrid agency' in that it treats her involvement in and coordination with Ted and Alexa's actions as an accountable matter." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This shows how preserving Ted's agency involves a delicate balance between autonomy and abandonment.

## Discussion

The discussion concludes that participants distribute care labor in ways that protect the service user's agency. It argues that trouble with Alexa creates opportunities to renegotiate agency and blame, and it draws design implications against expensive replacement-oriented care robotics.

- Claim: The study concludes that distributed labor can protect the service user's agency.
  - Interpretation: The authors generalize from the extracts to argue that agency is jointly managed across people and technology.
  - Evidence: "Our analysis showcases a powerful example of how participants in a smart homecare setting can distribute their labor in ways that prioritize and protect the agency of the service  user." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This is the paper's central conclusion and directly answers its purpose.

- Claim: Trouble with Alexa becomes an opportunity to renegotiate degrees of agency.
  - Interpretation: Failures do not merely interrupt care; they let participants withhold help, intervene, or assign blame in ways that preserve Ted's competence.
  - Evidence: "Secondly, we found that in moments where the interaction ran into trouble, i.e., when Alexa did not respond as requested, this presented participants with an opportunity to renegotiate their contingent degrees of agency by withholding assistance, by intervening to help, or by ascribing blame to Alexa as a  'scapegoat'." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This makes breakdowns analytically important for understanding agency in smart homecare.

- Claim: Participants combine inexpensive consumer technologies into a functional smart homecare system.
  - Interpretation: The practical system emerges from the care routine's participation framework, not from a purpose-built robotic replacement.
  - Evidence: "Firstly, it is striking that the participants in our data use the participation framework of their care routine to combine cheap consumer technologies into a highly functional smart homecare system." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This supports the paper's critique of expensive, high-tech care robotics imaginaries.

- Claim: The design implication is to observe naturalistic distributions of agency before building impairment-fixing devices.
  - Interpretation: The authors recommend starting from situated interactional roles and tasks rather than a medicalized model of individual deficit.
  - Evidence: "Rather than starting by building mediagenic gadgets that target an individual's impairments for a medical or technical 'quick fix', we might initiate a design process by observing the distribution of agency and shifts between interactional roles and tasks in this kind of naturalistic setting." (page unavailable; source-text-verified)
  - Why it matters: This makes the paper useful for assistive-technology design and for critiques of replacement-centered automation.
