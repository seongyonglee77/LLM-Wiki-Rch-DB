## 'I told you': Storytelling development of a Japanese learning English as a Second Language 1  Introduction

Demonstrating how learning a second (L2) or additional language takes place outside of the classroom or in other words 'in the wild' (Clark et al. 2011; Wagner this volume) is especially challenging. This is particularly so when the research involves interactional data captured longitudinally over months and when other interactions naturally occur outside of the recorded ones during the data collection period. The interactions occurring in L2 outside of the study are bound to have an impact on L2 learning; however, what effect they have is unknown. Such is  the  context  of  collecting  interactional  data  outside  a  controlled  setting  like a classroom. Regardless of the difficulties, this kind of data collection method can  generally  reveal  different  insights  into  second  language  acquisition  (SLA) than classroom interactions (Eskildsen and Theodórsdóttir 2015; Theodórsdóttir 2010).  In  SLA  'in  the  wild'  the  non-expert  L2  participant  has  generally  more freedom over the organization of the interaction and the turn taking, as the focus is usually on the interactional event which has interpersonal implications, and not on displaying newly taught linguistic knowledge. However, the research presented here ¹ (and Barraja-Rohan 2013) and particularly Theodórsdóttir's (2011; Theodórsdóttir and Eskildsen 2011) studies have also shown that at times an L2 speaker may take advantage of everyday interactions as language learning events by making her identity as L2-user relevant and displaying an orientation to language as object through e.g. extensive repair work on specific linguistic items. The focus here on L2 interactional competence represents a methodological  and  empirical  challenge  because  showing  participants'  orientation  to  an interactional resource as a 'learning object' ² is more delicate than showing an orientation to linguistic items. Rather, what can be documented are changes in the focal participant's interactional patterns, which can be tracked over time (see Hellermann 2006, 2009, 2011;  Ishida  2011;  Nguyen  2011;  Pekarek  Doehler  and Pochon-Berger this volume). ## 2  Storytelling

Storytelling is a ubiquitous social activity that cuts across cultures. It naturally inhabits social interaction in all spheres of human lives and as such constitutes an important social action. It is through personal stories that we connect with each other by sharing experiences, events or anecdotes, whether its communicative goal is to inform, explain, justify, tease, complain, gossip, seek sympathy or to have a good time. Ultimately, stories help us to form a sense of self and others to construct a reality and comprehend the world.

Even though storytelling has been studied in various fields, it is mainly in CA that the focus has been on its interactive nature, i.e., on the collaborative produc-

In CA, storytelling has been primarily described in English native speaker interactions and has received much attention (e.g., Goodwin 1984; Jefferson 1978; Mandelbaum 1989, 1993, 2003, 2013; Sacks 1974a, 1974b, 1992; Schegloff 1992, 1997 , Stivers 2008, 2013). For the purpose of this study the focus is personal narratives. Storytelling constitutes a specific and recognizable type of talk (Sacks 1992) that is  pervasive in ordinary conversations and as such warrants investigation. Storytelling involves a sequential activity which Sacks (1974a, 1992) identified as a three-part structure, each part being sequentially ordered and managed: preface, telling,  and  response  sequences.  The  preface  serves  to  project  a  forthcoming story that needs to be recognized as such by the co-participants so that the teller can secure the floor. Further, it provides an opportunity for the co-participants to indicate if they are already knowledgeable about the story source event, and if  so they can choose to block the story. Tellers often indicate in the preface or the beginning of the story how the recipients should react through various practices  and resources (e.g. syntax, prosody, body language), such as naming the location, giving a characterization of the protagonist, or alluding to a humorous telling, which is consequential to understanding the story (Mandelbaum 2013). In this way, tellers communicate their stance towards the events of the story; they express 'their affective treatment' of them (Stivers 2008: 37), indicating the teller's expectations in relation to how recipients should respond to the story.

A story is recognized as being in progress when the co-participants align as story recipients, thus allowing the teller to proceed with her or his extended turn while the recipients withhold their full turns. However, Sacks (1974a) noted that the course of the telling could be disrupted by intervening talk at any stage, and therefore completing a story is a co-constructed accomplishment. Jefferson (1978)

examined the sequential implicativeness of storytelling to the unfolding conversation, focusing in particular on story beginnings and endings. She showed that stories can be suspended or resumed, and can also generate further talk.

There are various ways of narrating a story as recipients may have different states of knowledge about the story. For instance, in multi-party conversations a story can be coproduced, which Lerner (1992) calls 'assisted storytelling', or a story can be solicited by another participant, or reminisced by a knowing coconversationalist prompting its telling by the teller. Structurally stories can occur in different positions in relation to the preceding talk: they can be initiated in first position or in second position that is in response to a question or a solicitation.

In story completion, there is a preference system at play whereby recipients are expected to affiliate with the teller's stance (Stivers 2008); however, recipients may resist affiliating. Recipients display their understanding that the story has reached completion and this may be done via a second story that is relevant to the first story (Sacks 1992). Because stories are joint accomplishments it takes interactional management on the teller's part to ensure that the recipients endorse the same position towards the reported events and this may entail modifying the telling. Thus storytellings are open to local interactional contingencies because recipients can display a variety of behaviors towards the telling, from affiliative to passive to disaffiliative, and the story may not reach completion or may take a different trajectory as recipients may intervene in the telling, derail or disrupt it (Mandelbaum 2013).

Storytelling is a natural locus for L2 learning particularly in naturally occurring interactions as it allows non-expert L2 speakers to not only learn language but also to engage in interpersonal relationship work (Hellermann 2008). Building interpersonal relationships with expert L2 speakers has been linked to the development of L2 interactional competence (Brouwer and Wagner 2004; Kasper and Wagner 2011) and as such is a not a negligible factor in SLA. To date, few studies exploring storytelling by L2 speakers from a CA perspective have been conducted and are discussed below. recipients' role

Wong (2013) showed that an L2 speaker in two separate telephone conversations  with  native  speaker  interlocutors  can  shape  the  same  story  differently depending on whether the recipient displays an affiliative behavior or not, and consequently the L2 speaker exhibits different levels of L2 interactional competence.  For  instance,  when  one  recipient  does  not  participate  beyond  minimal response  tokens  the  L2  storyteller  does  not  fully  develop  her  story.  However, when the other recipient displays a different behavior showing more engagement through questions, collaborative completions, and empathic comments the L2 storyteller develops her story. As a result, the L2 speaker displays a more sophisticated  L2  interactional  competence  employing  a  wider  range  of  interactional devices. With this study, Wong makes the point that interactional competence is linked to recipient-design. The study also indicates how friendship is enacted through storytelling and has sequential implicativeness on the development of the story.

In  a  cross-sectional  study,  Hellermann  (2008)  explored  non-elicited  storytelling in dyads in a classroom setting while students were off task. He studied two groups of differing English language proficiencies. His study indicated that novice L2 speakers were able to participate in the social practice of storytelling enabling them to engage in interpersonal relationship work, which contributed to language development and to build an 'effective learning community' (2008: 87). These stories constitute a social conversational practice that students engaged in while socializing with each other as members of a classroom community of practice and as members of an English speaking community. Hellermann found differences in the production format between the two groups: the lower proficiency students did not produce an extended preface to the story unlike the intermediate students who used more time locators while the lower group hardly used any. The intermediate group also accomplished twice as many stories as the lower group, and longer ones as well, and possessed a greater range of lexical-grammatical resources such as the use of past tense and other pre-sequence markers (e.g. 'for example', 'look'). Hellermann concluded that having larger lexical-grammatical repertoires enabled the higher proficiency level students to accomplish more intricate stories, yet, despite their limited resources, the beginning level groups could still engage in this social practice.

Storytelling takes much work to be achieved depending on whether or not the co-participants align as story-recipients, show affiliation, or interrupt the story. This interactional work is also co-dependent on grammar as there is a reflexive relationship  between  grammar  and  interaction  (Schegloff  1996),  therefore  for non-expert L2 speakers storytelling represents a complex interactional practice worth investigating.

## 3  Methodology

The focal participant was a Japanese female exchange student in her twenties, Akiko, who was filmed while having a casual conversation with an Australian native  speaker  of  English,  John,  in  an  Australian  university.  John  was  a  postgraduate student at the same university and was of similar age. On arrival Akiko had a TOEFL score of 564 ⁴ and in the first semester she was studying first year undergraduate subjects, including English in Use ⁵ , a subject targeting English as a Second Language (ESL) students.

As Akiko was keen on interacting with a native speaker to further her English conversational skills, the interactions were facilitated by the researcher and organized as conversations-for-practicing English. However, no agenda was set and the participants were free to talk about anything. These conversations resemble the usage-based L2 pedagogical practice 'conversation-for-learning' that is founded on the assumption that opportunities for using the language will improve the L2 users' L2 skills, albeit in unpredictable ways and domains (Kasper 2004; see also e.g., Hauser 2005; Kivik 2012; Mori and Hayashi 2006). However, while I agree with the assumptions about the link between usage and learning, it should be stressed that these conversations were not part of a pedagogical context, such

Hence,  I  use  the  term  'conversations-for-practicing',  not  'conversations-forlearning'. Moreover, the Australians in my corpus, not just Akiko's conversation partner, behaved very differently to what, for example, Kasper (2004) described, as there were very few sequences where the interactants orient to learning/teaching L2 linguistic items. In fact, when the Australians were introduced to the L2 users, they were simply told that the latter wanted to meet an Australian; there was no further explanation, and on the part of the L2 users the need for socializing greatly superseded the need for learning.

Akiko and John had briefly met each other socially on campus before interaction 1 was filmed. They did not really know each other, and throughout the first two interactions they were often involved in an activity occurring in first encounters, namely asking questions about each other to establish common knowledge and engage in topical talk (Svennevig 1999), and it was John who often initiated these questions. However, by interaction 3 this activity ceased and more storytelling took place pointing to an evolving relationship between the interactants.

Three interactions were recorded between Akiko and John over a five-month period. When interaction 1 took place Akiko had been in Australia for five weeks and was still in the process of becoming accustomed to her new environment. The interactions were recorded roughly at two months intervals, and Akiko and John also socialized outside the recording sessions. Each interaction lasted on average 20 minutes. Storytelling initiated by Akiko was generally in response to a question in second position.

A  turn  constructional  unit  (TCU)  is  composed  by  a  phrase,  clause,  sentence, lexical item or vocalization produced by a speaker, and it is determined by its syntactic, intonational and pragmatic completion (Ford and Thompson 1996). In relation to this Ford and Thompson (1996: 137) state that 'turn units are complex, [in] that they include intonational and pragmatic cues as well as syntactic ones, and that speakers design and place their turns according to these complex turn units'.  Turns can be constituted of one or more TCUs which can vary in their syntactic  and  interactional  complexity.  For  instance,  at  its  more  basic  level  a TCU can be constituted of one monosyllabic utterance such as 'huh' and in its more complex form by an if-clause, constituting a compound TCU which projects another one. An essential property of the TCU is its projectability, that is, in the course of a TCU a listener is able to project its end by recognizing its syntactic structure, pragmatic meaning and/or its intonational import. TCUs constitute an important locus in interaction as they form the most basic element of a turn where social actions are constructed. Moreover, TCUs reflect grammar in interaction. For instance, the compound TCU with an if-clause such as 'If I was asked what my major was I would say linguistics' shows the complexity of tense (second conditional) and clauses (conditional and main clauses).

Akiko initially responds to John's question then produces an expansion engaging in storytelling about a different outing. Akiko first indicates that she went to the CBD in an adverbial increment ('ah yeah, to the city and', line 5), to which she appends 'and' indicating that another TCU is forthcoming. However, Akiko interrupts herself by producing a cut-off, abandons her previous utterance and changes tack. In adding another TCU she engages in a new activity, that of storytelling, which is prefaced with a time locator (Jefferson 1978) 'the other day', then she proceeds to tell the story ('the other day we had ehr (0.2) friend', lines 5-6). The second TCU constitutes an expansion, since she has already produced the second pair part to John's question, and she is now expanding on her answer, talking about a different outing. Therefore, when Akiko initiates a story about going on a scenic drive 'The Great Ocean Road', she produces three TCUs in the expansion accomplished over three turns: the first TCU (lines 5-6) is followed by one adverbial increment ('from Japan', line 8) and the next two TCUs involve an incomplete TCU ('so we went to- (0.9)') and a completed one which includes a recycle of the previous TCU ('we went to the-the Great Ocean Road', lines 8-9). The 'so' produced after the increment is used as a turn holder, indicating that there is more talk to come. However, once Akiko gives the name of the touristic drive she does not elaborate on her story. The three-turn story from Akiko may have sufficed. Nonetheless John pursues an elaboration of the telling by asking Akiko a series of question i.e., how far they how far they went on the coast, whether they enjoyed it, what they did, etc., thus engaging in a turn-by-turn talk (data not shown). Stories are co-constructed between tellers and recipients (Goodwin 1984; Jefferson 1978; Schegloff 1997) and in this case John seeks to obtain more details about the story. In this short telling, we note that Akiko's grammar is composed of simple elements, such as adverbial phrases, past simple and two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction.

The terminal assessment ('yeah; it's very interesting', line 2) is uttered quickly so that she can launch into her own story and become the primary speaker. Prior to this, John had been informing her about interesting suburbs and particularly South Yarra, which now becomes a link to the next topic. Akiko moves the topic from the suburb South Yarra to a seafood restaurant in that suburb. Note that her second TCU (' . hh la:st week-end ↓ we went to South Yarra?', lines 2-3) starts with a turn holder (inbreath), and the temporal locator ('la:st week-end') projects a storytelling cast in the past, which she sustains ('we went'). Then she alludes to a previous conversation (lines 5-6) which took place outside the recorded sessions as there is no evidence in the recordings of such a discussion. The beginning of her utterance with a relative pronoun is not grammatically aligned to her prior TCU and appears disjunctive. She is thus engaging in a side sequence, a type of  parenthesis, which involves a new social action, that of joint remembering. Its turn format is recipient-designed inasmuch as this utterance is clearly added onto the last one and its interactional aim is to involve John in joint remembering, which now becomes topical for her story. The joint remembering is relevant as it is an interactional device (Norrick 2005) used to draw John into the narrative. Indeed, the storytelling directly concerns him as it appears that Akiko went to a restaurant in that suburb based on his advice.

In  line  8  Akiko  projects  the  end  of  John's  utterance  and  replies  with  an emphatic agreement token overlapping what John has started formulating as a question. The projection of what John was about to say is contingent on her having started the joint remembering activity and thus knowing the content of that prior conversation. The beginning of this TCU is then revised as the agreement token is followed by 'I' in one intonational unit. However, she interrupts herself to selfrepair and replaces the first person singular pronoun with a plural form ('(.) we went') after a micro pause. The replacement of the pronoun is in continuation with what she had formulated earlier when she had initiated the storytelling (line 2). However, in her telling she does not mention who she went with as she orients the topic toward the activity that took place, i.e., going to a restaurant in a particular suburb (South Yarra), which is relevant to John and their common history of interactions. She produces a compound TCU (lines 8-9), which she does not complete in her turn having projectable elements to it ('which is in'), so John can anticipate its possible completion (Lerner 1996) referring to a location. John produces an assessment (line 10) by latching onto Akiko's incomplete utterance, displaying an appreciative stance to the type of restaurant she went to. However, Akiko had not finished her TCU. The way this TCU is formatted with the use of the relative pronoun and the copula orients to a location. The location is topical but it does not need to be mentioned again as John already knows about it. Thus, when Akiko ends her TCU referring to a type of cuisine she produces a grammatically and semantically unexpected TCU completion ('Greece? (0.4) Greece style?', line 11), which is followed by a rather long silence.

Grammar becomes relevant at this point as the incorrect syntax threatens intersubjectivity. In this TCU ending, the syntax is incorrect as she appositions the substantive form ('Greece') to 'style' when an adjective should be used instead. She repeats 'Greece' twice after an interturn pause and a rising intonation pointing to an uncertainty marker (Brouwer 2000), indicating that she is unsure about her utterance. It appears that she was uncertain about its grammaticality because she engages in a repair sequence, which is resolved a few turns later at line 17 . Having resolved the grammatical trouble she can go back to the business at hand, i.e., storytelling and terminate her telling with a positive assessment of the restaurant (line 19). She utters it quickly latching onto John's response token. Then, John engages in the different activity of finding out the location of the Greek restaurant in South Yarra and they engage in turn-by-turn talk.

Initially  when  Akiko  responds  to  John's  question  she  produces  a  broken  start (Gardner 2007) ('.hh I::: (0.6) just (0.2) mm n::ots not hard', line 4), then she smoothly accomplishes multiple TCUs. In an expansion she comments that there is a wide choice of fish in her suburb (lines 5, 7) and in a post expansion, she confesses her fear of buying fish in Australia, admitting that she cannot explain her fear (lines 7 , 9). She deliberately retains the floor to sustain her multi-unit turns by employing various interactional devices: the appositional 'but' used as a turn holder (lines 9, 11), another turn holder, the audible inbreath ('.hh', line 9) and a 'rush through' (Schegloff 1987) (lines 7 , 9). The rush through is a technique designed to prevent the co-conversationalist to take the floor at a possible point of completion and indicates that a multi-unit turn is underway. In this way, Akiko does not pause between 'afraid of' and 'buying' (shown by the latching, lines 7 , 9), instead she accelerates her speech rate to avoid pausing between possible points of completion (cf. Schegloff 1987). At the end of her third TCU, she initiates another activity, which is storytelling (line 11). She uses a temporal locator 'last time' to indicate that she is about to embark on a storytelling, thus John continues to relinquish the floor. The purpose of the story is to illustrate a point: that seeing someone else cooking fish had given her a strong desire to eat fish and enabled her to overcome her fear of buying fish. Hence the story has sequential implicativeness (Jefferson 1978) and the preceding turn offers a preamble to the story. The story spans five TCUs and terminates with a resolution in which she reveals that she could buy and cook fish (lines 15, 17). To pursue her story Akiko uses discourse connectives such as 'but' (line 9, it is recycled in 11), 'and' (lines 13, 17) and 'so' (line 15) for coherence and interactional purposes (as turn-holders) as well as an interactional device (rush through) not observed before.

This sequence starts with Akiko talking about the farthest place she has been from Melbourne, which is Canberra. Her turn comes as an announcement of her travel experience in Australia and is punctuated by a series of unsuccessful formfocused self-initiated self-repairs. Overlapping John, she reminds him that she had already informed him about having been to Canberra after laughing together ('I  told  you  before', lines 7 and 10). This topic had not been discussed in the recorded  conversations  so  far,  therefore  it  took  place  in  another  conversation outside the recording sessions, which incidentally indicates that a relationship is developing naturally.

Then Akiko uses a turn holder (inbreath) to launch her story in line 10. She explains that she took John's advice that Canberra was very cold but it turned out to be the opposite, counter to expectation. She pursues her story despite John's overlapping  her  talk  mostly  with  laughter  as  he  realizes  that  he  gave  her  the wrong advice and the irony of the situation (line 33). This story occurs towards the end of the second interaction. It is obvious that social affiliation occurs not only over time but also during the course of the same interaction. In this story the participants are having a good time as Akiko recounts what she had to do to remedy her lack of suitable clothing. What is striking here is how she uses the turn taking mechanism to sustain her story, having to grasp the floor in overlaps to start her story and pursue it (lines 5, 7 , 10, 14, 16, 18, 20, 23, 37). Most of the overlaps are occupied by John's laughter: first, he remembers that she had already mentioned her visit to Canberra (at line 4 the state-of-change token is followed by laughter and an acknowledgement token at line 6), then he realizes that he gave her a bad piece of advice. Akiko's insistence on the uselessness of the clothes she brought (lines 13 &amp; 28) and the implication of that, i.e., borrowing clothes (lines 18 &amp; 20) has a double effect: showing the humor of her awkward situation and making John responsible for it.

Further,  what  is  of  interest  is  how  Akiko  employs  a  mixture  of  tenses  to achieve different effects: she uses the past simple to relate events and facts about Canberra (lines 10, 13) and the historical present to contrast the reality as she discovered it at her expense (in 16 'it is really hot' as opposed to being cold). The historical  present  marks  a  shift  in  her  narrative  whereby  she  emphasizes the inconvenience of bringing the wrong type of clothes (lines 18, 20, 37), and to  render the story more vivid thus highlighting her distinct experience of the weather in Canberra. Akiko returns to the past tense in the assessment phrase produced in 25, which could mark the completion of her story, indicating an emotional reaction to her experience. However, John does not orient to it as being completed and her subsequent turns indicate otherwise. In line 35 Akiko adds more elements to her narrative to reiterate the embarrassing situation, this time mentioning bringing gloves (line 28), and keeping the past tense as she had done previously (line 13). She then switches back to the present tense to indicate her experience (line 37). It is John who brings the story to completion with a 'clichécum-proverb', i.e., a generalization uttered in the present tense (Stubbs 1983: 24) as he is involved in her story being somewhat responsible for her predicament.

Akiko's mixture of tenses and events produce a nonlinear story, a trait also found in L1 speakers' narratives (cf. Ochs and Capps 2001). Her use of tenses may not be native-like as her story is not consistently told in the past tense but it symbolizes her experience. Research informs us that the use of tenses is not straightforward in native speaker storytelling. For instance, in analyzing a conversational narrative Stubbs (1983) noted that complex tenses mark boundary points in the narrative and the events are told in the simple past. However, Ochs and Capps (2001) remarked that stories are not necessarily related in a linear fashion and tellers  can  blend  past,  present  and  future  tenses  as  they  reflect  on  particular events and how significant they are in the course of the narrative. In contrast to excerpt 4 above, the previous excerpt 3 illustrates a consistent use of the past tense, which is employed to distinguish the story from the extended response to John's question.

Regarding producing multi-unit turns Schegloff (1996) observed that it takes practice, and Lerner (1991: 451) commented that 'the business of the turn unit is not specifically taken up with the job of claiming or proposing an expanded turn'.

Therefore, to produce a multi-unit turn requires additional work on the speaker's part. As Lerner pointed out, turn expansion is not necessarily projected at the outset and needs interactional work to be accomplished, which Akiko was able to achieve. Akiko demonstrated that she could use a variety of interactional devices to sustain multi-unit turns and even accomplish turns-in-a-series, as exemplified by excerpt 4. Indeed, she was able to achieve various stories beyond the two TCUs observed in interaction 1 and her grammar also became somewhat more complex even though it is still inaccurate. Further, the tellings in this interaction indicate that there is a social relationship developing between the participants since their first encounter and during the course of the same interaction where the participants are enjoying themselves and reveal details about their personal life. Getting to know John may have played a role in Akiko being more engaged interactionally as now they can share more common ground than in interaction 1.

## 4.3  Interaction 3

The excerpts in this interaction illustrate Akiko's continued and remarkable progress since interaction 1. They show a long story about a trip in the countryside that is gradually told in the course of the interaction. The story emerges very gradually in a fragmented manner, as it is disrupted by three intervening sequences, and its structure is revealed upon analysis. Akiko does not abandon the story and despite occasioning its temporary suspension she pursues it, sometimes with John's collaboration.

As the story is told and structured in a fragmented way it is reproduced in four separate excerpts below (5-8) and involves a few components: announcement of the trip (excerpt 5), background information with an embedded parenthesis (excerpt 6), event and account (excerpt 7), and a climax with background information (excerpt 8). It is Akiko who suspends the story on three occasions and self-interrupts twice.

Firstly in excerpt 5, Akiko initiates tangential talk via a side sequence co-constructed with John about the pronunciation of the name of the town she visited (Ballarat). Secondly, in excerpt 6, instead of completing her story about her next destination  (Hepburn  Springs),  she  self-repairs  and  abandons  the  sequential ordering of her story. She engages in a new action by asking John a few questions thereby interrupting her telling and in doing so she initiates a topic shift and engages in a different activity. Thirdly, after this sequence Akiko gives an account explaining the reason for her trip to Hepburn Springs and discontinues the telling (excerpt 7). In excerpt 8, Akiko returns to the story producing its climax after providing much background information.

Not only is the progressivity of the story interrupted but also the order of the telling is somewhat incongruous. In discussing rules related to storytelling organization, Sacks (1974a: 344) states that: '[a] joke's or a story's telling having been properly prefaced, its teller should proceed to tell it to its completion'. It seems that Akiko can only manage bits of her whole story at a time and cannot project the entire trajectory of her complex story. As a result the completion of the present story is not achieved sequentially and Akiko formats the story as separate parts and does not string them together to form a complete story in the same sequence. Being able to project a complex story like this one from the outset requires planning, as stories are framed and formatted to project and create particular expectations from the recipients such as, for example, humor in the case of a joke and a funny story (Sacks 1974a), or empathy in troubles-talk (Barraja-Rohan 2003; Jefferson 1980). Maintaining the trajectory to accomplish an interactional project of this nature requires the ability to manoeuver the turn taking system skillfully, which means to return  to  the  story  as  soon  as  it  is  derailed  or  interrupted  to follow a logical sequence and to bring it to completion. It is through experience in personal narratives in L2 that a project like this one can be accomplished more smoothly. Telling such an intricate story in a sequentially fitted manner therefore necessitates a very high degree of interactional competence, which Akiko does not seem to have yet acquired.

In excerpt 5 below, John poses a question that friends would ask each other, such as: 'what have you been doing ' (line  1).  This  type  of  question  is  usually asked at the beginning of an interaction since it is a ritual question that acquaintances, friends or colleagues habitually pose in the opening of a conversation. Akiko's answer is interesting in that it is colloquial ('ah these days'), which is framed as a confirmation check (line 2), but it also gives her time to think about a reportable event. She continues this strategy of searching for a reportable event by starting her turn with a hesitation marker, and then pauses considerably before completing her utterance with another colloquial phrase 'like what' (line 4). A long silence follows where she is in thinking mode. Then she begins her utterance with a story entry token (Jefferson 1978), indicating that she remembers some memorable event ('oh yes', line 6), and announces her story ('I went to (.) Ballarat ba-'). During this search for a newsworthy reportable event, John refrains from intervening and produces a confirmation token (line 3), thereby aligning as a story recipient.

A storytelling is a collaborative achievement, whereby the co-participant aligns as a storytelling recipient but can also actively participate in the telling (Goodwin 1984), and even derail the story (Mandelbaum 1989). The excerpts from interaction 3 demonstrate that at time 3 Akiko was able to engage in and sustain an intricate extended telling by displaying interactional resources not observed so far. In particular, excerpts 6 and especially 8 demonstrate the complexity of the interactional resources that Akiko was able to deploy, such as choral co-production, humorous story and direct reported speech. Her interactional progression was not, however, straightforward. Despite these gains she did not project the trajectory of her story, which was not framed at the outset as 'a trip with a funny twist', therefore she did not communicate what stance she was initially taking (cf. Stivers 2008). Instead, she designed her story in a nonsequential manner and presented it in a disorderly fashion producing separate sequences without an overall structure (cf. Sacks 1974a, 1992). Even though she suspended her story a few times she still managed to bring it to completion and achieve the expected response from her recipient, which was laughter, on two occasions (excerpts 6 and 8). Other gains included a more native-like phonology (indicated by the spelling showing vowel reduction), fluency and variations in her linguistic resources, such as pragmatic markers and her clauses becoming more complex, although she still produced grammatical inaccuracies.

In  producing  storytelling  and  particularly  sharing  personal  experiences, Akiko engaged in a specific social action, which resulted in interpersonal outcomes as her social relationship with John evolved from mere acquaintanceship to some form of friendship. It is apparent that after five months Akiko and John had a history of interactions as they shared common knowledge, and when they did  not,  they  took  steps  to  explain  it,  e.g.  Japanese  style  bath.  Their  evolving friendship has direct consequences for the development of Akiko's L2 interactional competence inasmuch as her social affiliation with John provided opportunities  for  practicing  L2  (e.g.  telling  more  stories  and  producing  longer  and complex turns) thus opening new possibilities for language learning.

## 5  Conclusion

It  is  clear  that  development  was  observed  in  Akiko's  ability  to  relate  personal stories from the first interaction to the third one over a period of five months. However, what also transpires from the data is that progressing along the interactional competence continuum is an arduous process. After five months, Akiko could not achieve a complex story smoothly. She displayed difficulties in accomplishing a long story sequentially, in projecting the trajectory from the outset (in her announcement) as well as using tenses consistently and other grammatical aspects  (e.g.  sentence  construction,  etc.).  The  observation  period  of  the  study may not be long enough to show more sustained gains in interactional competence and grammar. Developing such a competence is a multifaceted and delicate process requiring not only time but also opportunities for the non-expert L2 speaker to engage in and learn from a range of recurrent symmetrical social interactions with expert L2 speakers that facilitate diverse social actions to be accomplished.

The  analyses  in  this  study  demonstrated  that  the  practices  of  the  social action of storytelling are varied, thus offering non-expert L2 speakers formidable opportunities to employ a vast array of interactional resources, which was reflected  in  Akiko's  increasing  L2  interactional  repertoire.  Equally,  storytelling requires  a  certain  command  of  grammatical  structures  (cf.  Hellermann  2008). As Akiko's grammar became more sophisticated she was able to produce more complex TCUs (e.g. compound TCUs), thus grammar clearly plays an important role in developing L2 interactional competence. Therefore, in providing a context in which extended turns and various social practices can be accomplished (e.g. joint remembering, doing humor, building up a climax, impersonating people's voices through direct reported speech) conversational storytelling represents a fertile  environment enabling non-expert L2 speakers to use a range of interactional  resources  together  with  a  more  complex  grammar.  As  such  storytelling gives the non-expert L2 speaker great scope to further develop L2 interactional competence, an observation also made by Hellerman (2008).

Storytelling  appears  to  play  an  important  role  in  building  social  relationships as interactants connect with each other through personal narratives where they negotiate their social selves, by disclosing information about themselves, co-constructing stories and displaying affiliation. Akiko's tellings in interactions 2  and  3  provide  a  convincing  testimony  that  more  than  just  conversation-forpracticing English was taking place. As Akiko's relationship with John evolved into friendship her participation in talk-in-interaction heightened. Engaging in topical talk became easier with the participants displaying topic familiarity. It thus appears in this study that having a social affiliation with an expert/native speaker  created  a  favorable  condition  for  developing  L2  interactional  competence, a point endorsed by (Brouwer and Wagner (2004), Firth and Wagner (2007), and Kasper and Wagner (2011)). This affiliation not only produced many opportunities to engage in a recurrent social activity where there is reciprocity (both interactants share stories) but also affordances (van Lier 2000) whereby the novice L2 speaker was regularly exposed to complex language and interactional resources  not  usually  produced  by  not-yet-competent  L2  speakers.  It  is  worth mentioning at this stage that Akiko was also observed interacting with a close L2 friend, Carol, from Hong Kong (data not shown, cf. Barraja-Rohan 2013) whose linguistic competence was less advanced than Akiko's. In that exchange, which occurred one month after interaction 3, Akiko produced considerably less talk than with John, which mainly consisted of single TCUs, and she did not engage in storytelling unlike Carol. Storytelling, then, offers much potential to further uncover novice L2 speakers' interactional practices. On this basis, it needs to be explored in depth, particularly in naturally occurring interactions and from a CA perspective which provides fine-grained analyses.

Finally,  the  study  has  some  wide-ranging  implications  for  L2  pedagogy. Finding opportunities to interact in everyday situations in the L2 can be difficult, even for L2 speakers who are aware of the necessity of using L2 to increase their L2 learning opportunities (Eskildsen, Wagner, and Cadierno 2014). Conversationsfor-practicing, centered on the participants' needs to build social relationships rather than on curricular considerations, seem to be a valuable arrangement to bring  the  L2  speakers  into  situations  of  authentic,  everyday  social  interaction and language use, i.e., into the wild. This study has shown the slow and gradual development in Akiko's L2 interactional competence in situations that arguably would have been almost impossible for her to find in a classroom. The conversations-for-practicing thus gave her opportunities that she would not have had, otherwise: improving her L2 interactional competence, and from a broader perspective, building and maintaining a social relationship in which storytelling was a critical practice.
