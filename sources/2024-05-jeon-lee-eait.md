## Can learners benefit from chatbots instead of humans? A systematic review of human-chatbot comparison research in language education

<!-- image -->

Jaeho Jeon 1 · Seongyong Lee 2

Received: 26 October 2023 / Accepted: 16 April 2024 / Published online: 24 May 2024 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2024

## Abstract

Research has demonstrated the promising potential of chatbots in education. Moreover,  technological  advancements,  such  as  ChatGPT,  prompted  us  to  reexamine distinctions  between  pedagogical  roles  that  humans  and  chatbots  assume.  In  this context, a systematic review of 11 experimental studies on human-chatbot comparisons in language education was performed, yielding 64 statistical findings, which were  then  categorized  into  11  overarching  variables.  The  analysis  indicates  that chatbots  provide  benefits  comparable  to  those  afforded  by  human-human  interaction in some domains, such as eliciting utterances of similar sophistication, vocabulary,  and  grammar  levels  and  facilitating  improvements  in  speaking  and  listening proficiency. In contrast, chatbots were less effective than humans in areas that may demand socially appropriate interpersonal elements, such as sustaining interactivity, providing sufficient information in elaborations, and maintaining a positive attitude toward target language conversations over the long term. Based on the results, we suggest that chatbots be conceptualized as novel interlocutors rather than as simulations  striving  to  perfectly  mimic  humans  and  that  emphasis  be  placed  on  aspects humans should focus more on in educational scenarios where chatbots are involved. Additionally, other implications for researchers and teachers are discussed to inform future research and practice.

Keywords Chatbot · Systematic review · Language learning · Artificial intelligence · Computer-assisted language learning

## 1  Introduction

Chatbots  are  defined  as  artificially  developed  agents  that  possess  the  ability  to engage  in  natural  language  interactions  with  users  via  textual  and/or  auditory modes (Jeon, 2024; Jeon &amp; Lee, 2023). The growing interest in chatbots within the  domain  of  education,  in  particular  language  education,  can  be  primarily attributed to their conversational capacities, which increasingly emulate humanhuman  interaction  as  the  technology  continues  to  develop  (Choi  et  al.,  2023). Interestingly,  the  fact  that  this  technology  can  perform  human-like  interactions positions  it  uniquely  with  two  advantages  that  may  seem  contradictory.  First, due to their human-like ability to chat with humans, chatbots introduce authenticity  to  the  learning  experience (Lee &amp; Jeon, 2022). For example, their capacity  to  simulate  a  conversation  and  respond  in  a  context-sensitive  manner  enables learners to engage with learning in a more natural way (Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023a). According to Hwang et al. (2022), this interaction not only enhances learner engagement but also provides an authentic setting for the application of knowledge. Second, as implied by the term bot ,  the  fact  that  chatbots are machines makes repetitiveness one of their primary affordances (Jeon et al., 2023a).  For  instance,  this  allows  learners  to  revisit  learning  materials  repeatedly without the concerns associated with human interlocutors, a crucial aspect in mastering new skills and concepts (Zhang et al., 2023a). That is, as Fryer and Carpenter (2006) point out, different from typical human interlocutors, 'chatbots are willing to repeat the same material with students endlessly; they do not get bored or lose their patience' (p. 10). These two characteristics prompt us to question  what  similar  and  different  effects  chatbots  would  bring  when  compared  to humans and this blurry boundary between the roles of humans and chatbots in education is garnering increasing attention, as chatbots' human-likeness is exponentially increasing with technological advances (Jeon &amp; Lee, 2023; Lee et al., 2024).

## 2   Literature review

Human-like characteristics that chatbots possess make it possible for them to play different roles in education (e.g., Hwang &amp; Chang, 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Smutny &amp; Schreiberova, 2020). Analyzing 36 chatbot studies across several domains of education, Kuhail et al. (2022) identified four roles of chatbots as teaching agents, peer  agents,  teachable  agents,  and  motivational  agents,  which  are  typically assumed by humans in situations without chatbots (for another extensive discussion on chatbots' roles in education, see Kasneci et al., 2023). More specifically, in  recent  experiments, chatbots have been used in providing educational videos and following discussions (Qin et al., 2020), conducting formative assessments (Jeon, 2023), scaffolding group discussions (Tegos et al., 2020), guiding students in  solving problems step by step (Law et al., 2020), and motivating students in learning (Schouten et al., 2017).

<!-- image -->

More pertinent to the current study, researchers have also examined how chatbots  are  used  by  focusing  on  the  context  of  language  learning.  For  example, Huang et al. (2022) who reviewed 25 empirical studies on chatbots for language learning found five different pedagogical uses for chatbots: interlocutor, simulation,  transmission,  helpline,  and  recommendation.  Similarly,  after  reviewing  32 empirical studies, Jeon et al. (2023b) found five roles that chatbots play in language  education:  conversational  partner,  feedback  provider,  resource  provider, evaluator, and interviewer. More recently, focusing on the use of ChatGPT, Jeon and  Lee  (2023)  found  that  ChatGPT  can  play  the  roles  of  interlocutor,  content provider, teaching assistant, and evaluator. All of these studies show much overlap in the roles of humans and chatbots in education.

While the findings of previous studies on chatbots suggest the promising potential of  using  chatbots  as  human-like  agents,  researchers  are  still  left  facing  an  important question: 'Can learners benefit from chatbots instead of humans?' Despite the increasing  volume  of  systematic  and  meta-analysis  review  studies  on  chatbots  in education, particularly in the field of language learning, this gap has persisted due to the fact that previous reviews have not employed methodologies with a viewpoint focused  on  comparing  humans  and  chatbots,  instead  solely  focusing  on  chatbots' effects, the advantages of using chatbots, and their roles (Huang et al., 2022; Hwang &amp; Chang, 2023; Kuhail et al., 2022; Labadze et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023b).

Indeed, researchers have engaged in an ongoing discourse concerning chatbots' potential  as  human-like  agents  and  the  degree  to  which  they  can  be  compared  to human interlocutors. Some of them argue that conversational technology can serve as cost-effective, accessible, and even empathetic learning partners, with the potential to replace teachers in this role (e.g., Edwards &amp; Cheok, 2018). In contrast, others contend that the unique benefits provided by human partners may not be entirely replicated  by  computers,  regardless  of  their  level  of  technological  sophistication (e.g., Clark et al., 2019; Jeon and Lee, 2023). For example, in their study with 17 chatbot  users,  Clark  et  al.  (2019)  found,  'Participants  emphasized  similar  characteristics  as  important  in  both  human  and  agent-based  conversation …. Yet they [chatbots]  were  operationalized  very  differently,  being  discussed  in  almost  purely functional terms' (p. 2). Along the same lines, language researchers have conducted empirical studies to determine the extent to which chatbots are perceived as humanlike  in  educational  environments to obtain evidence of their social potential (e.g., Xu  &amp;  Warschauer,  2020).  These  investigations  have  mainly  employed  qualitative research methodologies, such as interviews and drawing tasks across different language settings and age groups, which have consistently characterized the potential of chatbots as closely though not completely human-like (e.g., Girouard-Hallam et al., 2021).

Specifically, Xu and Warschauer (2020) examined the extent to which young learners, aged between three and six years, perceived a chatbot as a human-like agent. In interviews,  the  children  were  asked  to  describe  the  chatbot,  and  their  descriptions were classified as artifact, living being, or in-between object. Approximately half of the participants regarded the chatbot as either a living being or an in-between object, suggesting that they acknowledged the social affordances of chatbots. Building on the theoretical framework employed by Xu and Warschauer (2020), Lee and Jeon (2022)

<!-- image -->

investigated  how  nine-year-old  English  as  a  Foreign  Language  (EFL)  learners  perceived a chatbot used in language tasks. Consistent with the findings of Xu and Warschauer  (2020),  they  discovered  that  over  two-thirds  of  the  participants  identified human-like qualities in chatbots. In a higher education context, Tai and her colleagues (Tai, 2022; Tai &amp; Chen, 2022a) investigated how secondary and college-level EFL students perceived chatbots after approximately 10 weeks of exposure to them in class. In interviews, a majority of the students expressed their perception of chatbots as humanlike learning partners. Consistent with Fryer and Carpenter (2006), it was also revealed that being aware that chatbots were artificial made learners feel less concerned about making mistakes and more at ease when interacting with chatbots than with human counterparts (Tai &amp; Chen, 2022b). In light of these findings, it can be inferred that chatbots are perceived as exhibiting a range of human-like qualities across different learner demographics and language learning contexts. However, although this line of inquiry  sheds  light  on  perceptions  of  chatbots  as  human-like  agents,  it  does  not  go further to offer direct insights into the comparative benefits of chatbots and humans.

Albeit limited, some direct comparisons have been made between chatbots and human interlocutors. However, as will be analyzed in the methodology section of the current paper, they have employed heterogeneous approaches to research design and have measured disparate variables. For example, some studies have compared students' interactions with chatbots and with human interlocutors in controlled laboratory settings (e.g., Timpe-Lauglin et al., 2022) and others in classroom contexts, for  example,  between  chatbots  as  a  replacement  for  some  human-human  interaction  and  only  human-human  interactions  (e.g.,  Dizon,  2020).  Researchers  have also used a range of variables to assess differences in student participation during interactions with humans or chatbots. Some of these variables have different names despite measuring similar outcomes. Such methodological discrepancies in humanchatbot comparison studies lead to inconsistencies among their findings. These pose a significant challenge to developing a comprehensive understanding of the relative advantages and drawbacks associated with both human-human and human-chatbot interactions. This systematic review aims to bridge this gap by providing a consistent interpretation of the existing literature.

## 3   Research rationale and objectives

As Edwards et al. (2018) underscore, it is important to examine what is gained and lost when  human-human  communication  is  replaced  by  interactions  with  conversational technology even if only partially. Indeed, this question is not new and has constituted a central focus of research since the proposal of the Turing Test in 1950 by Alan Turing, the goal of which was to develop human-like chatbots capable of convincing human raters that they are interacting with real humans (Turing, 1950). However, the significance of questions raised by this objective has been amplified by the rapid development of exponentially advanced versions of chatbot technology, as evidenced by the recent advent  and  increasing  popularity  of  large  language  models,  such  as  OpenAI's  GPT series or Google's Gemini (Jeon et al., 2023a; Kohnke et al., 2023; Tlili et al., 2023). Furthermore, as reviewed in the previous section, while many authors have explored this topic to different degrees, a systematic review may offer a unique perspective by applying  a  consistent  framework  to  analyze  these  studies  collectively,  allowing  for the identification of common themes across the studies (Li, 2015). As such, it is both timely and imperative to perform a systematic review to acquire diagnostic information on the impact of conversational chatbots on language learning in direct comparison to that of human interlocutors and to derive implications for future research on rapidly evolving chatbots and their application in education. However, as our literature review of the studies indicates, the methodological and experimental discrepancies in humanchatbot comparison research led to inconsistencies among the findings, which calls for a qualitative approach rather than a quantitative approach, to synthesizing studies that are disparate with different variables.

<!-- image -->

As demonstrated in review studies for adjacent domains, such as pedagogical agents and social robots, a systematic narrative review approach may address this concern. For example, analyzing how one-way pedagogical agents' personas affect learning outcomes, Davis et al. (2021) demonstrate the utility of a systematic narrative review for synthesizing disparate studies involving a multitude of variables, which also applies to the line of research that this study aims to analyze, that is, human-chatbot comparison research studies. This approach was also successfully employed by Li (2015), who qualitatively categorized and scrutinized experimental studies on social robots in education and effectively identified recurrent themes across a diverse range of variables. Therefore, grounded on these two previous reviews, a systematic narrative review approach was adopted in this study to provide a comprehensive and coherent overview of the current state of research on human-chatbot comparisons and to identify potential areas for future research regarding the use of chatbots compared to humans for language learning.

In this study, the term comparison is defined as analyzing and comparing the effects of at least two different learning conditions. As will be further discussed in the methodology section, four types of comparison studies involving chatbots have been identified: comparisons between (1) human-human and human-chatbot interaction conditions; (2) chatbot-assisted  and  human-only  conditions;  (3)  chatbots  and  traditional  instructional material conditions; and (4) different chatbot conditions. In light of the research scope of the current paper (i.e., human-chatbot comparison), studies that fell within the first two categories were analyzed. The following research questions (RQs) guided this review:

RQ1. What are the similarities and differences between the effects of human-chatbot interaction and those of human-human interaction?

RQ2.  What  are  the  similarities  and  differences  between  the  effects  of  chatbotassisted conditions and those of human-only conditions?

## 4   Methodology

## 4.1   Data collection

This review utilized a systematic narrative review method, conducting a qualitative synthesis of quantitative outcomes, which excludes collective analyses of effect size, sample size, and null effects as performed in a formal meta-analysis. This method was chosen to address the inconsistency in experimental conditions and in consideration  of  the  emerging  nature  of  the  studies,  thereby  enabling  a  comprehensive evaluation of the literature (Davis et al., 2021; Li, 2015). The following major academic platforms for educational research were consulted to search for articles for the current  review:  ProQuest,  the  Web  of  Science,  and  Scopus.  They  were  confirmed to provide sufficiently inclusive search results regarding conversational technology, as  shown in previous systematic reviews (e.g., Bibauw et al., 2019, 2022; Hwang &amp; Chang, 2023). The search was performed on March 30th, 2023, based on title, abstract, and keywords (TITLE-ABS-KEY) and restricted to peer-reviewed papers written in English. The search strategy involved the use of keywords such as 'chatbot*,' 'conversational agent*,' 'pedagogical agent*,' 'chatterbot*,' 'conversational system*,'  'dialogue  system*,'  'intelligent  personal  assistant*,'  'spoken  dialogue system*,'  'language  learning*,'  'language  teaching*,'  'language  acquisition*,' 'EFL*,' 'Foreign language*,' 'ESL*,' and 'second language*.' During the entire screening process, we followed an inclusive approach and did not impose stringent criteria regarding the quality of the studies. This decision was made considering the emerging nature of human-chatbot comparison research, where applying strict criteria could have resulted in a loss of information (Arksey &amp; O'Malley, 2005).

Figure  1  illustrates  the  process  of  article  search,  screening,  and  identification, along  with  specific  article  inclusion  and  exclusion  criteria.  The  articles  initially identified ( n =  394) were screened to assess their eligibility for inclusion, based on specific criteria aligned with the review's intended scope. After duplications were removed, the titles and abstracts of 162 records were reviewed. The criteria for this stage of the review included (1) relevance to the use of chatbots in language learning, (2) comparison elements involving chatbots, (3) experimental design with a control condition, and (4) statistical comparisons of language learning-related outcomes. As mentioned, the aim of this study was to conduct a systematic review of human and chatbot comparison studies; however, it became evident during the screening phase that  identifying  such  studies  based  solely  on  titles  and  abstracts  was  not  feasible. For example, some abstracts merely mentioned the use of a control group without providing additional details, making it impossible to determine whether comparison elements  between  humans  and  chatbots  were  present.  Consequently,  only  studies with titles or abstracts that explicitly indicated research designs that did not involve human-chatbot  comparisons  were  excluded,  while  studies  that  were  not  explicit about this focal point proceeded to the next stage to be further reviewed.

Next, during the eligibility stage, full texts ( n =  93)  were  reviewed,  and  articles  were  excluded  if  they  (1)  lacked  experimental  elements  (e.g.,  qualitative research); (2) addressed the use of one-way instructional agents, which are typically called pedagogical agents, instead of interactive agents; (3) did not pertain to learning but rather to the feasibility of using chatbot systems; (4) lacked sufficient empirical data on language learning outcomes or experiences (e.g., studies without statistical analysis); (5) addressed comparisons between the effects of using chatbots and of using traditional instructional materials such as CD-players on language learning outcomes; or (6) investigated comparisons between different chatbot conditions with each employing different pedagogical strategies.

After applying these exclusion criteria, studies involving human-chatbot comparison  elements  with  statistical  results  ( n =  11)  were  compiled.  These  studies were  then  classified  into  two  categories  as  follows:  (1)  comparisons  between human-human  and  human-chatbot  interactions  ( n =  6),  in  which  how  students produced  utterances  during  interactions  in  either  condition  was  examined  and the  impact  of  these  interactions  on  language  learning  outcomes  assessed  (e.g., Timpe-Lauglin  et  al.,  2022)  and  (2)  comparisons  between  chatbot-assisted  and human-only  conditions  in  formal  educational  contexts  ( n =  5),  in  which  the effects on language learning outcomes of supplementally employed chatbots and human-only conditions were explored (e.g., Dizon, 2020). The selected articles were published between 2017 and 2022. Summary information for these papers, including specific research designs and chatbot designs, is provided in Table 1.

## 4.2   Data analysis

Though we distinctly addressed both categories, namely (1) human-chatbot vs. humanhuman interaction and (2) chatbot-assisted vs. human-only conditions, we employed a consistent qualitative methodology for data analysis across these classifications. During an in-depth, iterative review of each of the selected papers, we identified a critical aspect that necessitated consideration for subsequent analyses: the selected literature for  this  review  includes  multiple  variables  to  compare  chatbot-use  and  human-only conditions,  utilizing  distinct  yet  occasionally  intersecting  concepts  while  employing  varying  terminology  to  denote  similar  notions.  For  instance,  Xu  et  al.  (2021) employed six dependent variables, comprising 'lexical diversity,' 'topical relevance,' 'accuracy,' 'intelligibility,' 'productivity,' and 'story comprehension.' Among these variables, they employed the term 'topical relevance' to assess the semantic coherence and pertinence to a specific topic of student expressions, whereas in other studies (Timpe-Lauglin et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022) such variables were utilized as 'irrelevant vocalization,' 'relevant vocalization,' and 'number of turns containing requests' to  assess  the  relevance  of  student  expressions  to  given  tasks.  Consequently,  it  was deemed inappropriate and infeasible to regard each article as a singular unit for analysis,  as  each paper included disparate quantities of variables with divergent concepts and terminology. Rather, in alignment with Li's (2015) methodology for a systematic review, we decided to regard each dependent variable as a discrete unit for evaluation. After identifying the operational definition of each dependent variable employed to compare humans and chatbots, we treated it as a sub-variable and then grouped subvariables with similar definitions into a categorical variable. Sub-variables are directly adopted from individual studies, while terms for a categorical variable were created or, in a few cases, selected among sub-variables to represent all the other sub-variables. For example, 'fluency' (Ockey &amp; Chukharev-Hudilainen, 2021; Timpe-Lauglin et al., 2022), 'word count' (Forsyth et al., 2019), 'speaking time,' 'number of turns' (Timpe-Lauglin et al., 2022), and 'productivity' (Xu et al., 2021) were consolidated into the encompassing variable, 'interaction quantity.' In another case, 'Interactional competence'  (Ockey  &amp;  Chukharev-Hudilainen,  2021)  and  'Interactivity'  (Forsyth et al., 2019) were grouped into the variable 'Interactivity,' as this term could represent 'Interactional  competence.'  The  entire  process  of  data  analysis  was  conducted  and reviewed through close collaboration between the authors, employing consensus coding. When our team encountered disagreements, we resolved them with the assistance of an external researcher with an advanced degree related to educational technology and abundant experience in publishing research on chatbots.

These factors  served  to  show  the  distinctiveness  between  human  performance  and chatbot capabilities under the additional conditions. Finally, to provide a general indication of prevalence and as a means of identifying areas for future chatbot research and development, we tallied the frequencies of all sub-variables considered in comparisons of chatbot use and human-only conditions in the 11 studies.

## 5   Results

## 5.1   The effects of human-chatbot interaction and human-human interaction on interaction patterns and learning outcomes

Six studies included in this category are focused on differences in the ways individual users interact  with  chatbots  and  with  human  interlocutors.  As  displayed  in Table  2,  we  identified  46  statistical  findings  from  these  six  studies  and  extracted nine variables for comparison: (1) sophistication, (2) vocabulary and grammar, (3) topic relevance, (4) accuracy, (5) interactivity, (6) interaction quantity, (7) sustained positive  attitude,  (8)  short-term  positive  attitude,  and  (9)  short-term  learning  outcomes.  Overall,  the  preponderance  of  evidence  indicates  that  human-chatbot  and human-human interactions  may  generate  similar  interaction  patterns  and  learning effects. However, each condition was also found to possess its own combination of strengths and weaknesses. These results are discussed below.

Similar effects The majority of  the  evidence  suggests  that  chatbots  may  be  capable of eliciting student utterances that are comparable to those observed in humanhuman interactions. In 30 of the 46 statistical results (65.2%), differences between the  two  conditions  were  found  to  be  statistically  non-significant.  As  shown  in Table 2, five of the nine categorical variables (sophistication, vocabulary and grammar,  topical  relevance,  short-term  positive  attitude,  and  short-term  learning  outcomes) were found to have a greater number of similar effects than different effects between the two conditions.

Specifically, eight out of nine results (88.9%) for sophistication and seven out of nine results (77.8%) for vocabulary and grammar showed that students demonstrated comparable  levels  between  the  two  interaction  conditions.  For  example,  Forsyth et al. (2019) compared whether students spoke differently in conditions where their interlocutor was either a human or a chatbot in the context of formative assessment. Specifically, in their study on two task scenarios (i.e., explaining factual information and retelling key ideas and evidence), Forsyth et al. (2019) found no statistically significant differences in student utterances between human-human and human-chatbot conditions regarding the sophistication category of syntactic complexity, cohesion, argumentative  text,  and  narrativity.  Similarly,  Ockey  and  Chukharev-Hudilainen (2021) investigated differences in student speech during interactions with either a human partner or a chatbot. In a paired discussion task where students were required to  discuss  different  positions  with  either  a  chatbot  or  another  human  interlocutor, they observed that the participants produced comparable speech in terms of grammar and vocabulary across the two conditions.

Similarly,  all  four  results  for  short-term  positive  attitude  and  the  two  results for short-term learning outcome showed similar effects between interacting with chatbots and with human interlocutors. For example, Fryer et al. (2017) found no difference in students' task interest between the two conditions when measured immediately  after  task  completion  with  either  a  chatbot  or  a  human  partner.  It was also found that there were no significant differences in short-term learning outcomes between the conditions. For example, in a book reading scenario where participants were guided by either a chatbot or a human interlocutor, Xu and her colleagues (2021, 2022) observed no statistically significant differences in students' story  comprehension  performance  between  the  two  conditions.  Additionally,  in terms of topical relevance, five out of eight results (62.5%) showed comparable levels across the interaction conditions. For instance, Timpe-Lauglin et al. (2022) reported that in a task requiring participants to make requests in a pragmatically proper manner, there was no significant difference between the two conditions. However, a clear pattern for topical relevance was not identified when using the number of studies as an analytical unit, as only two out of four studies demonstrated these comparable levels, which indicates a need for further research.

<!-- image -->

Different  effects On  the  other  hand,  statistically  significant  differences  between the two conditions were found in 16 out of 46 results (34.8%), with most favoring human-human interaction ( n =  13)  over  chatbot-human  interaction  ( n =  3).  Among the  nine  categorical  variables,  three  (interactivity,  interaction  quantity,  and  sustained positive attitude) were found to have a greater number of statistically different results than similar results between the two interaction contexts. Specifically, results for interactivity ( n =  2 out of 3), interaction quantity ( n =  4 out of 7), and sustained positive  attitude  ( n =  1  out  of  1)  showed  superior  results  for  human-human  interaction.  In  the  case  of  interactivity,  two  out  of  three  results  (66.7%)  indicated  that students  demonstrated  better  interactivity  when  they  interacted  with  humans  than with chatbots. For instance, Ockey and Chukharev-Hudilainen (2021) observed that during the paired discussion task, participants were evaluated by professional raters as  producing  utterances  that  exhibited  more  communicative  appropriateness  and reciprocity when interacting with humans than with chatbots. The other result indicated similar levels of interactivity between the two conditions only when performing a low-order thinking task (i.e., factual information delivery task) and not when performing a high-order thinking task (i.e., retelling with evidence) (Forsyth et al., 2019).

Among seven results for interaction quantity, five showed a statistically validated difference between the two conditions, with four results (57.1%) favoring human-human interactions and one result (14.3%) favoring human-chatbot interaction,  while  two  (28.6%)  showed  no  statistical  difference.  However,  an  inconsistency was found in the pattern of interaction quantity as reflected in the three varied results reported in Timpe-Lauglin et al. (2022). In their study, participants were asked to make appropriate requests to both chatbots and humans, and it was found  that  the  variables  'fluency,'  'speaking  time,'  and  'productivity,'  which were categorized under 'interaction quantity' for the analysis, yielded different comparison outcomes with each favoring a different result. Next, only one study, conducted by Fryer et al. (2017) who examined long-term changes in students' task  interest  based  on  the  type  of  task  partner,  specifically  addressed  sustained positive  attitude.  They  found  a  more  positive  outcome  in  the  human-human condition  than  in  the  human-chatbot  condition.  Last,  the  accuracy  variable revealed no meaningful pattern of similarities or differences between the two conditions, favoring neither condition over the other.

## 5.2   The effects of chatbot-assisted conditions and those of human-only conditions on learning outcomes

In the current study, chatbot-assisted conditions were broadly construed to include a  variety  of  approaches  to  integrating  chatbots  as  supplementary  resources  in human-led instruction, both in and outside classrooms. Whereas in the previous section,  the  effects  of  human-chatbot  and  human-human  interactions  were  compared as different instructional conditions, the focus of this section is on chatbots as auxiliary components to human-human activities (i.e., human-chatbot collaboration).  Eighteen  statistical  findings  from  five  experimental  studies  were  in  this category. Unlike human-chatbot and human-human comparison research, this line of  investigation  only  centered  on  examining  learning  outcomes,  particularly  the impact  of  chatbots  on  (1)  students'  long-term  speaking  outcomes  and  (2)  longterm listening outcomes, rather than on differences in human-chatbot and humanhuman interaction patterns.

Similar effects Incorporating  chatbots  as  part  of  teacher-led  instruction  did  not consistently  yield  superior  outcomes  compared  to  conditions  involving  only human-human interaction. As illustrated in Table 2, 11 out of 18 results (61.1%) were  found  to  be  statistically  non-significant  between  the  two  conditions.  This tendency  was  especially  pronounced  for  listening  proficiency,  in  which  three out  of  four  results  (75%)  showed  no  significant  difference.  For  example,  Dizon (2020) examined the impact of using screenless smart speaker chatbots on EFL students' speaking and listening proficiency. He found no statistically significant difference in post-treatment listening proficiency scores between an experimental group that used a chatbot and a control group that did not in on-site EFL classes for 10 weeks. Reflecting on future directions for incorporating chatbot technology into pedagogy, Dizon suggested that, instead of smart speakers without a screen as in his study, screen-based devices displaying live on-screen written transcriptions should be used to help students focus on chatbot utterances and notice mistakes in their utterances. Hsu et al. (2021), who used a screen-based chatbot for an 18-week EFL course, still found no difference in listening  proficiency  improvement between the experimental and control groups, suggesting that a longer-term experiment might be necessary to obtain meaningful results. Focusing on device modality,  Tai  and  Chen  (2022a)  conducted  a  10-week  in-class  treatment  comparing  three  different  EFL  teaching  conditions:  an  experimental  group  with  a screen-based chatbot, another experimental group with a non-screen chatbot, and a control group. They found that the group with a screen-based chatbot performed significantly better than the control group on a post-test of listening proficiency, but there was no difference between the group with a non-screen chatbot and the control  group.  Student  interview  data  supported  the  results,  with  many  students reporting that the written transcription was particularly helpful for improving their listening proficiency. However, considering the discrepancies between the findings of Hsu et al. (2021) and Tai and Chen (2022a) as well as the absence of detailed descriptions of pedagogical information in both studies, such as detailed information regarding in-class activities, further investigation is warranted.

<!-- image -->

Regarding  speaking  proficiency,  eight  out  of  14  results  identified  similar effects between the chatbot-assisted and human-only conditions. However, seven out  of  these  eight  statistically  non-significant  results  were  derived  from  Tai's (2022) study, in which students utilized chatbots outside the classroom to supplement their in-class activities, rather than in class. Specifically, Tai (2022) found no statistically significant difference between interacting with chatbots and with native  English  speakers  outside  the  classroom,  whereas  engaging  with  chatbots led to better speaking proficiency than interacting with non-native English speakers.  Among  the  investigations  conducted  in  classrooms,  excluding  results  from Tai's (2022) research, only one out of four studies reported a similar advantage for using chatbots (Tai &amp; Chen, 2022b). However, this advantage was observed only when comparing the human-only condition with the chatbot-assisted condition, where no screen-based chatbots were used. Tai and Chen (2022b) found that the device modality resulted in different learning effects, which will be discussed further in the subsequent section.

Different effects With regard to specific speaking skills, Tai (2022) discovered that using chatbots was more effective than interacting with L2 speakers, for enhancing fluency,  content,  and  vocabulary,  while  no  difference  was  found  in  pronunciation and grammar skills in L2 oral proficiency tests. In the other three studies comparing the effects of chatbot-assisted and human-only conditions on speaking proficiency, three  out  of  four  results  (75%),  except  for  one  of  the  results  from  Tai  and  Chen (2022b), were found to be statistically significant, favoring chatbot-assisted conditions. However, these were not consistent for device types. Hsu et al. (2021) compared using devices with screens with using no chatbots, and Dizon (2020) compared using non-screen devices with using no chatbots, while as noted above Tai and Chen (2022b) compared both types of devices with no chatbot use. However, whereas Dizon (2020) found that students who used a non-screen chatbot as part of in-person instruction improved significantly more in speaking proficiency than those who did not use a chatbot, Tai and Chen (2022b) found no difference between using a  non-screen  chatbot  and  not  using  a  chatbot.  On  the  other  hand,  Tai  and  Chen's (2022b) results showing that using a chatbot with a screen was significantly more effective than using no chatbot were consistent with Hsu et al.'s (2021) finding of a significant advantage for using a device with a screen. Also as noted above, Tai and Chen's (2022b) interview data underscore the crucial  role  of  on-screen  transcription in chatbots' positive effect on speaking proficiency by enabling learners to identify and correct their speech errors. However, Tai and Chen's (2022b) and Dizon's (2020) conflicting results suggest that device modality may not sufficiently explain the impact of using chatbots on improving speaking proficiency.

## 6   Discussion

The primary objective of the current study was to explore the impact of using chatbots as an alternative to human-human interaction in language education. For this objective,  a  total  of  11  studies,  each  belonging  to  one  of  the  following  two  categories of human-chatbot comparison research, were examined: (1) human-chatbot vs.  human-human  conditions  and  (2)  chatbot-assisted  vs.  human-only  conditions. Across  the  11  studies  reviewed,  we  found  64  statistical  results  comparing  chatbot  use  and  human-only  conditions.  Our  assessment  of  these  statistical  outcomes showed that (1) overall, the comparative outcomes specifically between human-chatbot and human-human conditions were mainly similar, albeit with some instances favoring  human-human  interaction  and  (2)  the  results  for  comparisons  between chatbot-assisted and human-only conditions resulted in mainly non-significant differences as well, but in this case, with some results favoring chatbot-assisted conditions. Each of these findings will be examined in detail, followed by an examination of possible future research directions.

Delving further into six studies that compared human-chatbot and human-human interactions, we extracted nine variables for comparison. In most of these studies, student  utterances  generated  during  interactions  with  either  a  chatbot  or  a  human interlocutor were compared, while a few examined how the two conditions affected learning outcomes. Specifically, 46 results included 39 for students' utterance comparison between the two conditions, as well as one for sustained positive attitude, four  for  short-term  positive  attitude,  and  two  for  short-term  learning  outcomes. Out of these 46 results, 30 showed no statistically significant differences between the  conditions.  On  the  other  hand,  among  16  statistically  different  results,  13  out of these 16 results indicated more positive outcomes for students in human-human than in human-chatbot interactions in terms of topical relevance, interactivity, and interaction quantity, and students in human-human interaction conditions exhibited a greater long-term positive attitude compared to those in human-chatbot interactions. However, except for these 13 out of 46 statistical results, human-human conditions did not yield outcomes superior to those of human-chatbot conditions.

Regarding RQ1, based on the overall findings, it seems that chatbots can produce interactional effects similar to humans in many aspects. However, it should be noted that variables which were relatively mechanical elements, such as degrees of utterance sophistication or vocabulary and grammar level of utterances, as well as variables tested within short-term scenarios, such as short-term engagement, were more likely than those focused on requiring social elements, such as interactivity, elaboration of information, or sustained positive attitude, to show similar results in the two conditions. Specifically, in accordance with the findings of Clark et al. (2019), who observed a tendency among chatbot users to distinguish between social and transactional roles in conversations, focusing primarily on transactional purposes in agent interactions, students tended to elaborate less and use fewer social utterances when interacting with chatbots (e.g., Ockey &amp; Chukharev-Hudilainen, 2021; Timpe-Lauglin  et  al.,  2022).  Also,  a  decrease  in  motivation  for  interaction  with  chatbots  was reported after the early stage of a task involving human-chatbot interaction (Fryer et  al.,  2017).  In  contrast,  it  is  of  note  that  Fryer  et  al.  (2017)  found  that  students tended to maintain a more stable interest in their interactions with human partners. In  most  of  the  studies,  students  also  demonstrated  a  greater  degree  of  interactivity  with  human  interlocutors  than  with  chatbots  (e.g.,  Ockey  &amp;  Chukharev-Hudilainen, 2021). These results may be attributed to students being aware that they were interacting with machines despite chatbots' human-like ability to chat with humans (Fryer and Carpenter, 2006; Jeon, 2024; Lee &amp; Jeon, 2022; Tai &amp; Chen, 2022b). In summary, considering these relative strengths, the decision to use chatbots in education should be guided by what educators intend to accomplish.

Next,  we  identified  18  statistical  results  from  five  studies  concerning  chatbotassisted vs. human-only conditions, which were grouped into two dependent variables: the impact of chatbot use on students' speaking and its impact on listening proficiencies. With respect to speaking proficiency, six out of 14 results indicated that incorporating chatbots along with human-human interaction led to more favorable  effects  on  speaking  proficiency  than  not  employing  chatbots.  However,  when device modality was considered, the studies yielded mixed outcomes. For example, Tai  and  Chen  (2022b)  demonstrated  the  positive  effects  of  chatbot  utilization  on speaking skills only when chatbots were deployed on screen-based devices, whereas using chatbots on devices without screens did not produce significant results. In contrast,  Dizon  (2020)  observed  a  positive  effect  on  speaking  proficiency  even  when chatbots were utilized on devices without screens. Therefore, the effects of device modality on the development of speaking skills remain uncertain. In addition, out of four results for listening skills, only one condition comparing a group with a screenbased device and a human-only condition revealed a statistically significant difference in post-listening test scores. The inconsistency regarding device modality was also observed in the results for listening proficiency.

Regarding  RQ2,  unlike  the  previous  section,  where  most  results  were  similar between  human-human  and  human-chatbot  conditions  or  favored  human-human conditions, it is of significant note that no results showed a preference for humanonly conditions over chatbot-assisted conditions. These findings may support earlier studies that advocate for the use of chatbots in collaboration with human interlocutors, rather than relying solely on chatbots (Jeon &amp; Lee, 2023). Advancing the discussion, this review identifies specific domains where collaboration between humans and chatbots may be more effective, while also emphasizing the significance of considering device modality to maximize the effect. That is, the evidence in the current review suggests that integrating chatbots into formal educational settings could be more beneficial for enhancing students' speaking proficiency than for improving their  listening  skills.  Furthermore,  considering  the  direct  comparison  results  from Tai and Chen (2022a, b), using a device with a screen might be more effective than using one without a screen. However, given the inconsistent findings across studies that used similar types of devices regarding screen availability, the extent to which device modality directly influences improved outcomes remains unclear and is left for future research.

Future  research  designs  comparing  humans  and  chatbots  can  be  informed  by the limitations identified in the selected studies. First, only five of the 11 reviewed experimental studies considered additional conditions to determine when and how using chatbots was more or less effective than human interlocutors. These conditions included task difficulty (Forsyth et al., 2019), difficulty level of chatbot prompts (Xu et al., 2021), device type (Tai &amp; Chen, 2022a, b), and L1 or L2 human interlocutor control conditions (Tai, 2022). In these studies, the considered conditions led to varying  results,  highlighting  the  differential  impact  of  human-chatbot  interactions on learning as compared to human-only interactions. Given this, it is reasonable to assume that the inconsistent results identified across the selected studies might be attributed to differences in factors that were not consistently controlled, such as the educational level of participants, the use of L1 or L2, the duration of intervention, and the type of experimental setting (e.g., formal or informal). As this review only focused on different comparison conditions, its results provide only a general sense of similar and different learning effects between chatbot use and human-only conditions. Therefore, there is a need for more research that takes into account different age group, L1 or L2 teaching scenario, varying number of sessions, and formal or informal setting in order to gain a more accurate and robust understanding of how chatbots produce different outcomes compared to human counterparts.

<!-- image -->

Second, chatbots possess a range of technological attributes, such as different modes of multimodality and embodiment (i.e., how chatbots look and interact), all of which may moderate the effects found in the current study (see Jeon et al., 2023a for a comprehensive discussion on chatbots' different modes of multimodality and embodiment).  However,  with  a  few  exceptions  where  device  modality  was  considered  in relation to multimodality (Tai &amp; Chen, 2022a, b), these two multidimensional concepts were not adequately explored. As knowledge of chatbots' impact on learning develops, it is  crucial  to  understand  how  chatbots  are  effective,  rather  than  merely examining whether they are effective or not (Jeon et al., 2023a). This implies that when examining their effectiveness compared to humans, researchers should provide detailed explanations of the chatbots' distinct characteristics in terms of multimodality and embodiment. Therefore, it is recommended that future researchers not only provide comprehensive information about the chatbots used but also assess how different features of chatbots lead to similar or different learning effects compared to those of human interlocutors.

Third,  concerning  chatbot-assisted  vs.  human-only  conditions,  there  is  a  need for a clearer depiction of human-chatbot collaboration that occurs in a classroom. Many studies within this category were found not to provide a clear account of how chatbots  were  integrated  into  human-led  classroom  activities,  which  may  explain the mixed results obtained. In addition to this, how tasks were administered might have influenced chatbots' impact on student learning. For example, while Tai and Chen (2022b) found that device modality moderated the chatbot's effect, other studies did not confirm this finding, producing mixed results. To help resolve this issue, future researchers should consider interaction effects between device types used and other pedagogical elements, such as what type of chatbot activities were utilized and when.

Next, differing from recent large language models, such as ChatGPT, the chatbots examined in this study mostly followed predefined dialogue paths or featured simplistic  question-and-answer dialogue structures based on user input (Kuhail et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2024). However, considering the specific pedagogical contexts in which  students'  utterances  were  limited  and  predictable,  we  assume  that  technological  advancements may not lead to significantly different results from those of the current study, except for some cases where unbounded interaction contexts are required.  Nonetheless,  comparing  large  language  models  with  humans  in  educational settings remains an intriguing area for future research. The current research may serve as an evaluation of the present state of human-chatbot comparison studies and as a reference point for future investigations that compare humans and more advanced chatbots such as large language models.

<!-- image -->

Last, this research was framed around the question, 'Can learners benefit from chatbots  instead  of  humans?'  This  question  allowed  for  a  comprehensive  discussion comparing the different and similar learning impacts of chatbots and humans. However, as indicated by the findings from the second research question, collaboration between humans and chatbots might offer greater benefits than using chatbots alone. In this context, an important and interesting extension of this study would be to focus on examining the complementary roles of humans and chatbots in the classroom setting and to find the most effective combination (Holstein &amp; Aleven, 2022; Jeon &amp; Lee, 2023). For example, as shown in Huang et al. (2022), researchers can adopt a social affordances perspective to further explore the most effective complementary roles that chatbots and humans can assume in a classroom setting.

## 7   Implications

Implications for researchers This review provides implications for researchers concerning theoretical understanding of chatbot technology in education and methodological variations in studies reviewing chatbots. First, the findings from the current study support the notion of conceptualizing chatbots as novel interlocutors that contribute uniquely to student learning (Lee &amp; Jeon, 2022; Xu &amp; Warschauer, 2020). Previous qualitative studies on students' perceptions of chatbots have consistently revealed that students attribute both mechanical and social features to chatbots (e.g., Clark et al., 2019; Xu &amp; Warschauer, 2020). Some researchers suggest that students' awareness of chatbots' mechanical nature even facilitates language learning by alleviating students' anxiety about making errors, a common obstacle to language learning when students interact with human interlocutors (e.g., Fryer &amp; Carpenter, 2006; Zhang et al., 2023a). Likewise, Lee and Jeon (2022) discovered that when students perceive chatbots as in-between objects, they can benefit from the ubiquity of access and personal agency of working with machines while experiencing the emotional satisfaction  of  attributing  human-like  qualities  to  chatbots  as  interlocutors.  In  line with this viewpoint, the current study identified areas where chatbots may be more or  less  effective  than  human  interlocutors,  supporting  the  concept  of  chatbots  as novel interlocutors, rather than evaluating them based on their similarity to humans as the gold standard. Thus, the findings emphasize that the pedagogical role of chatbots should be understood in a way that allows students to benefit from their unique advantages without perceiving them as complete simulations of human partners and therefore as replacements.

<!-- image -->

Second,  unlike  previous  reviews  on  chatbots  in  education,  this  review  adopted a  methodologically  different  approach  to  examining  the  impact  of  chatbots  by focusing  on  human-chatbot  comparison  research.  While  not  providing  direct insight  into  similar  and  different  effects  that  chatbots  have  in  comparison  to humans, previous reviews in the field of language education have primarily concentrated  on  chatbots  themselves,  such  as  their  overall  effectiveness  (Bibauw et  al.,  2022;  Zhang  et  al.,  2023b),  affordances  (Huang  et  al.,  2022;  Jeon  et  al., 2023a, typologies (Bibauw et al., 2019), roles (Jeon et al., 2023b), and methods for  optimizing  their  potential  for  pedagogical  applications  (Fryer  et  al.,  2020; Jeon et al., 2023b). By complementing these previous reviews, the present study helps researchers identify the specific pedagogical potential of chatbot technology in comparison with humans, thereby offering a more nuanced understanding of the educational benefits of chatbots.

Implications  for  teachers Regarding  pedagogical  implications,  first,  this  study  presents a searchable directory, which can help teachers determine the advantages and limitations of employing chatbots in their pedagogical contexts. Based on this information, educators may choose to allocate additional instructional time and effort to areas  in  which  chatbots  might  not  be  as  effective  while  capitalizing  on  chatbots  to serve purposes for which they demonstrate comparable or occasionally superior capability compared to human interlocutors. For example, teachers aiming to evaluate and raise the levels of sophistication, grammar, or vocabulary of student utterances might be able to consider utilizing chatbots when human interlocutors are not available. On the  other  hand,  when  tasks  are  intended  to  cultivate  students'  interactional  competence or encourage them to provide sufficient information, human interlocutors may be more effective as conversation partners than chatbots. Furthermore, although chatbots might be as efficient as human interlocutors in fostering short-term engagement and learning, their prolonged use, especially if they reach the limits of their reciprocity, could weaken students' interest in chatbot activities. As suggested by Divekar et al. (2022), incorporating chatbots to complement human-human interaction may prevent students'  loss  of  interest  while  preserving  the  chatbots'  effectiveness  for  achieving intended learning outcomes, but the right balance for each situation must be explored further. Also, the influence of chatbot use on students' motivation when used to supplement human-human interaction is an important topic that has yet to be investigated.

Our analysis also reveals that teachers should consider task difficulty when integrating  chatbots  in  their  instructional  contexts.  Additional  conditions  identified  in this  review  encompass  task  type,  chatbot  prompt  complexity,  and  device  modality, all of which are associated with task difficulty. In general, studies that include these conditions suggest that as tasks became more difficult and complex, students responded in a more targeted and accurate manner and demonstrated improved performance with humans than with chatbots. For example, Xu et al. (2021) observed that  as  chatbot  prompts  increased  in  difficulty,  statistically  significant  differences between  human  and  chatbot  interactions  emerged  or  became  greater  in  terms  of topical relevance, lexical diversity, and productivity. Tai and Chen (2022a, b) also found that learners who received supplementary support in the form of live written transcripts of their chatbot interactions achieved superior speaking outcomes compared to those lacking such support. Therefore, it is important for teachers to carefully consider the difficulty level of tasks, and as they become more demanding, it might be desirable to involve human interlocutors instead of chatbots or devise additional support mechanisms within chatbot systems.

## 8   Limitations of the current review and concluding remarks

Limitations in this review suggest the need for caution in interpreting the findings. First, we employed a simple counting method for systematically assessing the results of  research  comparing  chatbot-use  and  human-only  conditions.  As  stated  by  Li (2015), while this method may be suitable for providing a general indication of the impact of chatbots compared to human-only conditions, especially when only a few studies fulfill the inclusion criteria, a meta-analysis review that incorporates effect size, sample size, and null effects may offer a more accurate estimation of measured variables,  particularly  as  more  research  emerges  in  the  future.  Second,  our  literature search process does not guarantee the inclusion of all prior literature on humanchatbot comparison. Future studies need to include additional databases and search terms, as other types of literature found, such as working papers or conference proceedings, can lead to different findings. Last, because we counted individual statistically generated results from studies, studies reporting higher numbers of statistical results may have been overrepresented in our outcomes. Additionally, we recognize that  only  a  limited  number  of  studies  met  the  inclusion  criteria,  and  some  author teams may have been overrepresented in our sample. Therefore, the implications of the current review should be regarded as offering a general, preliminary overview of human-chatbot comparison research, which establishes a starting point for more robust analyses of forthcoming publications.

In conclusion, this systematic review may contribute to technology-enhanced language learning and chatbot research fields with novel findings specific to research on human-chatbot comparison. Gaining insight into how students respond and learn differently  with  chatbots  compared  to  humans  is  critical  for  the  successful  development  of  future  chatbot-based  pedagogical  designs,  particularly  as  chatbots  are increasingly being integrated into education at a rapid pace and with growing societal focus on their potential for helping students learn.
