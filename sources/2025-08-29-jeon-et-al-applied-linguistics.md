## Generative AI and its dilemmas: exploring AI from a translanguaging perspective

Technological advances in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) have produced multimodal and  multilingual  models  that  have  the  potential  to  achieve  a  long-sought  educational  goal: increasing equity and access for linguistically diverse learners. However, despite their capability to receive adaptive inputs combined with their proficiency in multiple languages and modalities, GenAI systems are designed to operate in the realm of statistical probability, standardizing what is already prevalent in society and under-representing minority voices. In this regard, the possibilities and the limitations of GenAI collide, resulting in instructional dilemmas regarding whether to invest effort into opening space for greater social equality or conveniently use the technology to maintain the social inequality of the status quo. Drawing on a translanguaging perspective, we introduce and illustrate the three dilemmas of creativity-standardization, inclusion-fixity ,  and meaning-form ,  which educators will inevitably encounter when integrating AI into their classrooms. We further discuss how this new and increasingly powerful technology can be understood and addressed by highlighting translanguaging as an analytical perspective to understanding and resolving the three dilemmas its use entails.

## Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) not only offers educators numerous affordances, such as  automated essay grading and student profiling but also has the potential to increase and enhance equity and access for linguistically diverse learners due to its capacity to communicate through multiple languages and modalities (Prado and Warschauer 2024; Stornaiuolo et al. 2024). Nevertheless, these systems are designed to operate in the realm of statistical probability, where standardization limits, and even prevents, fair representation of linguistic diversity (Kuteeva and Andersson 2024; Payne, Austin and Clemons 2024). In this article, we explore three dilemmas that the use of GenAI can introduce when integrated into education: creativity-standardization , inclusion-fixity , and meaning-form . Technological  advances  in  GenAI  encourage  scholars  to  envision  the  translanguaging  possibilities of multimodal and multilingual learner-AI communications. For example, they might imagine multilingual learners making full use of their linguistic resources in turn-by-turn interactions  with AI  or  embellishing  their  utterances  with  any  combination  of  text,  image,  audio, and video modes to add further meaning (Tai 2023, 2024). However, while these technological advancements may seem to provide the utopian potential for multilingual education, as discussed below, they also entail the dystopian outcome of what Payne, Austin and Clemons (2024) describe as 'bias-referencing societal prejudices while elevating standardized varieties [of content]' (p. 553).

The clash between GenAI's potential and limitations can be particularly more consequential for linguistically diverse learners as it directly relates to the empowerment and marginalization of their voices. Drawing on a translanguaging perspective that seeks a more inclusive, creative, and meaning-centered approach to education, we frame this clash by outlining three dilemmas, which educators will inevitably encounter when they integrate GenAI in the classroom: creativity-standardization, inclusion-fixity, and meaning-form. Finally,  we  suggest  that  the  inherent limitations and biases present in extensive language models be addressed by adopting a translanguaging  approach,  such  as  prompting  educators  to  actively  promote  the  use  of  students' diverse linguistic, sociocultural, and semiotic resources to question and challenge GenAI, since the manner in which we frame inquiries or tasks greatly influences how we understand and use the responses generated by GenAI.

## Demystifying AI: collisions of its potentials and limitations

We introduce two concepts to describe how ChatGPT, as an example of GenAI, works in terms of input and output in its interaction with a user, and potentially produces the three dilemmas regarding equity and access: fl exible receiver and stochastic parrot . We coined the former to represent GenAI's capability to receive adaptive input , and the other we borrowed from Bender et al.'s (2021) seminal work in the field of natural language processing to focus on how it produces output in the realm of statistical probability.

Serving as a fl exible receiver , ChatGPT operates based on statistical machine learning models, which emulate neural networks. To learn to produce a response, neural-like architectures process  data  through  layers  of  interconnected  nodes,  learning  to  infer  the  semantic  meaning  of words and the syntactic structure of sentences from large amounts of training data (Bender et al. 2021; Birhane and McGann 2024). This process involves identifying similar patterns in the training dataset, followed by synthesizing and further abstracting the patterns (Bender et al. 2021; Chomsky, Roberts and Watumull 2023). Due to this learning mechanism, it is able to respond to almost any user input, as long as it can find some estimates of the input in its training data, and generate the most statistically probable response. As a result, minor errors in users' input will not disrupt communications, and ChatGPT will attempt to identify estimates of users' input using its training data, even if the input is subtle or incomplete. Thus, it is possible that users mix words from different languages and use language or other modalities in their own manner as a starting point for their inquiries.

Meanwhile, the term stochastic parrot was first introduced by Bender et al. (2021) and has subsequently been used by researchers in applied linguistics (e.g. Kuteeva and Andersson 2024) to metaphorically criticize large language models (LLMs)' purely statistically produced outputs. When they introduced the term, Bender et al. stated that an LLM 'is a system for haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms it has observed in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how they combine, but without any reference to meaning' (p. 617). This description of the mechanism of output production suggests that what ChatGPT produces does not actually carry meaning. Another implication is that statistically probable outputs may

|

introduce biases and overrepresentations of perspectives and usages inherent in the training data.

In human-ChatGPT interaction, it is of note that ChatGPT serves as both a flexible receiver and a stochastic parrot. It responds to a wide range of user inputs, including those with typos, local expressions, multiple languages, images, and users' creative terms, while what it produces is quite limited to standardized, fixed, and form-focused expressions, leading to the three dilemmas, which we elaborate on in detail in the following section.

## Exploring the dilemmas from a translanguaging perspective

We  now  explain  each  of  the  dilemmas  by  drawing  on  data  from  our  own  interactions  with ChatGPT, a version of GPT-4o, which may resemble how students-including linguistically diverse learners-use this technology. To clarify the collision of its potentials and limitations, we view it through the lens of translanguaging, demonstrating how translanguaging can serve as a theoretical lens to diagnose and potentially address the limitations inherent in GenAI, including ChatGPT. We base our understanding of translanguaging on the definition provided by Otheguy, García and Reid (2015), which describes it as 'the deployment of a speaker's full linguistic repertoire without regard for strict adherence to the socially and politically defined boundaries of named (and usually national and state) languages' (p. 283).

## Creativity-standardization dilemma

Translanguaging does not limit language use to a single linguistic system or a single fixed identity for an individual language user. In a translanguaging space, students' use of different language resources and various ways of producing their own language use based on individuals' fluid identities is legitimized (Otheguy, García and Reid 2015). That is, it encourages linguistically diverse learners to be creative language users, not limiting their use of language to a single language system or rule. To illustrate how the creativity-standardization dilemma occurs, a sample illustrated as a creative use of language from a translanguaging study conducted by Li (2018) was adopted: 'Departyment 政府部 门 ' is a Chinglish example of wordplay that reads like 'English, but a monolingual English speaker may find it difficult to understand precisely their meanings and connotations' (p. 13). Li explains that the expression means 'department + party, mocking government departments spending time and resources on parties' (p. 12). This explanation, in addition to our background knowledge of the history and political contexts, helped us grasp what this creative use of Chinglish implies. Next, when we used this expression in our conversation with ChatGPT, it responded appropriately, seemingly demonstrating a certain degree of understanding (see Fig. 1).

However,  it  still  remained  unclear  whether 'departyment'  was  understood  by  ChatGPT  in terms of 'an understanding of the sociopolitical context in which these expressions occur' (Li 2018, p. 13). It is of note that ChatGPT accepts the creative use of language to a certain degree, but in a manner that human interlocutors who share similar understandings and cultures would not (see Jones 2025; Zhu et al. 2025 for further discussions).

## Inclusion-fixity dilemma

Researchers have reported that AI technology which does not include emergent efforts to subvert stereotypes and biases can further reinforce those that are already prevalent in society (Bender et al. 2021; Jeon, Lee and Coronel-Molina 2024; Payne, Austin and Clemons 2024). In contrast, as shown in Li and Lee (2024), translanguaging perspectives help us view human linguistic activity as characterized by change as the only constant, existing in a state of constant flux-a state that data used for training AI cannot capture, as AI stores data in a static manner. This perspective enables the inclusion of diverse voices, as language activity is not considered fixed but rather fluid. Birhane and McGann (2024) provide an example of how limited GenAI can be in terms of its  inclusivity,  as  it  cannot  accommodate 'change' as a constant (Li and Lee 2024). They continue  by  offering  one  such  effort  in  human-inclusive  language  activity,  algospeak,  which  can serve as a source for explaining the extent to which GenAI can be exclusive. Algospeak is a term coined by combining 'algorithm' and 'speak,' referring to alternative expressions that will not be automatically filtered by Internet algorithms and thus avoid possible governmental monitoring (Birhane and McGann 2024). ChatGPT does not infer algospeak mainly because the data used to train the model are not, and cannot be, current enough to include emerging minority voices that use inclusive language (Birhane and McGann 2024). However, as shown in Fig. 3, even with the typo 'leg botty,' ChatGPT brought up the phrase 'LGBT community,' exhibiting some grasp of what this algospeak term means; this may suggest that it was present in the training data and demonstrates its ability as a flexible receiver, but not its significance as a proxy for a red-flagged term, showing that it does not understand the intention behind the use of the term.

As shown in Fig. 4, to further examine its understanding, we asked using a mix of two languages, ' 한국어로 leg booty를 뭐라고 해석해? [What is the meaning of leg booty in Korean?].' In response, ChatGPT translated the two English words into Korean, stating, 'The translation of leg booty into Korean generally means legs and buttocks (translated from Korean into English).' On the bright side, it seems to be promising that ChatGPT responds to different languages in a grammatically sound manner generating its response by mixing the two languages as well; however, the construction of the meaning shown in its response, which was likely the most statistically probable and representative response in the training data (Payne, Austin and Clemons 2024), suggests such contexts as fitness, fashion, and dance. This focus on fixed data sets makes conversations less inclusive for people who want to discuss sensitive topics related to marginalized populations (Benjamin 2021). Even if students can create and introduce new terms to represent a certain minority group as shown in the leg booty term example, it is not possible for AI to communicate using those terms in an inclusive way until the terms become prevalent enough to be included in training data. However, by that point, the intention behind using the terms may have vanished (Birhane and McGann 2024; Li and Lee 2024).

## Meaning-form dilemma

The  conversation  data  presented  in  Figs  1-4  illustrate  ChatGPT's  form-focused  output  in response to humans' meaning-focused input. Otheguy, García and Reid (2015) point out that translanguaging does not prioritize 'a pure, well-bounded, and essential collection of lexical and structural  features'  (p.  299).  Rather,  it  focuses  on  meanings  that  individuals  produce  in  their actual language practices, as emphasized by Li's (2018) assertion that a crucial component of communication is 'to understand how the different modes are put together to create meaning' (p. 22). However, plenty of research has demonstrated that what GenAI produces is more rigid and formulaic compared to human writing due to its standardized operational mechanism (Jiang and Hyland 2025; Kuteeva and Andersson 2024).

The meaning-form dilemma is the underlying source of the other two dilemmas. As Bender and Koller (2020) argue, meaning arises in 'the relation between the form and something external to language' (p. 5187), explaining that meaning, by connecting language to phenomena beyond its boundaries, is at the heart of communicative intent, which is absent from ChatGPT's output. Regarding this discussion, Bender et al. (2021) state:

"Text generated by an LM [Large Language Model] is not grounded in communicative intent, any model of the world, or any model of the reader's state of mind. It can't have been, because the training data never included sharing thoughts with a listener, nor does the machine have the ability to do that (p. 616)."

For example, when the word 'departyment' was provided, ChatGPT seemed to focus on the linguistic difference between 'department' and 'departyment' to identify similar patterns of playful word-making found in its training data. Although it further derived a semblance of context for the term, it fell short of reaching a shared understanding of the communicative intent of the querent. Similarly, regarding the algospeak expression 'leg booty,' it did not seem to understand the purpose of using algospeak, namely, to avoid directly mentioning the term 'LGBT+' while still discussing the topic.

## Paths forward: translanguaging as a way to reframe AI

As  Bender  et  al.  (2021)  stated,  humans  tend  to  attribute  meaning  to  language  based  on  the assumption that it was written by someone with whom they share common communicative intent, even if the language was produced by machines that cannot have intent, shared or otherwise (Kidd and Birhane 2023; Lee and Jeon 2024). They argue that this tendency can mislead users into believing that what is produced by AI represents authentic voices in society, especially considering that human-AI communication feels similar to actual human-to-human interaction. This false and dangerous conception can lead to harmful results when the dilemmas we introduced are not noticed and addressed in a way that amplifies perceived similarities and underestimates limitations. In response, as shown in this essay, we propose that future researchers and educators note GenAI's potential while addressing its limitations by adopting translanguaging as an analytical approach, following Li and Lee (2024), who stated:

'multilingualism needs to be conceived as dynamic, where resources (phonic, graphical, lexicogrammatical, syntactical, and so on) from different languages and modes (verbal, visual, gestural,  kinetic,  tactile,  and  so  on)  are  thrown  together  and  integrated  in  a  semiotic  repertoire which individuals draw on to produce creative and/or critical interventions (p. 875).'

From a translanguaging perspective, we highlight that GenAI offers significant potential through various affordances relevant to creativity, inclusion, and meaning, providing rich multilingual communication opportunities. As suggested by the term fl exible receiver , what GenAI enables linguistically diverse learners to do is central to this approach. As shown in our conversations with ChatGPT, we could, although to a limited degree, interact with it by creatively using language to critique  external  societal  contexts,  inclusively  expressing  thoughts,  and  mixing  different  languages, even with some typos, all of which focused on meaning rather than form itself. In an increasingly complex technological context, where the use of diverse semiotic resources, such as visual, gestural, kinetic, and tactile, becomes available for human-machine interaction, alongside

|

the verbal resources used in our conversation data, it is conceivable that different languages and modes will be integrated in a student's semiotic repertoire for their interaction with machines (Jeon, Coronel-Molina and Lee 2025).

However, we found that GenAI, focusing primarily on language form, responded similarly to how individuals unfamiliar with shared cultural contexts or the hidden intentions behind neologisms might respond. As indicated in previous research, this response quality stems from GenAI's reliance on statistical probabilities when generating outputs (Jiang and Hyland 2025; Jones 2025). This reliance may contribute to the standardization and fixation of certain cultural norms and understandings in society-already prevalent in many cases (Bender et al. 2021; Kuteeva and Andersson 2024).

In modern post-humanist communication, GenAI has emerged as a central non-human actant with which learners are constantly invited to interact (Li and Lee 2024). In this sociotechnical context, it is important that educators include GenAI in ways that encourage learners, particularly those from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds, to engage in translanguaging practices, sufficiently utilizing its adaptability as a flexible receiver, rather than simply conforming to  GenAI's  form-focused outputs-outputs that must always be subjected to critical thinking (Kuteeva and Andersson 2024). Furthermore, GenAI's outputs should be positioned and contextualized within learners' increasingly complex networks of sociotechnical connections; it serves merely as one among many inputs arising from interactions with both human and non-human actants, while learners remain acutely aware of how those outputs are machine-generated.

To achieve this, educators must first encourage learners to critically examine AI-generated outputs so that they can assess bias, ethics, and trustworthiness. They should then design meaning-­ centered educational practices in which learners draw on their own semiotic repertoires alongside GenAI, integrating AI-generated content into wider sociotechnical connections with other human and non-human actants. In this way, GenAI's outputs serve as objects of critical analysis and can be (re-) contextualized to better align with the societal values that translanguaging scholarship has long pursued, namely creativity, inclusion, and meaningfulness of language use in society at large and in education specifically (Jeon, Coronel-Molina and Lee 2025; Li 2018; Tai 2023, 2024).

In the face of the increasingly prevalent use of AI in society, it is neither realistic nor desirable to ignore or ban this technology. Instead, we should recognize and learn the potential and limitations that GenAI presents based on how it actually operates and on the degree to which it helps us achieve what we want to pursue for more inclusive, creative, and meaning-centered education. This may pave the way for the more equitable development and use of AI, but this journey will require close collaboration among several key professionals, including critical applied linguists, technology developers, and educators. As an initial step forward in this direction, we suggest thinking about and reframing AI from a translanguaging perspective, which we believe will help move towards solutions aligned with societal values of equity and access for all learners, including linguistically diverse learners.
