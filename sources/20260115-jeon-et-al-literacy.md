## The AI turn in literacy practices: toward an inclusive educational framework

Jaeho Jeon , Serafin M. Coronel-Molina and Seongyong Lee ID

## Abstract

In this exploration of the impact of generative artificial intelligence (AI) on literacy practices, we critically refl ect on its standardized-value-prompting nature, which we found to come from two characteristic tendencies: homogenization and anthropomorphism. Because these tendencies shape how AI influences users ' meaning-making, its growing ubiquity creates tension with the promotion of diversity with inclusivity that has been sought throughout the history of literacy education. In response, drawing on the perspective of New Literacy Studies, which conceptualizes literacy as a situated social practice, we propose the ' AI turn ' in literacy as a nuanced shift within the digital turn, whereby educators are encouraged to embrace the enhanced meaning-making potential enabled by technological advancements. Specifically, we propose the ICIC framework as a coherent and systematic approach to help students learn four literacy skills: inquiry techniques, critical thinking, infusion of meaning, and creativity. These skills are elaborated with a focus on how they might ultimately safeguard and enhance equity for all learners. Developing these skills, along with careful approaches to innovation, will prepare students to navigate the complexities of AI-assisted learning and actively engage in meaning-making practices.

Key words: AI turn, ChatGPT, generative artificial intelligence, large language model, literacy

## Introduction

From the first writing systems to the digital revolution, the scope of literacy practices has expanded in response to significant societal and technological shifts that have transformed how people engage in meaning-making processes. Accordingly, literacy is now widely acknowledged as a multiplicity of meaning-making practices utilizing various technologies (Gee, 2015a; Kress, 2003; Mills, 2010; The New London Group, 1996). In this context, the recent emergence and spread of generative artificial intelligence (AI) is significantly influencing how individuals engage in literacy practices. It continues to advance at a rapid pace, blurring areas of application and incorporating different languages and modes of communication that can accommodate the needs of diverse users (Jeon et al., 2025). At the same time, this technology has sparked controversy among educational scholars, based on the question as to whether it enhances or hinders learners ' development of literacy skills.

In this paper, we take the position that the advent of advanced AI can have a major positive impact on literacy practices and teaching, suggesting that educators approach its use carefully and knowledgeably. In particular, it is necessary to understand and utilize the educational potential of AI in terms of its capacity for information synthesis. Accordingly, the following discussion begins by identifying what generative AI uniquely provides and how it differs from preceding non-generative technologies, analyzed through the lens of New Literacy Studies (Bloome and Green, 2015; Gee, 2015b; Street, 1995). We then support our position that its emergence is a critical event in the history of human literacy practices, requiring a coherent and systematic framework to safely include it in literacy education. Finally, based on its unique capabilities and risks, we describe how understanding AI from this perspective can help educators regard AI as a recent continuation of the trajectory of literacy education and how teaching with AI can be implemented in a way that safeguards and enhances equity for all students.

## AI, equity, and New Literacy Studies

Given its close relevance to reading and writing practices, this paper focuses on AI technologies powered by large language models, typically represented by ChatGPT, using the simpler term ' AI ' more generally (rather than generative AI) to broaden its relevance for the readership. Much remains to be learned about how literacy practices are developing with this new technology compared to those shaped by conventional, non-generative technologies and how such understanding can inform literacy education, in which diversity and equity are central concerns (e.g.

Gee, 2015a, 2015b; Heath, 1983). Regarding issues of equity in AI, Bender et al. (2021) have cautioned that, because the internet textual data used for training technology are disproportionately accessed and (re-)produced by certain groups, ' the voices of people most likely to hew to a hegemonic viewpoint are also more likely to be retained ' (p. 613). Hegemonic viewpoints embedded in the data on which AI is trained and subsequently projects can range from ideological biases (Bender et al., 2021; Jeon et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025) to privileging English, in that users obtain better results in English than when using low-resource languages, such as Swahili or Quechua (Lai et al., 2023). Such concerns have led educators to protectionist calls, such as banning AI tools from classrooms, or to solutionist approaches, such as providing a checklist for what students may and may not do with AI (e.g. Song et al., 2025).

In this paper, however, we agree with literacy researchers (e.g. Buckingham, 2019; Ciampa et al., 2023; Foster et al., 2024; Merchant, 2023a, 2023b; Mihailidis et al., 2025) who contend that such short-sighted approaches might increase inequity, as marginalized students typically have limited opportunities to access and deeply reflect on technology and its output. Furthermore, building on a long history of integrating media and culture in education (Buckingham and SeftonGreen, 2005; Burth et al., 2024; Johnson et al., 2024; Levido et al., 2025), we also note that priority should be given not to the reactive learning moderated by technological affordances (as has been common in popular technology-focused articles and practices) but to learning through a systematic and coherent framework that fosters a proactive and situated approach to technology. Thus, we argue that educators fi rst need to have a clear understanding of how AI systems differ from non-generative digital technologies and then take measures to incorporate this new technology in an inclusive and equitable manner. We believe this approach can be informed by New Literacy Studies that supports a view of languaging as deeply human and embedded in social interaction and lived experience (Buckingham, 2019; Burth et al., 2024; Gee, 2015a; Heath, 1983; Johnson et al., 2024; Levido et al., 2025; Street, 1984).

To understand what AI is designed to offer that is not possible without it, we first explain why we characterize its functioning as ' standardized-valueprompting ' , based on homogenization and anthropomorphism (discussed below), which account for the novel affordances AI introduces and distinguish it from non-generative digital tools.

Unlike its predecessors, AI offers automation of information synthesis. It does this by predicting the statistically most likely next word in a sequence, drawing on patterns learned from large data sets on the Internet

(Birhane and McGann, 2024). Unlike conventional search engines such as Google ' s and social media such as Facebook, which require users to locate and manually synthesize different information sources to generate texts, AI retrieves multiple responses to information requests from the internet and automatically synthesizes them to produce a cohesive response (Bender et al., 2021; Chomsky et al., 2023). That is, whereas non-generative chatbots or search engines operate in a ' locating information ' manner, typically matching user input to pre-existing output written by other users or quoting excerpts from web pages, AI rephrases and combines different pieces of text data in a ' rewriting information ' manner to generate coherent responses (Jeon et al., 2025). Pedagogically, one result would be that a student can ask an AI for a summary of a novel or help in drafting an essay and receive a complete text outcome within seconds, bypassing the cognitive work of critical reading and writing. Thus, this operating mechanism can undermine the purpose of literacy education, which is to develop learners ' reasoning and expressive powers. Moreover, due to the two above-mentioned underlying processes (i.e. homogenizing and anthropomorphizing), the mainstream texts on which the synthesis depends challenge literacy educators ' central concern with promoting inclusiveness and equity (e.g. Burth et al., 2024; Heath, 1983; Levido et al., 2025; Scollon and Scollon, 1981; Street, 1984).

First, AI is homogenizing , as it operates by producing a grammatically sound string of words in complete sentences based on statistical probability, which tends to gravitate toward what is already prevalent in society and is predisposed to replicate formulaic features of mainstream discourse, as empirically shown in research (e.g. Jiang and Hyland, 2025). Specifically, these models act mechanically and therefore lack key features essential to human communicative activity, such as embodiment, participatory dialogue, and grounded meaning as they do not share lived experience with humans (Hawkins, 2005; see Birhane and McGann, 2024 for a more in-depth discussion). That is, these models are designed to produce grammatically accurate and coherent language outputs that are not grounded in reality, do not share common bodily experience with querents, and do not require reciprocity with an interlocutor with agency in a particular communicative context. Also, as statistical probability is based on what is already prevalent in society and does not always equate to social equity, and as at any rate they do not have intentionality or sense of ethical responsibility, these models are prone to reproducing predisposed content (Birhane and McGann, 2024).

From a New Literacy Studies perspective, human linguistic activity is embedded in shared knowledge, lived experience, intention, worldview, and context.

Second, AI is anthropomorphizing . Humans tend to attribute human qualities even to an object when they try to understand it better, and this tendency is strengthened when it displays some human-like features, such as the use of language (Jeon and Lee, 2024; Jeon et al., 2025). In addition, AI ' s tendency to produce misinformation and bias can be especially dangerous as it reproduces them without consciousness and on a large scale. This issue highlights the need to approach AI-generated text in education differently from other contexts. AI is designed to sound informed, fl uent, and confident, making it more persuasive and what it produces more acceptable than if it communicated in an obviously robotic style. Because of this technical nature, if used uncritically, it can lead to AI ' s unilateral imposition of abstracted and generalized literacy practices upon its users (Olson, 1977; Ong, 1982).

Kidd and Birhane (2023) argue that, as also shown in some empirical studies (e.g. Lee and Jeon, 2024), this imposition succeeds because AI is conversational in nature and perceived as knowledgeable and intentional, leading users to anthropomorphize it and regard it as having human expertise. Nowadays, it is not uncommon to observe students talking to ChatGPT when they are expected to understand and learn something. When AI produces output in an articulate human voice or written text, students easily believe it to be true, unaware that it is composed of chains of statistically probable words rather than grounded in knowledge. This tendency is further reinforced by public discourse in which AI is often consulted as a sentient source that is able to understand, reason, and draw conclusions (Bender and Koller, 2020). Such uncritical portrayals, combined with AI ' s capability of confidently generating fluent and convincing language, blur the boundaries between human linguistic activity and that of AI, increasing the likelihood that the latter is accepted at face value.

Thus, AI is designed to reproduce what is already prevalent in society, and because its outputs have human-like persuasive qualities that often encourage uncritical acceptance, it functions as a standardizedvalue-prompting system, in opposition to the diversity that literacy education seeks to promote.

This tension between how New Literacy Studies defi nes literacy practices and how AI produces responses to queries parallels the still evolving discussion of how reading and writing are understood, accepted, and expanded in the field of literacy (Merchant, 2023a). As Street (1995) described, literacy can be conceptualized from the perspective of either an autonomous model or an ideological model. In an autonomous model, literacy is conceptualized as a set of cognitive processes and discrete skills independent of the social context in which they are used. According to this view, an individual either possesses or lacks these cognitive and linguistic skills, and this distinction is used to label people as either literate or illiterate. This conceptualization makes it plausible to argue that literacy education should be focused on the acquisition of specific cognitive and linguistic skills. In contrast, the ideological model sees literacy as situated, shared cultural practices that dictate when, where, and what registers of written language are appropriate to be used. According to this model, literacy is not a fixed ' thing in-and-of-itself ' (Bloome and Green, 2015, p. 20), but rather the contextualized use of written language by people interacting within particular social spaces and relationships. It is understood in terms of concrete social practices and ideologies in which different literacy practices are embedded. The opposition between these two theoretical stances is popularly designated as the ' Great Divide ' in literacy education (Olson, 1977; Ong, 1982; Street, 1984) and has been addressed in the long history of literacy, particularly in the fi elds of media and digital literacy education (e.g. Buckingham, 2019; Johnson et al., 2024; Kress, 2003; Levido et al., 2025; Merchant, 2023a; Mills, 2010).

To equate AI ' s engineered ability to produce syntactically complete and grammatically correct strings of words with human linguistic activity (Birhane and McGann, 2024), which invariably depends on external and internal context, is to understand literacy activity solely from an autonomous model of literacy perspective. That is, if educators and students mistakenly believe that what AI produces can replace human literacy activity, they are actually heading back to a time when literacy was simply understood as a selfcontained, autonomous system of conventions and rules (Street, 1995). As the Great Divide suggests, the view that there is a single standard for literacy, which tends to be incorporated in AI ' s machine learning, reinforces societal marginalization when users uncritically accept its output and internalize its

## The AI turn

Technological innovations in literacy tend to favor privileged classes who gain early access and thus the opportunity to define emerging norms (Gee, 2015a, 2015b; Graeber and Wengrow, 2021). In response, literacy scholars have sought ecologically situated and proactive approaches to technology (Buckingham, 2019; Foster et al., 2024; Johnson et al., 2024; Merchant, 2023b). In line with these scholars, we also note that reactive, technology-centred approaches to AI - whether solutionist or protectionist - can further entrench existing privileges. In contrast, we argue that the collective efforts of educators who adopt an ecologically situated and proactive approach to technology (as outlined in our framework) have the potential to benefit all learners, especially those who do not have ample opportunities to use and deeply reflect on technology and its output.

The trajectory of literacy has evolved from the introduction of writing to print technology and to what Mills (2010) termed the digital turn . Given the pervasive spread of digital technologies, the transitions from orality to writing and from single-script to mass production do not sufficiently accommodate the multimodal literacy practices of the current era (Merchant, 2023a; Mills, 2010), in which AI-assisted literacy practices are beginning to replace earlier forms of digital literacy. It is of note that the framework of the digital turn provides an analytical lens through which digital literacy practices and issues can be understood (Hull and Katz, 2020; Miller, 2013) well enough to find ways to ensure equitable access to digital tools, enable users to critically engage in digital literacy practices, and establish policies that protect individual confidentiality (Mills, 2010).

As AI-supported literacy practices are the latest manifestations of the digital turn, it is critical to clearly understand how AI differs from previous digital technologies as well as from human meaning-making activity as a basis for extending equity-based practices to AI contexts. This need led us to conceptualize the ' AI turn ' in literacy practices as a nuanced shift within the digital turn. The AI turn refers to new types of literacy emerging with the use of AI that go beyond print and non-generative technology-based literacies by enabling people to create and add new meanings based on information generated and synthesized by AI.

Some critics (e.g. Bender and Koller, 2020; Chomsky et al., 2023) argue, however, that these are the processes by which people develop creativity and critical thinking and should not be offloaded to a device that does their thinking for them. While acknowledging this point, we nevertheless suggest that, by receiving synthesized information in response to well-crafted questions and engaging in close and reflective reading of AI outputs, students can participate in further meaning-centred, critical, and creative processes. That is, an appropriate degree of automated resource synthesis can support rather than substitute for human meaning-making practices. Determining how this ideal can be achieved must be the primary goal, and the ICIC framework suggested below is one way to pursue it.

## ICIC framework for inclusive AI-assisted literacy teaching and learning

It is important to note that, unlike conventional technologies, in which human-to-human communication is the default interaction as people both write and read texts, a machine that generates language presents a fundamentally different relationship. As discussed earlier, resisting AI ' s standardized-value-prompting nature is essential. Without proper education, students may easily anthropomorphize AI as possessing human-like agency, given its fluent and confident language production. This misperception then may increase their tendency to accept AI outputs uncritically, a response that reinforces the homogenizing effects inherent in its statistical language mechanisms.

In response, literacy educators need a coherent and systematic framework to guide students in utilizing AI ' s potential for meaning-making that reflects critical, inclusive conceptions of literacy as a socially situated practice (Gee, 2015a, 2015b; Heath, 1983). To meet this need, we propose the ICIC Framework (see Figure 1).

In the following discussion, we first conceptualize the ICIC framework as a cyclical loop of a literacy skill set appropriate for applying the AI turn to the development of the widely recognized 4C competencies for human - human mediated technological environments: communication, collaboration, critical thinking, and creativity (Marope, 2014; Silber-Varod et al., 2019). We then note the differences between AI and other technologies to highlight unique features of human - AI interaction. For this purpose, we have replaced communication and collaboration, which are grounded in human - - human interactions, with two critical skills in interacting with AI: infusion of meaning and inquiry techniques . We have also modified the meanings of the other two 4C terms, critical thinking and creativity, to clarify what they specifically entail in the context of AI. As

## Inquiry techniques

Inquiry techniques in the ICIC framework begin with the ability to craft questions that elicit optimal outcomes in human - AI interactions. While similar to communication skills in the 4C framework, they differ in that students ' inquiry techniques, which are the sole incitements for meaning-making in the human - AI interface, should be more heavily emphasized. Literacy educators ' recognition of the importance of inquiry-based learning might extend to the use of AI. Inquiry practices should be understood not as a standard set of prompt-engineering skills (systematic chains of prompts, see Lee et al., 2025 for samples in education settings) to be learned and applied regardless of purpose and context but as situated approaches to discovering, examining, and evaluating knowledge. In this type of learning, students are encouraged to identify, explore, and resolve problems on their own rather than seeking often pre-determined solutions to problems designed for them (Jeon, 2025). Although AI can quickly provide answers and solutions, teachers need to foster curiosity among students and teach them how to craft inquiries that yield reliable information for them to develop their own answers and solutions. Theoretically, the need for learning inquiry techniques is rooted in Vygotsky ' s (1978) sociocultural theory, which posits that learning occurs when students are guided to construct knowledge through exploration and understanding.

Effective inquiry techniques also involve close analysis of AI outputs, as human - AI interaction rarely ends in a single exchange. In literacy education, this principle aligns with traditional concepts of reading as textual analysis (Buckingham, 2019). AI outputs should be subjected to close reading and treated as springboards for further inquiry. Buckingham ' s (2019) observation in the context of social media applies to interactions with AI: Textual comprehension based on discourse analysis and semiotics can help ' make the familiar strange ' (p. 70). ## Critical thinking

As inquiry techniques are about posing questions and analyzing what AI generates, critical thinking is about evaluating answers to those questions within the broader context in which the technology is situated. In settings where AI-produced texts are increasingly prevalent, the importance of critical thinking also increases. Critical thinking is a form of metacognition, defined as thinking about one ' s own thinking (Kuhn, 1999). An important takeaway from Kuhn ' s research on cognition is that students progress from viewing knowledge as absolute facts given by authorities toward more sophisticated epistemic thinking, whereby knowledge is understood as constructed, invariably fallible, and open to evaluation and change. Buckingham ' s (2019) approach situates critical thinking not only as an individual cognitive skill (Kuhn, 1999) but also as a socially embedded practice that involves two key dimensions related to societal structure: audience and production. The concept of audience emphasizes understanding how different groups engage with, interpret, and are influenced by technology. Production, on the other hand, involves examining the ownership and control of technology and data, as well as their implications for the outputs technology generates. In this context, questions about the degree to which AI represents different groups in its outputs and its accessibility to different populations are critical, along with those about concerns regarding big tech companies ' AI ownership, control of user privacy, and primary focus on profitability. Educators can further probe the epistemology of what AI produces (i.e. whether its outputs are open to critical inquiry and change) by asking: Who benefits the most and the least from AI technology? Who owns it? How are collected data managed? How can we make this technology inherently equitable and inclusive? How can we make this technology broadly accessible?

Further, students need to learn to validate what AI produces on their own, even when they do not possess advanced technical expertise, for which Russo et al. (2024) suggest leveraging argumentation theory, particularly the use of critical questions to assess claims. In practice, this means teaching students to treat AI outputs as arguments (rather than facts) and to interrogate them through structured questioning based on evidence-based reasoning. For example, students can ask: What reliable sources support this claim? What assumptions underlie this reasoning? Do other credible sources agree? Are there any interests at stake related to the claim and the company that owns the AI? These questions parallel those used to evaluate expert opinion in all fields and help students move beyond passive acceptance toward active validation. However, it is important to note that this process should occur after students are epistemologically prepared and understand how AI systems operate within a wider societal structure.

As one practical strategy for fostering critical comprehension skills in literacy instruction, Jeon (2025) presented students with biased paragraphs produced by ChatGPT and asked them to identify and critique the biases, which enabled them to become comfortable challenging texts that they might otherwise have considered authoritative, as is often the response to AI-generated content. They might then be challenged to write an argumentative essay using AI-generated content as support for their claims, which might further encourage them to evaluate the validity and strength of the evidence and reasoning presented by the AI.

## Infusion of meaning

In our model, the collaboration component of the 4C model, which focuses on human - human interaction, is replaced with infusion of meaning, as students need to learn how to intentionally bring reality-based meaning to what AI mechanically produces. If students unconditionally accept and use outputs at face value without engaging with them transactionally, that is, without infusing meaning into AI-produced text, the statistical and extrapolative nature of AI, which leans toward homogenized patterns, results in derivative thinking. In this regard, it is important for students to understand that they are partners in the construction of meaning in any text they read and that their ability to connect AI-generated content to their own lives, experiences, and shared understandings with others that have shaped their schemas is a vital part of the process. Most importantly, educators need to frame literacy productions as ' situated practices ' that are inherently in process and open to supplementation and interpretation by others (e.g. Burnett and Merchant, 2018; Merchant, 2023a), rather than as completed outcomes, which reflects the way that AI is designed to operate (Olson, 1977; Ong, 1982).

The fi rst step toward infusing meaning into AI-produced text is to have students prompt AI in ways that lead it to simulate human reasoning processes rather than simply consume its outputs. That is, rather than accept what the tool produces at face value, students might be able to prompt AI to generate alternative perspectives, counterarguments, or reasoning chains related to their initial ideas. This approach aligns with process-based learning theories by making thinking visible (Aljaafreh and Lantolf, 1994). In this process, students learn to engage with AI as a dialogic partner for constructing and refining arguments rather than as a shortcut to answers (Jeon, 2025). For example, in a

Next, educators should encourage students to contextualize AI outputs in ways that relate to their lived experiences in their local communities and their communities of practice (James, 1890; Lave and Wenger, 1991), in this case their literacy or subject area classrooms. Finally, the infusion of meaning should include action-oriented components, which may take the form of tangible outcomes or at least community validation (Lave and Wenger, 1991). In other words, students ' AI-supported reasoning and innovative ideas should be tested in real-world contexts. For example, after critically validating basic facts about snakes and identifying local conservation issues and initiatives, students can share their findings with community members to confirm how well their explanations and arguments, conceptualized and strengthened with the help of AI, align with the community ' s needs and values. If alignment is achieved, these ideas can serve as a foundation for initiating interventions.

Notably, critical thinking plays an important role in the process of infusion of meaning. Students should learn that AI-generated texts can be articulate and coherent, yet shallow or irrelevant. Rather than automatically attributing meaningfulness to AI-generated outputs, students can be encouraged to ask: Does this sentence (paragraph, passage, etc.) actually say something relevant to me or the community? Does it address the question in a deep, attentive way, or is it just superficially coherent and reproducing what already exists? Those who develop the ability to infuse meaning into AI-generated texts will be able to identify superficial or irrelevant content and discard it in pursuit of content that is meaningful and relevant to their lives and purposes. By emphasizing the infusion of meaning, educators can ensure that in an increasingly AI-powered setting, students remain focused on what is unique to human meaning-making activity, including understanding, personal connection, and thoughtful communication.

## Creativity

Creativity in the context of AI use is defined as the ability to craft meaning-infused constructions into original presentations that provide new insights and evoke further thinking. Creativity goes beyond the infusion of meaning to levels of understanding that surpass the capabilities of technology, which should be seen as a platform, not a destination, for learning. In settings where AI can easily produce formulaic, normative outcomes, the unique constructions humans can make often emerge as original, surprising, passionate, disruptive, and groundbreaking ideas that fall outside predictable and pre-emptive patterns. Even when AI does produce exceptional outputs, these differ from human meaning-making because they lack intentionality grounded in shared human understanding (Birhane and McGann, 2024). AI does not produce content because it has internal or external motivation to express something or to solve problems but in response to cues in the language it receives and generates. In contrast, humans create for such reasons as to understand or reveal some aspect of the world, to persuade or entertain others, to resolve tensions, to meet unique needs, and to experience joy in the process, using literacy for meaning-making practices ranging from writing technical manuals for specific products to coining neologisms that sometimes make their way into the lexicon or vanish over time.

First, to foster creativity, it is important for educators to understand the power of speculation and imagination (e.g. Shaw et al., 2024; Wohlwend, 2008), particularly of that which, while impossible to achieve in the present, may become possible in the future. As we have observed in the emergence of different types of literacy (e.g. media literacy, news literacy, or algorithm literacy), the definition of literacy has been fluidly expanded to include what people might need to understand in order to navigate the ever-changing and future-oriented landscape of reading and writing (Nichols et al., 2024). Rather than being constrained by what is currently possible with AI, literacy education should encourage students to speculate about AI and its impact and imagine in whatever way possible such as possible futures in which AI becomes much more prevalent or completely obsolete. Students need to be guided into imaginatively reflecting on issues relevant to their own future: What will happen if we do not regulate big tech companies? What will happen if a government controls the data available to all citizens ' personalized chatbots? What if individuals have personalized robots engineered to blur the meaning of being human? Next, educators need to challenge students to articulate their own values regarding the use of AI. Instead of having their imaginations and reasoning tamed into thinking in ways that are embedded in AI, students need to shape their own ideas and values regarding how reading and writing might function in an increasingly rich but unfiltered information environment and how they want AI to impact their own lives and their communities. Some might simply assume that such speculation can also be delegated to AI - that they can ask it to imagine these futures. However, what humans do to create and share values and beliefs is fundamentally different from what machines do. One example might be people ' s tendency to coin new terms that reflect their individual/group identities (Birhane and McGann, 2024), as can be seen in the use of the neologism-related phenomenon Algospeak , used to avoid algorithmic monitoring on the internet in order to share communications only with members of an inside group. As discussed in Jeon et al. (2025), once AI captures Algospeak, the term immediately loses its purpose. On a similar note, machines cannot conceive of social movements such as Me Too or Black Lives Matter or alter their connotations as both supporters and detractors do. These movements become relevant to people ' s lives as a matter of lived experience rather than as language alone, and they cannot be reproduced simply through big data or machine learning algorithms because these value creations are specific to each individual ' s perspective and experience, which are themselves subject to evolution.

Finally, these values and beliefs should not remain theoretical or simply reflective but lead to actions (James, 1890). Students, as representatives of their communities ' values and beliefs, should feed them back into the landscape of AI development and use in society. Current foundational models, characterized by their reliance on internet data and processed in so-called black boxes largely in pursuit of profitability, can be counterbalanced, if partially and gradually, by other personalized AI products that students imagine or develop. These eventually can enter public discourse, foundational data sources, and open-source materials that can initiate other personalized, quality-focused AI systems that do not strictly adhere to benchmarks celebrating only efficiency.

What is most important in the age of AI is that this literacy education pathway toward a more diversified and inclusive approach based on the four components of our ICIC framework (inquiry techniques, infusion of meaning, critical thinking, and creativity) is cyclical. We envision a future where students ' creativity, grounded in thorough learning of the first three components, contributes to more inclusive AI-human collaborations and products in use environments which, while still artificial, will be far less homogenizing. However, this future will require students to become more inquisitive, critical, meaning-focused, and creative in each new cycle than in the previous one; guiding this progressive development will be the essential role that educators should play.

Moving away from the autonomous model of literacy might help us rethink AI ' s role in literacy education. Students are not in competition with AI for producing written texts. Machines will always be faster at generating outputs than humans, but it should be understood that their production processes are essentially different from human meaning-making activity. AI is not an infallible oracle; it is merely a tool that supports literacy practices when infused with students ' personal resources including their histories, original insights, humor, empathy, and divergent thinking. This conceptualization also helps students adapt to a future in which routine tasks may be fully automated, often producing texts that are coherent, fl uent, and grammatically correct but are not fi nished results. Rather, they are material to which humans add relevant meaning and value by thinking critically and creatively beyond what algorithms can achieve. As Chomsky et al. (2023) note, human intelligence arises from creative conjectures and critiques, not merely from description and prediction, which are the typical outputs of AI. Students should learn to harness AI ' s capacity to synthesize diverse information quickly and use it to construct and apply their own ideas and perspectives as creative meaning-makers.

## Conclusion

As we have discussed so far, it is important to understand the ways in which AI operates and how it can be differentiated from human linguistic activity. For educators to actualize the four components of the ICIC framework discussed above, we emphasize the need to fi rst create environments where students can openly share their use of AI and develop strong moral dispositions against using it unethically and uncritically.

Also, echoing Buckingham ' s (2019) call for governmental regulation to ensure the fair and transparent development of technology, along with structural support for implementing systematic educational

Both students and educators need to learn how to cope with AI ' s standardized-valueprompting nature, which should be addressed in professional development programs to teach and discuss necessary skills as well as prioritized in all classroom uses of AI. Equitable access to AI in public education might also be expanded to the larger society, and more transparent and comprehensive regulations for big tech companies ' data collection and control should be implemented. We can also create public venues where students ' imaginative speculations about possible future scenarios regarding AI can be tested and explored.

As the digital turn in literacy practices has kindled discussion on equity issues, the focus on the ' AI turn ' in literacy scholarship and the framework we have introduced may provide a starting point from which educators can pay increasing attention to such ethical issues as the power dynamics behind the uses of AI and how they affect its inclusive uses in classrooms. Researchers and educators must be both critical and creative in designing equitable literacy education that draws on AI ' s affordances without allowing the technology itself to determine the nature and quality of education.

Moving forward, it is recommended that researchers investigate students ' actual AI-assisted meaning-making practices based on each component of the ICIC framework. We also recommend further exploration of AI-assisted multimodal approaches to literacy practices and development. We invite critical examination of each component of the ICIC framework introduced in the current paper and further expansion or modifications of the suggested visions for the future. As humanity speeds into an increasingly sophisticated technological future, this paper may provide a stimulus for intensified inquiry into ways to equip today ' s learners with the knowledge, skills, and dispositions they will need to go ' beyond the information given ' (Bruner, 1973) in tomorrow ' s world of AI-powered literacies.
