## Bibliographic Metadata

Aljasser, Amal. 2025. "Investigating EFL students' perceptions of feedback: a comparative study of instructor and ChatGPT-generated responses in academic writing." *Education and Information Technologies*. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-025-13649-8.

## One-sentence Summary

In a mixed-methods investigation of 112 advanced Saudi undergraduate EFL learners across two feedback scenarios, students valued ChatGPT-4 for speed, accessibility, and surface-level linguistic correction, but consistently preferred instructor feedback for personalization, affective support, pedagogical depth, and structural/methodological revision (pp. 1, 8–13).

# Structured Summary

## Purpose

The study investigates how Saudi English as a Foreign Language (EFL) undergraduate students perceive and compare instructor-generated feedback and ChatGPT-generated feedback within an advanced academic writing course. Rather than evaluating AI feedback in isolation, the study directly contrasts instructor and AI feedback across concurrent writing stages to understand their relative impact on writing performance, clarity, structure, motivation, confidence, and learner preferences (pp. 1–2).

## Background (Theory & Literature Review)

The theoretical framework draws on **Vygotsky's (1978) socio-cultural theory**, specifically the *Zone of Proximal Development* (ZPD), framing feedback as pedagogical scaffolding that enables learners to bridge the gap between their actual and potential development levels under guidance (p. 2). This is complemented by **constructivist learning theory**, which posits feedback as an interactive, reflective process of active revision and knowledge construction (p. 2).

Within L2 writing research, the literature review contrasts **direct corrective feedback** (providing explicit corrections, often preferred for lower proficiency) with **indirect feedback** (signaling error locations to prompt student problem-solving and deeper cognitive engagement) (p. 3). Emerging scholarship on Generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Grammarly) highlights affordances such as real-time access, objectivity, and iterative drafting opportunities (pp. 2–3). However, substantial concerns persist regarding AI's lack of true personalization, generic output, absence of deep structural/rhetorical engagement, and risk of learner overreliance (pp. 3–4). Regional studies in Saudi Arabia and the Middle East demonstrate that while learners welcome AI for grammatical accuracy and preliminary organization, they consistently rely on human instructor feedback for critical thinking, complex rhetorical analysis, and contextual nuance (pp. 3–4).

## Method

- **Design:** Mixed-methods design combining quantitative survey items (4-point Likert scale) with qualitative thematic analysis of open-ended responses.
- **Context & Participants:** An online advanced academic writing course at a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, conducted via Microsoft Teams. Participants were 112 senior undergraduate EFL students enrolled in an English Language and Translation program, characterized as CEFR C2-level learners (p. 5).
- **Assignments:** Four sequential research paper sections: Assignment 1 (Introduction), Assignment 2 (Literature Review), Assignment 3 (Methodology), and Assignment 4 (Results & Discussion) (p. 5).
- **Procedure (Two Scenarios):**
  - *Scenario 1 (Assignments 1 & 2):* Blended/concealed feedback. Students submitted drafts to the instructor, who provided combined feedback incorporating both her own assessment (as in-text comments) and ChatGPT-generated evaluation (labeled as "overall comments"). Students were unaware of AI involvement (pp. 5–6).
  - *Scenario 2 (Assignments 3 & 4):* Explicit independent AI consultation. Students were explicitly required to consult ChatGPT-4 independently for initial feedback before submitting their drafts to the instructor, who then provided independent instructor feedback (p. 6).
- **Instruments & Analysis:** Survey A (post-Scenario 1) and Survey B (post-Scenario 2) measured perceptions of clarity, relevance, usefulness, timeliness, accessibility, personalization, anxiety, self-confidence, fairness, and relative preference. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA, and a six-phase thematic analysis protocol (pp. 6–7).

## Findings

- **Perceptions of AI Feedback (RQ1):** Students acknowledged that ChatGPT provided clear, highly relevant, timely, and accessible suggestions (mean scores on relevance reached 1.03–1.14 on a 1-to-4 scale where 1 represents Strongly Agree) (pp. 8–9). However, agreement was weaker regarding personalization (means 1.50–1.93) and affective support (pp. 8–9).
- **Relative Effectiveness Across Writing Aspects (RQ2):** ChatGPT was deemed highly effective for surface-level mechanical concerns, syntax, and sentence organization. In contrast, instructor feedback was rated significantly more effective for global organization, coherence, academic clarity, methodology, and argumentative depth (pp. 10–11).
- **Motivation & Confidence (RQ3):** Instructor feedback exerted a substantially greater positive effect on student motivation and self-esteem while more effectively mitigating assignment anxiety. The personal relationship, constructive tone, and perceived empathy of the instructor provided essential reassurance that AI could not replicate (pp. 10–12).
- **ChatGPT Affordances & Challenges (RQ4):** Affordances included immediate turnaround, round-the-clock accessibility, and encouragement of autonomous revision. Key challenges included superficiality, generic commentary, inability to address complex methodological errors, and an overwhelming volume of suggestions that burdened students during prioritization (pp. 10–12).
- **Quantitative Group Comparisons:** Total response means across tasks were R1-A1 (M = 1.48), R2-A2 (M = 1.34), R3-A3 (M = 1.59), and R4-A4 (M = 1.64). A one-way ANOVA indicated no statistically significant difference across the four assignment stages (*F* = 2.40, *p* = 0.072), indicating relatively stable evaluative patterns across the semester (p. 8).

## Discussion & Conclusion

The findings substantiate a **complementary hybrid feedback model**. While ChatGPT serves as an effective first-pass revision assistant for mechanical polish and preliminary structure, it cannot substitute for the teacher's contextual, disciplinary, and affective scaffolding. Human instructors remain essential for interpreting rhetorical intent, guiding complex methodological reasoning, fostering feedback literacy, and providing motivational support. Writing instruction should strategically integrate AI for lower-level drafting while reserving instructor capacity for higher-order academic concerns (pp. 11–14).

## Purpose

The study addresses a defined empirical gap in computer-assisted language learning and AI writing pedagogy: while prior studies investigated either AI feedback or human feedback in isolation, few empirical studies have directly compared learner perceptions of both sources operating simultaneously within an authentic academic writing curriculum (pp. 1–2). The study's specific aims are:
1. To compare EFL undergraduate perceptions of instructor feedback versus ChatGPT-generated feedback.
2. To determine the relative effectiveness of each feedback mode across discrete writing dimensions (clarity, organization, grammar, methodology).
3. To evaluate the affective impact of both feedback types on student motivation, self-confidence, and anxiety.
4. To identify the practical benefits and operational challenges of integrating ChatGPT into university-level academic writing instruction.

## Theory & Literature Review

The paper establishes its conceptual foundation across three interconnected domains:

1. **Sociocultural & Constructivist Foundations:**
   - Citing **Vygotsky (1978)**, feedback is defined as pedagogical scaffolding operating within the learner's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): *"the distance between the actual development level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peer"* (p. 86 / paper p. 2).
   - In constructivist models (Ferris, 2012; Bitchener, 2008), feedback is an interactive, recursive cycle where learners actively negotiate meaning, reflect on critique, and iteratively reconstruct text (p. 2).

2. **Corrective Feedback in L2 Writing:**
   - Distinguishes **direct feedback** (supplying target linguistic forms, beneficial for novice/low-proficiency writers) from **indirect feedback** (indicating error presence without providing corrections, prompting active cognitive problem-solving and engagement) (Eslami, 2014; Ferris & Roberts, 2001; paper p. 3).
   - Highlights the importance of **personalized feedback** (Koenka & Anderman, 2019): *"Personalized feedback, or specific, student-centered information delivered to students about their performance in a motivationally optimal manner, is a vital and feasible strategy... to support their students' learning and motivation"* (p. 15 / paper p. 3).

3. **Generative AI in Writing & Contextual Evidence:**
   - Reviews findings on LLM feedback affordances: rapid turnaround, accessibility, non-judgmental repetition, and grammatical density enhancement (Guo et al., 2024; Teng, 2024; paper pp. 2–3).
   - Examines documented limitations: lack of pedagogical personalization, superficial or generic commentary, inability to evaluate complex rhetorical structures, and risks of learner overreliance (Kostka & Toncelli, 2023; Lundberg, 2024; Polakova & Ivenz, 2024; paper pp. 3–4).
   - Synthesizes regional Middle Eastern and Saudi higher-education studies (AlGhamdi, 2024; Alnasser, 2022; Al-Sofi, 2024; Alshammri, 2024; Alzahrani & Alotaibi, 2024), finding a consistent pattern: Saudi EFL students readily utilize AI tools for surface-level linguistic mechanics but express an explicit preference for teacher feedback when addressing critical analysis, research methodology, and complex academic discourse (pp. 3–4).

## Gaps & Research Questions

**The Research Gap:** Prior literature predominantly examined AI tools or instructor feedback as separate treatments across different cohorts, leaving a gap regarding how advanced learners perceive and reconcile both sources when experienced concurrently in an authentic writing course (p. 2).

**Research Questions:**
- **RQ1:** How do EFL students perceive the feedback provided by their instructor compared to feedback generated by ChatGPT in improving their academic writing skills? (p. 4)
- **RQ2:** What is the relative effectiveness of instructor feedback versus AI-generated feedback in enhancing specific aspects of academic writing (e.g., clarity, structure, coherence)? (p. 4)
- **RQ3:** How do different feedback types (instructor vs. AI) impact students' motivation and confidence in writing academic papers? (p. 4)
- **RQ4:** What are the key challenges or benefits that students associate with using ChatGPT for academic feedback? (p. 4)

## Theoretical Framework

The study synthesizes:
- **Scaffolding within ZPD (Vygotsky, 1978):** Feedback functions as a dynamic mediating artifact calibrated to learner development.
- **Constructivist Revision Theory (Ferris, 2012):** Writing development occurs through reflective, learner-driven revision stimulated by dialogic feedback.
- **Feedback Literacy Framework (Yu & Liu, 2021):** Learners must cultivate the cognitive, affective, and operational capacity to interpret, evaluate, and enact feedback from diverse human and automated agents.

## Methodology

### Context
Conducted within an online, two-semester advanced academic writing course at a major public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Delivered via Microsoft Teams, the course required students to complete an original four-part research paper across structured two-week cycles (p. 5).

### Participants
- **Sample Size:** $N = 112$ senior undergraduate EFL students (English Language and Translation major) (p. 5).
- **Proficiency Level:** CEFR C2-level (near-native advanced comprehension and production), ensuring participants had the linguistic competence to critically evaluate nuanced academic feedback (p. 5).
- **Instructor Consistency:** The same instructor taught all cohorts across both semesters, maintaining uniform grading criteria, rubric application, and pedagogical delivery (p. 6).

### Data Sources
- **Writing Submissions:** Four sequential Word document assignments graded via standardized rubrics (Appendices A–D):
  - Assignment 1 (A1): Introduction & Background (10% intro, 20% purpose, 20% RQs, 20% significance, 20% delimitations, 10% style) (p. 15).
  - Assignment 2 (A2): Literature Review.
  - Assignment 3 (A3): Methodology.
  - Assignment 4 (A4): Results and Discussion.
- **Survey Instruments:**
  - **Survey A (Post-Scenario 1):** 10 Likert-scale items assessing feedback clarity, relevance, usefulness, timeliness, accessibility, personalization, anxiety reduction, self-confidence, and fairness; 2 comparative items; 2 open-ended qualitative prompts (pp. 6, 14).
  - **Survey B (Post-Scenario 2):** 10 parallel Likert-scale items evaluating independent ChatGPT consultation vs. instructor feedback; 2 comparative items; 3 open-ended prompts (including submission of raw ChatGPT feedback logs) (pp. 6, 15).
  - Both surveys used a forced-choice 4-point Likert scale ($1 = \text{Strongly Agree}, 2 = \text{Agree}, 3 = \text{Disagree}, 4 = \text{Strongly Disagree}$) to eliminate neutral midpoint bias (p. 6).

### Procedure
- **Scenario 1 (Blended/Concealed AI Use — Tasks 1 & 2):** Students submitted A1 and A2. The instructor provided combined feedback consisting of her own detailed in-text annotations plus ChatGPT-4 output presented as "overall comments." Students were blind to AI involvement (pp. 5–6).
- **Scenario 2 (Explicit Autonomous AI Consultation — Tasks 3 & 4):** Students were instructed to consult ChatGPT-4 independently on drafts of A3 and A4 before formal submission. The instructor subsequently reviewed the revised submissions and provided independent in-text feedback without consulting AI (p. 6).

### Analysis Methods
- **Quantitative:** Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals) and inferential one-way ANOVA across the four task rounds (R1-A1, R2-A2, R3-A3, R4-A4) (pp. 7–8).
- **Qualitative:** Thematic analysis of open-ended responses adhering to the six-phase framework (familiarization, initial coding, theme search, theme review, theme definition/naming, reporting) (Bernard & Ryan, 2010; Guest et al., 2012; Vaismoradi et al., 2013; paper p. 7).

## Findings

### RQ1: Perceptions of Instructor vs. ChatGPT Feedback

- **High Agreement on Clarity & Timeliness:** ChatGPT feedback was rated highly for clarity and immediate relevance. In R1-A1, relevance scored $M = 1.03$ ($SD = 0.192$), and in R2-A2, $M = 1.14$ ($SD = 0.448$). Timeliness scored $M = 1.14$ to $1.31$ across tasks (p. 8).
- **Lower Agreement on Personalization:** Personalization ratings for ChatGPT were noticeably weaker, ranging from $M = 1.50$ ($SD = 0.962$) in R2-A2 to $M = 1.93$ ($SD = 1.032$) in R3-A3 (p. 8).
- **Preference for Instructor In-Text Comments:** When asked whether in-text instructor comments were most helpful, students expressed near-unanimous agreement in Scenario 2 ($M = 1.17$, $SD = 0.384$ in R3-A3; $M = 1.28$, $SD = 0.658$ in R4-A4). Conversely, when asked if ChatGPT feedback was most helpful, agreement dropped sharply in later independent tasks ($M = 2.44$, $SD = 0.909$ in R3-A3; $M = 2.53$, $SD = 0.961$ in R4-A4) (p. 9).
- **ANOVA Results (Table 2):** Overall mean perceptual scores across tasks were R1-A1 ($M = 1.48$, $SD = 0.345$), R2-A2 ($M = 1.34$, $SD = 0.352$), R3-A3 ($M = 1.59$, $SD = 0.446$), and R4-A4 ($M = 1.64$, $SD = 0.637$). The one-way ANOVA yielded $F = 2.40$, $p = 0.072$, indicating no statistically significant variance across task cycles at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level (p. 8).

### RQ2: Relative Effectiveness Across Writing Dimensions

- **Surface Mechanics vs. Global Structure:** Qualitative coding revealed that ChatGPT excelled in identifying grammatical errors, tense consistency, mechanical slips, and sentence-level phrasing improvements (p. 10).
- **Higher-Order Rhetorical & Disciplinary Concerns:** Instructor feedback was decisively favored for macro-level organization, coherence, academic tone, research delimitation clarity, and methodological justification (pp. 10–11).
- **Ambiguity in Generic AI Guidance:** Students reported frustration when AI feedback provided vague directives (e.g., "use better transitions") without contextualized models, whereas instructor annotations provided actionable, task-specific examples (p. 10).

### RQ3: Motivation, Self-Confidence, and Affect

- **Affective Superiority of Human Feedback:** Instructor feedback exerted a significantly stronger positive emotional impact. Students reported feeling validated, supported, and less anxious when receiving constructive human guidance ($M = 1.64\text{--}1.82$ for confidence boosts across tasks) (pp. 8, 12).
- **AI Feedback Limitations in Affective Support:** While AI was appreciated for non-judgmental speed, it failed to alleviate assignment-related anxiety ($M = 2.00$ in R4-A4) because learners felt AI comments lacked genuine pedagogical investment or empathy (pp. 8, 12).

### RQ4: Benefits and Challenges of ChatGPT Feedback

- **Affordances:** Instant turnaround, 24/7 availability, iterative pre-submission checking, and promotion of autonomous drafting behaviors (pp. 10–12).
- **Challenges:**
  1. *Lack of Contextual/Disciplinary Awareness:* AI struggled to evaluate methodological rigor and nuanced data interpretation (p. 12).
  2. *Feedback Overload:* Extensive, unprioritized lists of AI suggestions created cognitive burden and confusion regarding revision hierarchy (pp. 10–12).
  3. *Absence of Pedagogical Personalization:* Inability to adapt critiques to individual student growth trajectories or specific course rubrics (p. 12).

## Key Claims

1. **Complementary Role:** ChatGPT is highly effective as a supplementary, first-pass revision tool for surface-level linguistic accuracy and mechanical formatting, but it is inadequate as a stand-alone replacement for instructor feedback (pp. 1, 13).
2. **Pedagogical Depth & Personalization:** Human instructor feedback remains essential for complex academic discourse, structural organization, methodological soundness, and rhetorical coherence (pp. 11–13).
3. **Affective Scaffolding:** Emotional reassurance, motivational reinforcement, and anxiety reduction in high-stakes academic writing depend fundamentally on the perceived authenticity and empathy of human instructor feedback (p. 12).
4. **Cognitive Burden of AI Overload:** Unfiltered AI feedback generation can overwhelm learners, necessitating instructor mediation to guide feedback prioritization and revision literacy (pp. 10–12).
5. **Hybrid Instructional Framework:** Language educators should adopt structured hybrid workflows that integrate AI speed for mechanical polishing while preserving teacher intervention for higher-order intellectual development (pp. 12–13).

## Discussion

The discussion contextualizes the findings within contemporary writing research and sociocultural theory:

- **Validation of Sociocultural Scaffolding:** The strong preference for instructor feedback corroborates **Bitchener and Knoch (2010)** and **Ferris (2012)**, reinforcing that effective corrective feedback requires contextual awareness of the learner's individual developmental trajectory and task demands (p. 11).
- **Speed vs. Substance in AI Feedback:** While the immediacy of ChatGPT aligns with findings by **Teng (2024)** and **Guo et al. (2024)**, its lack of authentic personalization supports warnings by **Kostka and Toncelli (2023)** and **Cheng (2024)** regarding superficial and formulaic commentary (pp. 11–12).
- **Rhetorical and Methodological Limitations:** The finding that ChatGPT struggled with methodology and analytical structure confirms **Lundberg's (2024)** conclusion that LLMs lack the domain-specific reasoning required for higher-order academic prose (p. 12).
- **Affective & Relational Dimensions:** The study highlights that feedback is not merely an informational transaction; it is a relational act. Automated systems cannot provide the psychological validation necessary to reduce academic writing anxiety, supporting **Song and Song (2023)** and **Polakova and Ivenz (2024)** (p. 12).

## Conclusion

The study concludes that while AI tools like ChatGPT-4 provide substantial logistical and mechanical benefits (speed, round-the-clock availability, surface grammar error identification), human instructor feedback remains indispensable for university-level academic writing instruction. Instructors provide the critical scaffolding, disciplinary contextualization, task-specific nuance, and emotional encouragement that drive meaningful writing development. Consequently, writing programs should deliberately architect **hybrid pedagogical frameworks** that leverage AI for initial drafting while centering instructor expertise on high-level argumentation and revision (p. 13).

## Unique Contributions

1. **Empirical Direct Comparison:** One of the earliest empirical studies in the Gulf/Saudi EFL context to directly compare instructor and ChatGPT-4 feedback within the same cohort and curriculum across multiple authentic assignment cycles (pp. 4–5).
2. **Dual-Scenario Methodological Design:** Novel methodological separation between concealed AI use (Scenario 1) and autonomous student AI consultation (Scenario 2), isolating awareness effects from tool efficacy (pp. 5–6).
3. **Affective & Cognitive Load Insights:** Identification of "AI feedback overload" as an emerging cognitive challenge, alongside empirical documentation of AI's limitations in alleviating writing anxiety (pp. 10–12).

## Limitations

- **Sample Specificity:** Conducted at a single public university in Saudi Arabia exclusively with senior, high-proficiency (CEFR C2) translation students, limiting direct generalizability to beginner/intermediate learners or diverse cultural settings (p. 13).
- **Single Model & Rapid LLM Evolution:** Evaluated only ChatGPT-4 (general-purpose model), excluding specialized academic writing assistants (e.g., Grammarly, Writefull, Elicit, Scite) or subsequent LLM iterations (p. 13).
- **Instructor Confound:** The same instructor delivered all instruction and human feedback, meaning teacher personality and instructional style may have influenced student perceptions (p. 6).
- **Qualitative Reporting Detail:** Thematic analysis procedures, while structured, lack explicit inter-coder reliability metrics or full coding codebooks (p. 7).

## Future Work

- **Longitudinal & Diverse Cohort Studies:** Multi-semester investigations tracking writing proficiency growth over time across diverse student proficiency bands (A2–B2) (p. 13).
- **Comparative AI Tool Benchmarking:** Direct comparative studies testing general-purpose LLMs against specialized, domain-trained academic writing platforms (p. 13).
- **Adaptive Hybrid Interface Design:** Development of pedagogical interfaces that automatically filter and organize AI suggestions according to instructor rubrics before student review (p. 12).
- **Objective Writing Gains Measurement:** Incorporating pre- and post-test standardized writing performance metrics alongside perceptual survey data (pp. 13–14).
