# Structured Summary

## Purpose
To examine how Saudi EFL learners perceive the effectiveness of ChatGPT-generated feedback in comparison to instructor feedback in developing academic writing skills, and to investigate learners' preferences, motivation, confidence, and performance regarding both feedback types.

## Background (Theory & Literature Review)
- Feedback is a crucial factor in language learning, particularly for EFL learners mastering complex linguistic conventions in academic writing.
- Grounded in **Vygotsky's socio-cultural theory** (Zone of Proximal Development; ZPD) and **constructivist learning theory**, feedback serves as scaffolding that guides students through challenging tasks.
- Feedback types include **direct feedback** (instructor provides correct form) and **indirect feedback** (instructor indicates errors without correction).
- AI tools such as ChatGPT offer immediate, accessible, consistent, and objective feedback, but prior research notes concerns about lack of personalization, generic comments, and limited contextual depth.
- Previous studies in Saudi/Middle Eastern contexts found AI tools beneficial for grammar accuracy and organization, yet students preferred instructor feedback for assignments requiring critical thinking, complex analysis, and rhetorical skills.
- **Research gap:** Most prior studies examined AI feedback and instructor feedback in isolation. The concurrent impact of both types on motivation, engagement, and writing performance remains underexplored.

## Method
Mixed-methods design combining quantitative (4-point Likert-scale surveys) and qualitative (thematic analysis of open-ended responses) approaches. ChatGPT-4 was used as the AI tool.

## Findings
- Students generally found instructor feedback more useful and personalized than ChatGPT feedback.
- ChatGPT feedback was valued for being immediate, accessible, and clear in identifying areas for improvement.
- Instructor feedback was more effective for revising general organization, clarity, and content structure.
- ChatGPT was more effective for surface-level corrections (grammar, sentence organization).
- Instructor feedback had a greater positive effect on students' motivation, confidence, and anxiety reduction.
- Key challenges of ChatGPT included lack of personalization, generic comments, overwhelming volume of feedback, and ineffectiveness for methodological and analytical aspects.
- ANOVA results showed no statistically significant differences across assignment groups (F = 2.40, p = 0.072).

## Discussion & Conclusion
- Human feedback remains preferable for most students due to its individualized, detailed, and emotionally supportive nature.
- AI tools are useful complements but should not replace instructor feedback.
- A **hybrid model** combining AI-generated feedback for preliminary revisions and instructor feedback for higher-order concerns is recommended.
- Future feedback systems should embed mechanisms for instructor input and adaptive learning algorithms to address current AI limitations.

# Deep Summary

## Purpose
This study addresses the gap in research comparing the effectiveness of AI-generated feedback (specifically ChatGPT-4) and instructor feedback concurrently. It aims to compare the effect of both feedback types on EFL learners' academic writing skills and to investigate their preferences, motivation, confidence, and performance.

## Theory & Literature Review
- **Vygotsky (1978)** defines the ZPD as "the distance between the actual development level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peer" (p. 86; source_page-verified unavailable in parsed text).
- **Koenka and Anderman (2019)** define personalized feedback as "specific, student-centered information delivered to students about their performance in a motivationally optimal manner" (p. 15; source_page-verified unavailable).
- Prior studies (AlGhamdi 2024; Alshammri 2024; Teng 2024) report that AI tools provide immediate, consistent, and accessible feedback but note concerns about consistency, personalization, and over-reliance.
- Saudi-context studies (Al-Sofi 2024; Alnasser 2022; Alotaibi 2023) indicate students prefer instructor feedback for complex tasks while using AI for lower-level linguistic accuracy.

## Gaps & Research Questions
**Gap:** Previous studies reported results for each feedback type in isolation. The impact of using both types concurrently on motivation, engagement, and writing performance is underexplored.

- **RQ1:** How do EFL students perceive instructor feedback compared to ChatGPT feedback in improving academic writing skills?
- **RQ2:** What is the relative effectiveness of instructor versus AI-generated feedback in enhancing specific aspects of academic writing (clarity, structure, coherence)?
- **RQ3:** How do different feedback types impact students' motivation and confidence?
- **RQ4:** What are the key challenges or benefits students associate with using ChatGPT for academic feedback?

## Theoretical Framework
- **Vygotsky's socio-cultural theory** (ZPD): Feedback acts as scaffolding within the learner's zone of proximal development.
- **Constructivist learning theory**: Feedback is an interactive process where learners actively revise knowledge through reflection and instructor input.

## Methodology

### Context
Online advanced academic writing course at a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, conducted via Microsoft Teams. Students produced a research paper divided into four assignments: introduction, literature review, methodology, and results/discussion.

### Participants
112 senior EFL undergraduate students enrolled in the English Language and Translation program. All were C2-level learners according to CEFR, in their final year of undergraduate studies.

### Data Sources
- Four writing assignments (A1–A4)
- **Survey A** (after Tasks 1–2): 10 Likert-scale items + open-ended questions about combined feedback (students unaware of ChatGPT involvement)
- **Survey B** (after Tasks 3–4): 10 Likert-scale items + open-ended questions comparing self-consulted ChatGPT feedback with instructor feedback
- Both surveys used a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Agree, 4 = Strongly Disagree) to eliminate neutrality

### Procedure
- **Scenario 1 (Tasks 1–2):** Students submitted assignments to the instructor, who provided combined feedback from her own evaluation and ChatGPT. Students were unaware of ChatGPT's involvement; ChatGPT comments were labeled as "overall comments."
- **Scenario 2 (Tasks 3–4):** Students were explicitly instructed to consult ChatGPT independently for feedback before submitting to the instructor, who then provided her own feedback without consulting ChatGPT.
- The same instructor taught all 112 students across two semesters to ensure consistency.

### Analysis Methods
- **Quantitative:** Descriptive and inferential statistics; ANOVA to compare mean scores across groups.
- **Qualitative:** Thematic analysis following six phases (familiarization, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining/naming themes, producing the report) based on Vaismoradi et al. (2013) and Guest et al. (2012).

## Findings

- **RQ1 (Perceptions):** Students found ChatGPT feedback clear and relevant, with mean scores for relevance as low as 1.03 (strong agreement). However, instructor feedback was rated more helpful, especially in-text comments. Students felt instructor feedback was more individualized and detailed.
  - Evidence: "students felt that the instructor's feedback was more useful than ChatGPT's feedback" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)
  - Evidence: "ChatGPT's feedback was immediate and accessible" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)

- **RQ2 (Effectiveness by aspect):** Instructor feedback was most effective for revising general organization and clarity. ChatGPT feedback was effective for grammar and sentence organization but less effective for content and structural issues.
  - Evidence: "instructor feedback was most effective for revising the general organization and clarity of students' writing" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)
  - Evidence: "AI tools are more effective in providing feedback for grammar and sentence organization" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)

- **RQ3 (Motivation and confidence):** Instructor feedback had a greater positive effect on motivation and confidence. Students felt more supported and less anxious with instructor feedback. ChatGPT feedback was considered less encouraging.
  - Evidence: "The effect size of instructor feedback was greater than that of AI feedback on students' motivation and confidence" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)

- **RQ4 (Challenges and benefits):** Benefits included instant access, comprehensive grammar suggestions, and self-directed learning. Challenges included lack of personalization, generic comments, overwhelming volume, and ineffectiveness for methodological/analytical aspects.
  - Evidence: "The main weakness of feedback given by AI tools is the absence of personalization in the feedback" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)
  - Evidence: "ChatGPT provides a huge amount of feedback which creates a burden among the students" (N/A; source_page-unavailable)

- **ANOVA:** F = 2.40, Sig. = 0.072 — differences among group means were not statistically significant at the 0.05 level.

## Key Claims
1. Students prefer instructor feedback for personalization, depth, and emotional support.
2. ChatGPT excels at surface-level corrections (grammar, syntax) but struggles with higher-order concerns (content, structure, methodology).
3. A hybrid model combining AI and instructor feedback is pedagogically optimal.
4. Overwhelming volume and generic nature of AI feedback are significant learner-reported challenges.

## Discussion
- The preference for instructor feedback aligns with **Bitchener and Knoch (2010)**: effective feedback must be specific to the student's needs.
- ChatGPT's accessibility and immediacy align with **Teng (2024)**, who found EFL students value the neutrality and ease of access of AI feedback.
- The lack of personalization in AI feedback supports concerns raised by **Kostka and Toncelli (2023)** and **Cheng (2024)** regarding generic AI comments.
- Instructor feedback's superior impact on motivation and confidence aligns with **Song and Song (2023)** and **Polakova and Ivenz (2024)**.
- The finding that students trust instructor feedback more for content and organization aligns with Saudi-context studies by **Al-Sofi (2024)** and **Alnasser (2022)**.

## Conclusion
AI tools offer advantages for providing writing feedback, but human feedback remains preferable for most students. Instructor feedback is more individualized, detailed, motivating, and effective for higher-order writing concerns. ChatGPT is more effective for grammar and sentence-level corrections. The study recommends using AI tools to **complement** rather than replace instructor feedback.

## Unique Contributions
- One of the first studies in the Saudi EFL context to directly compare concurrent perceptions of instructor and ChatGPT-4 feedback using a mixed-methods design.
- Employs a dual-scenario design (blended vs. independent AI consultation) to isolate awareness effects.
- Provides empirical evidence for a hybrid feedback model in advanced EFL academic writing instruction.

## Limitations
- Single-institution sample (one public university in Riyadh).
- Advanced learners only (C2 level); findings may not generalize to lower proficiency levels.
- Used only one AI tool (ChatGPT-4), a general-purpose model not optimized for academic writing.
- Short-term study; longitudinal effects unknown.
- Sample size (n=112) is sufficient for descriptive research but limits generalizability.

## Future Work
- Longitudinal studies tracking writing development over time.
- Studies with lower-proficiency learners and different educational settings.
- Comparison of ChatGPT with specialized academic writing tools (Grammarly, Writefull, Elicit).
- Development of adaptive AI feedback systems incorporating learner-specific data and instructor input.
- Tracking AI model evolution across versions.
