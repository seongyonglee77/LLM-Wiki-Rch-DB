## Bibliographic Metadata

Aljasser, Amal. 2025. "Investigating EFL students' perceptions of feedback: a comparative study of instructor and ChatGPT-generated responses in academic writing." Education and Information Technologies 30: 22967-22988. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-025-13649-8.

## One-sentence Summary

This mixed-methods study of 112 advanced Saudi EFL undergraduates finds that ChatGPT feedback was valued for speed, access, clarity, and surface-level writing support, but instructor feedback remained more trusted for personalization, organization, content, motivation, confidence, and context-sensitive academic guidance.

## Keywords

# Structured Summary

## Purpose

The study investigates how Saudi EFL learners perceive instructor feedback compared with ChatGPT-generated feedback in an online advanced academic writing course. Its central problem is not whether AI feedback is useful in isolation, but how students evaluate AI and instructor feedback when both enter the same writing-feedback ecology. The article positions this comparison as a response to a gap in prior AI-feedback studies, which often examined teacher feedback or AI feedback separately rather than asking how learners experience both concurrently.

The four research questions ask about learners' comparative perceptions of instructor and ChatGPT feedback, the relative usefulness of each for academic-writing aspects such as clarity, structure, and coherence, their effects on motivation and confidence, and the benefits and challenges students associate with ChatGPT feedback.

## Background (Theory & Literature Review)

The paper frames writing feedback as central to EFL academic-writing development. It uses Vygotsky's sociocultural theory to explain feedback as scaffolding within the learner's Zone of Proximal Development, then connects this view to constructivist accounts in which learners revise through reflection, interaction, and instructor input. This framing makes human feedback valuable because it can be adaptive, dialogic, and sensitive to individual learner needs.

The literature review contrasts this human-feedback tradition with the affordances of AI feedback. Studies on ChatGPT, Grammarly, Wordtune, and related tools are presented as evidence that automated feedback can be immediate, accessible, objective, consistent, and useful for iterative self-improvement. At the same time, the review emphasizes recurring concerns: AI feedback can be generic, insufficiently personalized, unreliable, ethically risky, and less effective for deep content, structure, methodology, and rhetorical judgment.

The Saudi and Middle Eastern EFL literature narrows the gap. Prior Saudi studies are summarized as generally positive about AI support for grammar, organization, and preliminary revision, while also reporting student preference for instructors when assignments require critical thinking, complex analysis, and contextual depth. Aljasser's contribution is to place instructor and ChatGPT feedback side by side in a real advanced-writing course, with students experiencing both hidden AI-assisted feedback and overt self-directed ChatGPT consultation.

## Method

The study used a mixed-methods design in an online advanced academic writing course at a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The participants were 112 senior students in an English Language and Translation program. The article describes them as final-year undergraduates and C2-level English learners under the CEFR, making them suitable participants for evaluating feedback on academic writing, research-paper structure, and complex linguistic choices.

The course required students to produce a research paper through four staged assignments: introduction, literature review, methodology, and results/discussion. In Scenario 1, for Assignments 1 and 2, the instructor provided feedback based on her own evaluation plus ChatGPT input, but students were not told ChatGPT had been used; ChatGPT input was labelled as overall comments. In Scenario 2, for Assignments 3 and 4, students were explicitly instructed to consult ChatGPT before submission; after that, the instructor provided her own feedback without consulting ChatGPT.

Survey A followed Scenario 1, and Survey B followed Scenario 2. Both surveys used a four-point Likert scale from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree and included open-ended questions. Quantitative analysis used descriptive statistics and ANOVA to compare response patterns across the four assignment points. Qualitative analysis used thematic analysis of open-ended responses, including familiarization, initial coding, theme searching, theme review, theme naming, and reporting.

## Findings

Quantitatively, students generally agreed that ChatGPT feedback was clear, relevant, useful, timely, accessible, and fair. Mean scores were low on the four-point scale, where lower values reflected stronger agreement. Students rated relevance especially strongly in early tasks, with a mean of 1.03 for R1-A1 and 1.14 for R2-A2. ChatGPT's clarity and usefulness were also rated positively, though some ratings became weaker in later assignments. Personalization, anxiety reduction, and self-confidence were comparatively weaker areas, with means around 1.50-1.93 for personalization and 1.65-2.00 for anxiety-related items.

The overall ANOVA comparison across R1-A1, R2-A2, R3-A3, and R4-A4 reported F = 2.40 and p = 0.072, so the total mean differences across the four response groups were not statistically significant at the 0.05 level. However, item-level results showed significant differences for several specific perceptions, including whether ChatGPT clearly identified improvement areas, whether its comments were relevant to the assignment, whether feedback was fair, and whether instructor or ChatGPT feedback was most helpful.

Qualitatively, students valued the feedback in Scenario 1 because they believed it came from the instructor and read it as specific, actionable, motivating, and responsive to their writing. This hidden-AI condition is important: when students did not know AI was involved, they attributed the usefulness of the combined feedback to the teacher's personal understanding. Students especially appreciated feedback on sentence structure, tense, research questions, simplification, transitions, readability, and organization.

In Scenario 2, students appreciated using ChatGPT independently because it made feedback immediate and gave them a sense of self-directed learning. They could compare ChatGPT comments with instructor comments, and many saw value in having multiple feedback sources. Yet the same open responses also showed preference for instructor feedback. Students trusted instructor comments more for content, organization, methodology, analysis, and task-specific academic judgment. ChatGPT was described as helpful for grammar, sentence organization, and coherence, but less helpful for explaining specific academic mistakes or handling methodological and analytical aspects of research writing.

## Discussion & Conclusion

The discussion argues for a complementary rather than replacement model. Instructor feedback is interpreted as more individualized, comprehensive, motivational, and contextually aware. ChatGPT feedback is interpreted as useful for speed, access, standardization, and practical surface-level revisions. The study therefore supports hybrid feedback designs in which AI tools supply fast preliminary assistance while instructors remain responsible for personalization, scaffolding, emotional support, and deeper academic-writing guidance.

The conclusion states that AI feedback has clear benefits for academic writing, especially grammar and sentence-level organization, but that most students still prefer human feedback because it is more individualized and detailed. Pedagogically, the study recommends integrating AI feedback with teacher feedback and cautioning against indiscriminate AI feedback for all students or all writing aspects, since large volumes of generalized AI comments may demotivate or worry some learners.

## Purpose

Aljasser's study addresses a precise problem in AI-mediated EFL writing pedagogy: many studies had evaluated AI feedback or instructor feedback separately, but students increasingly encounter both in the same learning environment. The study asks how advanced Saudi EFL learners perceive feedback from ChatGPT compared with feedback from their instructor, and how those perceptions vary by feedback source, visibility of AI involvement, writing task, and affective response.

The article's logic is comparative and ecological. It does not treat ChatGPT as a standalone writing tutor. Instead, it examines feedback as part of a course workflow in which learners receive comments on successive stages of a research paper. This matters because academic writing feedback involves more than grammar correction: students need help with research purpose, literature review, methodology, results, discussion, organization, coherence, and confidence as writers.

The study's practical aim is to determine where ChatGPT can complement instructors and where teacher feedback remains necessary. This makes the paper useful for designing AI-supported writing instruction, especially in EFL settings where students may need frequent feedback but where instructors cannot always provide immediate responses.

## Theory & Literature Review

The literature review begins from the premise that writing is central to language learning and that feedback is necessary for improvement. It anchors the role of feedback in sociocultural theory, especially Vygotsky's concept of scaffolding in the Zone of Proximal Development. In that framing, feedback helps learners perform beyond what they could do independently, and instructor support matters because it can be calibrated to the learner's developmental level.

The review also invokes constructivist learning theory through Ferris and Bitchener. Feedback is not simply correction delivered to passive students; it is part of an interactive revision process in which learners reflect, problem-solve, and build academic-writing knowledge. The review then connects this to older L2 writing-feedback debates over direct and indirect corrective feedback. Direct feedback gives students the correct form, which may suit lower-proficiency learners. Indirect feedback marks problems without supplying the correction, encouraging learners to solve the problem themselves. This distinction prepares the later argument that effective feedback depends on learner needs, proficiency, task complexity, and the kind of writing problem being addressed.

Personalization is a recurring theoretical theme. The review presents personalized feedback as specific, learner-centered information that supports motivation and performance. Instructors are implicitly associated with this kind of feedback because they know students' histories, learning profiles, assignment goals, and classroom context. AI systems, by contrast, may offer fast and repeatable responses but often lack learner-specific data, contextual awareness, and adaptive pedagogical judgment.

The AI-feedback literature is balanced rather than dismissive. Studies such as AlGhamdi (2024), Pitychoutis (2024), Teng (2024), Guo et al. (2024), and Song and Song (2023) are used to show that ChatGPT and other AI tools can promote self-improvement, support grammar and style, supply immediate comments, and increase confidence around surface-level writing revisions. Studies such as Kostka and Toncelli (2023), Ho (2024), Lundberg (2024), and Polakova and Ivenz (2024) are used to mark the boundaries of that benefit: generic comments, limited personalization, weak support for deeper content and structure, and possible over-reliance.

The Saudi and Middle Eastern review sharpens this contrast. Wordtune and ChatGPT studies in Saudi EFL contexts are reported as showing benefits for writing performance, organization, grammar, feedback improvement, and personalized support. However, the review repeatedly notes that students prefer instructors for assignments involving critical thinking, complex analysis, rhetorical skill, and contextual depth. In this way, the literature review builds a clear expectation: ChatGPT may help with speed and surface-level revisions, but instructor feedback should remain stronger for complex academic-writing decisions.

## Gaps & Research Questions

The gap is that prior studies often examined AI-generated feedback and instructor-generated feedback separately. Aljasser argues that this misses the lived instructional situation in which learners may receive, compare, or combine both. The study therefore asks four questions:

1. How do EFL students perceive instructor feedback compared with ChatGPT feedback for improving academic writing?
2. How effective are instructor and AI feedback for specific aspects of academic writing such as clarity, structure, and coherence?
3. How do instructor and AI feedback affect motivation and confidence?
4. What benefits and challenges do students associate with using ChatGPT for academic feedback?

## Theoretical Framework

The paper's framework combines sociocultural scaffolding, constructivist revision, written corrective feedback, personalized feedback, and AI-mediated learner autonomy. Sociocultural theory explains why feedback should be responsive and developmental. Constructivism explains why feedback should provoke revision and reflection. Corrective-feedback theory distinguishes different types of intervention. Personalized feedback theory explains why students may value comments that seem to address their own writing, emotional needs, and learning history. AI-feedback research adds the affordances of immediacy, access, consistency, and self-directed revision.

The implicit theoretical claim is that effective AI integration depends on matching feedback source to feedback function. ChatGPT is better aligned with quick, repeatable, surface-oriented assistance; instructors are better aligned with individualized scaffolding, task-specific academic judgment, motivation, and higher-order content support.

## Methodology

### Context

The study took place in an online advanced academic writing course in an English Language and Translation program at a public university in Riyadh. The course used Microsoft Teams for synchronous classes, assignment submission, discussion, and individualized feedback. Students wrote a research paper in four staged assignments: introduction, literature review, methodology, and results/discussion. The staged structure allowed feedback to be studied across different academic-writing components rather than only at the final-paper stage.

### Participants

The sample consisted of 112 senior Saudi EFL students. They were final-year undergraduates and were described as C2-level learners according to the CEFR. The article justifies this advanced sample by arguing that students with high English proficiency can evaluate sophisticated feedback on complex linguistic structures, research-paper organization, and academic content.

### Data Sources

The main data sources were Survey A, Survey B, and open-ended survey responses. Survey A was administered after Assignments 1 and 2 in Scenario 1, where students received combined instructor and ChatGPT-supported feedback but were unaware of ChatGPT's role. Survey B was administered after Assignments 3 and 4 in Scenario 2, where students independently consulted ChatGPT before instructor feedback. The appendices show that the surveys included Likert items on clarity, usefulness, relevance, timeliness, accessibility, personalization, anxiety, self-confidence, fairness, and comparative preference, plus open-ended prompts asking students to explain helpful and less helpful feedback.

### Procedure

Scenario 1 created a partially blinded feedback condition. Students submitted the introduction and literature review to the instructor. The instructor provided feedback based on her own evaluation and ChatGPT input, but ChatGPT's involvement was not disclosed. ChatGPT-generated feedback was labelled as overall comments, while the instructor's actual feedback appeared as in-text comments.

Scenario 2 made ChatGPT use explicit and student-directed. Students consulted ChatGPT on methodology and results/discussion assignments before submitting their work. The instructor then provided feedback without using ChatGPT. This design allowed the study to compare student responses when AI involvement was hidden versus when students actively used ChatGPT and compared it with instructor comments.

### Analysis Methods

Quantitative Likert-scale data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and inferential comparison. The article reports mean values, standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, F values, and significance values. It specifically states that ANOVA was used to answer the first research question by testing whether perceived feedback effectiveness differed across groups.

Qualitative open-ended responses were analyzed thematically. The article describes a six-phase process: familiarization, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. The thematic analysis was used to interpret students' perceptions of clarity, usefulness, emotional impact, preferences, and comparative trust in instructor versus ChatGPT feedback.

## Findings

### Survey Results

The survey results show broad student approval of ChatGPT feedback, especially for clarity, relevance, usefulness, timeliness, accessibility, and fairness. On the four-point scale, low means indicated stronger agreement. Students rated ChatGPT relevance very positively in early tasks, with a mean of 1.03 for R1-A1 and 1.14 for R2-A2. They also rated usefulness positively, with means between 1.21 and 1.44, and timeliness positively, with means between 1.14 and 1.31. Ease of access and review was similarly strong, with means around 1.18 to 1.28.

However, the results weaken around personalization and affective support. ChatGPT feedback felt less personalized, with means from 1.50 to 1.93. Anxiety reduction was moderate, with means from 1.65 to 2.00. Self-confidence and self-esteem effects were also moderate rather than strong, with means from 1.64 to 1.82. These results support a nuanced interpretation: students did not reject ChatGPT, but they saw its strongest value in access, clarity, and practicality rather than emotional or individualized support.

The comparative preference items complicate a simple pro-AI conclusion. Students found in-text instructor comments especially helpful in later assignments, with means of 1.28, 1.17, and 1.28 for R2-A2, R3-A3, and R4-A4. In contrast, ratings for ChatGPT as the most helpful source were weaker in later tasks, with means of 2.44 for R3-A3 and 2.53 for R4-A4. This is important because later assignments concerned methodology and results/discussion, where higher-order academic judgment was more salient.

The total-response comparison across R1-A1, R2-A2, R3-A3, and R4-A4 reported means of 1.48, 1.34, 1.59, and 1.64, respectively. The reported ANOVA result was F = 2.40, p = 0.072, meaning the overall difference among group means did not reach the 0.05 significance threshold. Therefore, the safest interpretation is not that one feedback mode statistically dominated overall, but that student perceptions varied by item, task, and feedback function.

### Qualitative Findings

The qualitative findings show that students valued specificity and actionability. In Scenario 1, when students believed all feedback was teacher-generated, they appreciated comments that identified problems in sentence structure, tense, research questions, transitions, readability, and organization. The feedback's motivational value was linked to its combination of positive reinforcement and constructive criticism. Because students did not know AI was involved, they attributed the feedback's usefulness to the instructor's understanding of their needs.

Students also identified weaknesses in the hidden combined feedback. Some comments lacked examples or explanations, and advice such as improving transitions was perceived as too broad when not accompanied by concrete models. Students wanted more detailed feedback on research significance and delimitations. This matters because even feedback mediated by a teacher and AI can fail when comments remain general or insufficiently exemplified.

In Scenario 2, students liked the speed and breadth of ChatGPT feedback and found value in comparing AI comments with instructor comments. Independent ChatGPT use helped some learners feel more self-directed. ChatGPT was useful for grammar, sentence organization, and coherence, and it gave students another source of revision ideas before receiving the instructor's comments.

Yet the qualitative data clearly favored instructor feedback for trust and academic judgment. Students considered instructor feedback more accurate for content and organization. ChatGPT comments were sometimes too numerous, making it difficult to decide which revisions mattered. Students also found ChatGPT less effective for methodology, data analysis, and explaining specific academic mistakes. The qualitative findings therefore align with the theoretical distinction in the literature review: AI feedback is efficient and helpful, but teacher feedback remains stronger for personalization, prioritization, and complex academic-writing decisions.

## Key Claims

The paper's central claim is that ChatGPT should complement, not replace, instructor feedback in EFL academic writing. This claim is supported by the combination of positive ChatGPT ratings and persistent instructor preference for more personalized, detailed, and confidence-building feedback.

A second claim is that feedback usefulness depends on writing level and task type. ChatGPT is most useful for grammar, sentence organization, coherence, accessibility, and preliminary revision. Instructor feedback is more useful for general organization, clarity, content, methodology, analysis, emotional support, and matching comments to a student's learning profile.

A third claim is affective. Students did not experience ChatGPT as equally supportive in reducing anxiety or building confidence. Instructor feedback, especially when it included positive and negative comments, was more strongly associated with feeling supported, motivated, and confident.

A fourth claim concerns feedback load. AI feedback may be fast and extensive, but extensive feedback can become overwhelming if it does not prioritize what students should revise first. This is especially relevant for advanced academic-writing tasks with multiple possible revision targets.

## Discussion

The discussion is organized around the four research questions. For RQ1, the article argues that students generally perceived instructor feedback as more individualized and detailed than AI feedback. The instructor's advantage is linked to comprehensiveness and knowledge of each student's learning profile. ChatGPT's advantage is speed, access, and neutrality, but its main weakness is lack of personalization.

For RQ2, the discussion differentiates writing aspects. Instructor feedback was most effective for organization and clarity because it could address the overall structure and task demands of the student's paper. ChatGPT was more useful for grammar, sentence organization, and specific practical suggestions. The paper's interpretation is not that ChatGPT is weak across the board; rather, it is functionally limited when the writing problem requires content-level, methodological, analytical, or rhetorical judgment.

For RQ3, instructor feedback is interpreted as more powerful for motivation and confidence. Students felt more supported, confident, and less anxious when the instructor provided both positive and corrective comments. ChatGPT's speed was appreciated, but it did not offer the same affective reassurance for significant assignments.

For RQ4, the benefits of ChatGPT are immediacy, accessibility, standardization, and practical revision suggestions. The challenges are generic feedback, limited personalization, weak suitability for academic context, difficulty with methodology and data analysis, and feedback overload. The discussion attributes these problems to algorithmic limitations, limited contextual awareness, lack of learner-specific data integration, and constrained adaptability to writing goals.

## Conclusion

The conclusion reiterates that students saw value in AI feedback but still preferred human feedback for individualized, detailed, confidence-building guidance. AI feedback was strongest for grammar and sentence organization; instructor feedback was strongest for organization, clarity, motivation, self-confidence, and alignment with student learning profiles. The paper concludes that AI should be used as a supplement to instructor feedback, not as a replacement.

## Unique Contributions

The study's first contribution is its comparison of hidden AI-assisted feedback and explicit student-directed ChatGPT feedback within the same writing course. This design captures two realistic AI-integration scenarios: instructor-mediated AI use and learner-initiated AI consultation.

The second contribution is its focus on advanced Saudi EFL learners writing staged research-paper assignments. Because the assignments move from introduction and literature review to methodology and results/discussion, the study can show how ChatGPT's usefulness changes as the writing task becomes more analytically demanding.

The third contribution is its affective dimension. The study does not only ask whether feedback improves writing; it asks how feedback affects motivation, confidence, anxiety, trust, and perceived personalization.

## Limitations

The sample was restricted to senior EFL students at one public university in Riyadh, so the findings may not generalize to other institutions, regions, age groups, or proficiency levels. The participants were advanced C2-level learners; lower-proficiency learners might need different kinds of correction, scaffolding, examples, and teacher mediation.

The study used ChatGPT-4 only. Since LLMs evolve quickly, the results may not transfer directly to later model versions or to specialized academic-writing tools such as Grammarly, Writefull, or Elicit. The design also measured perceptions rather than directly measuring writing improvement through pre/post writing scores or independent ratings of assignment quality.

There are reporting limitations in the extracted source. The paper gives useful means, standard deviations, and ANOVA values, but the relationship between task labels, feedback-source visibility, and item-level significance is not always easy to interpret from the tables. Because this card was generated from a parsed Markdown file and page-checked against the PDF, the substantive summary is reliable, but full methodological appraisal still requires human review.

## Future Work

The article recommends research across different educational settings and proficiency levels. It also suggests longitudinal designs to examine how combined AI and instructor feedback affects writing development over time. Future studies could compare ChatGPT with specialized academic-writing tools and newer LLM versions. Another useful direction would be to test adaptive AI feedback systems that integrate learner profiles, assignment rubrics, and instructor oversight so AI feedback becomes more targeted without displacing human pedagogical judgment.
