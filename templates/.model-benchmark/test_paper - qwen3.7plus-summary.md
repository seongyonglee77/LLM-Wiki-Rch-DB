# Quick Card

## Bibliographic Metadata

Aljasser, Amal. 2025. "Investigating EFL students' perceptions of feedback: a comparative study of instructor and ChatGPT-generated responses in academic writing." *Education and Information Technologies*. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-025-13649-8.

## One-sentence Summary

In a mixed-methods investigation of 112 advanced Saudi EFL undergraduates completing a four-stage academic writing course, students consistently preferred instructor feedback for personalization, content-level depth, methodology guidance, and motivational reassurance, while valuing ChatGPT-4 feedback for its immediacy, accessibility, and sentence-level grammatical support — leading the study to advocate a complementary hybrid feedback model (pp. 1, 8–13).

## Keywords

ChatGPT feedback; instructor feedback; EFL academic writing; Saudi higher education; AI-generated feedback; mixed methods; learner perceptions; written corrective feedback; feedback literacy.

# Structured Summary

## Purpose

The study addresses a specific gap: prior research examined AI-generated feedback and instructor feedback each in isolation, leaving unclear how EFL students perceive and reconcile both sources concurrently within the same academic writing curriculum (p. 2). The study compares instructor and ChatGPT-4 feedback across four sequential assignments to evaluate their relative effectiveness on writing performance, clarity, structure, motivation, confidence, and learner preferences.

## Background (Theory & Literature Review)

- The theoretical foundation rests on **Vygotsky's (1978) socio-cultural theory** (Zone of Proximal Development as scaffolding) and **constructivist learning theory** (feedback as an interactive, reflective revision process) (p. 2).
- L2 writing research distinguishes **direct corrective feedback** (preferred for low-proficiency learners) from **indirect feedback** (which engages learners in problem-solving and is preferred for advanced learners) (p. 3).
- The literature on AI feedback highlights **affordances**: immediacy, accessibility, objectivity, and support for iterative revision (Guo et al., 2024; Teng, 2024) — and **limitations**: lack of personalization, generic commentary, weak engagement with content and structure (Kostka & Toncelli, 2023; Lundberg, 2024; Polakova & Ivenz, 2024).
- Saudi and Middle Eastern EFL studies (AlGhamdi, 2024; Alshammri, 2024; Al-Sofi, 2024; Alnasser, 2022; Alotaibi, 2023) converge on a pattern: learners welcome AI tools for grammar and preliminary revision, but prefer instructor feedback for critical thinking, complex analysis, and rhetorical sophistication (pp. 3–4).

## Method

- **Design:** Mixed-methods combining 4-point Likert-scale surveys (eliminating neutral midpoint) with thematic analysis of open-ended responses.
- **Participants:** 112 senior EFL students (CEFR C2) in an English Language and Translation program at a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (p. 5).
- **Setting & Tasks:** Online advanced academic writing course delivered via Microsoft Teams across two semesters. Students completed four sequenced assignments: introduction (A1), literature review (A2), methodology (A3), and results/discussion (A4) (p. 5).
- **Procedure (Two Scenarios):**
  - *Scenario 1 (A1 & A2):* Instructor provided blended feedback (her own in-text comments + ChatGPT-generated overall comments). Students were **unaware** of ChatGPT's involvement (pp. 5–6).
  - *Scenario 2 (A3 & A4):* Students were **explicitly required** to consult ChatGPT-4 independently before submission. The instructor then provided independent feedback (p. 6).
- **Instruments:** Survey A (post-Scenario 1) and Survey B (post-Scenario 2), each with 10 Likert items and open-ended prompts (pp. 6, 14–15).
- **Analysis:** Descriptive statistics and ANOVA for quantitative data; six-phase thematic analysis for qualitative data (p. 7).

## Findings

- **Perceived quality of ChatGPT feedback (RQ1):** Strong agreement on clarity and relevance (mean 1.03–1.64); weaker agreement on personalization (mean 1.50–1.93) and anxiety reduction (mean 1.65–2.00) (pp. 8–9).
- **Relative effectiveness (RQ2):** ChatGPT excelled at grammar, sentence organization, and basic coherence. Instructor feedback was rated superior for global organization, content development, methodology, and analytical judgment (pp. 10–12).
- **Motivation & confidence (RQ3):** Instructor feedback exerted a stronger positive effect on motivation and confidence, and more effectively reduced anxiety. AI feedback was valued for speed but viewed as less encouraging (p. 12).
- **Benefits & challenges (RQ4):** Benefits: instant, accessible, standardized. Challenges: generic output, inability to address methodology/data analysis, overwhelming volume of suggestions that burdened revision prioritization (pp. 10–12).
- **ANOVA (Table 2):** Group means ranged from 1.34 (R2-A2) to 1.64 (R4-A4); *F* = 2.40, *p* = 0.072 (no statistically significant difference at α = 0.05) (p. 8).

## Discussion & Conclusion

The study concludes that while ChatGPT offers meaningful logistical and mechanical benefits, **human feedback remains indispensable** for advanced academic writing. Instructor feedback provides individualized, developmentally attuned, contextually situated, and affectively supportive scaffolding that AI cannot replicate. The study recommends a **hybrid feedback model**: AI tools should serve as a first-pass revision support for surface-level mechanics, while instructor capacity is reserved for higher-order writing concerns, methodological reasoning, and motivational support (pp. 11–14).

# Deep Summary

## Purpose

The study's core research problem is not simply whether ChatGPT can provide useful feedback, but whether and how EFL learners perceive the **combined presence** of instructor and AI feedback within a single authentic writing curriculum (pp. 1–2). The study explicitly positions ChatGPT not as a replacement but as a complementary feedback source whose perceived usefulness is weighed against instructor feedback.

## Theory & Literature Review

The literature review builds on three interconnected layers:

1. **Sociocultural & Constructivist Foundations:** Feedback is framed as scaffolding in Vygotsky's ZPD — the distance between independent problem-solving and guided performance (p. 86 / paper p. 2). Constructivist theory positions feedback as an interactive revision cycle (Ferris, 2012; Bitchener, 2008; p. 2).
2. **Corrective Feedback in L2 Writing:** The review distinguishes direct feedback (supplying the correct form — useful for low-proficiency learners) from indirect feedback (signaling errors without correcting — engaging for advanced learners) (Eslami, 2014; Ferris & Roberts, 2001; p. 3). **Personalized feedback** is cited as "specific, student-centered information delivered to students about their performance in a motivationally optimal manner" (Koenka & Anderman, 2019, p. 15; paper p. 3).
3. **AI Feedback — Promising but Incomplete:** ChatGPT and similar tools offer rapid, accessible feedback for iterative revision (Guo et al., 2024; Teng, 2024; pp. 2–3), but persistent concerns include generic output, weak engagement with content, and risks of overreliance (Kostka & Toncelli, 2023; Lundberg, 2024; Polakova & Ivenz, 2024; pp. 3–4). Saudi-context studies report that students value AI for grammar but rely on instructors for critical thinking and rhetorical sophistication (pp. 3–4).

## Gaps & Research Questions

**Research gap:** Prior international and Saudi studies examined AI and instructor feedback in isolation, leaving the concurrent impact of both on motivation, engagement, and writing performance underexplored (p. 2).

- **RQ1:** How do EFL students perceive instructor feedback compared to ChatGPT feedback for improving academic writing?
- **RQ2:** What is the relative effectiveness of instructor vs. AI feedback for clarity, structure, and coherence?
- **RQ3:** How do different feedback types affect motivation and confidence?
- **RQ4:** What are the key benefits and challenges of ChatGPT for academic feedback? (p. 4)

## Theoretical Framework

Three guiding assumptions:
1. Feedback is scaffolded assistance calibrated to the learner's ZPD (p. 2).
2. Feedback is a constructive, revision-oriented, dialogic process (p. 2).
3. Effective feedback must be personalized to fit learner needs (p. 3).

## Methodology

### Context

Online advanced academic writing course at a public university in Riyadh, delivered via Microsoft Teams. Students wrote a four-part research paper (introduction, literature review, methodology, results/discussion) over two semesters, with two-week cycles at each stage (pp. 5–6).

### Participants

112 senior EFL students in an English Language and Translation program; CEFR C2-level (near-native proficiency). The advanced-proficiency sample enables meaningful critique of sophisticated feedback but limits generalizability to lower levels (p. 5).

### Data Sources

- Four sequential Word document assignments graded via standardized rubrics (Appendices A–D).
- **Survey A** (post-Scenario 1) and **Survey B** (post-Scenario 2): 10 Likert items (clarity, relevance, usefulness, timeliness, accessibility, personalization, anxiety reduction, self-confidence, fairness) + 2 comparative items + open-ended prompts. Forced-choice 4-point scale (1 = Strongly Agree, 4 = Strongly Disagree) to eliminate neutral midpoint bias (pp. 6, 14–15).

### Procedure

- **Scenario 1 (Blended/Concealed — A1 & A2):** Instructor provided her own in-text comments plus ChatGPT-generated overall comments. Students were **blind** to AI involvement (pp. 5–6).
- **Scenario 2 (Explicit Independent — A3 & A4):** Students consulted ChatGPT-4 independently before submission. Instructor then provided independent feedback without AI assistance (p. 6).
- The same instructor taught all cohorts to ensure consistency (p. 6).

### Analysis Methods

- **Quantitative:** Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals) and one-way ANOVA across four task rounds (pp. 7–8).
- **Qualitative:** Six-phase thematic analysis (familiarization → initial coding → theme search → theme review → theme definition/naming → reporting), following Vaismoradi et al. (2013), Guest et al. (2012), Bernard & Ryan (2010) (p. 7).

## Findings

### RQ1: Perceived Quality of ChatGPT Feedback

Students generally agreed that ChatGPT feedback was clear, relevant, useful, timely, accessible, and fair. Strongest agreement was on relevance (R1-A1 mean = 1.03, R2-A2 = 1.14) and clarity (R3-A3 = 1.58, R4-A4 = 1.64). However, personalization ratings were weaker (mean 1.50–1.93) and anxiety reduction only moderate (mean 1.65–2.00) (pp. 8–9). Instructor in-text comments were rated more helpful than ChatGPT feedback, especially in later tasks (pp. 8–10).

### RQ2: Relative Effectiveness by Writing Dimension

ChatGPT was effective for grammar, sentence organization, and basic coherence. Instructor feedback was superior for global organization, clarity, content development, methodology, and analytical judgment. Qualitative data show students found ChatGPT's guidance "quite general" while instructor feedback was "more specific and task-oriented" (pp. 10–12).

### RQ3: Motivation and Confidence

Instructor feedback had a greater positive effect on motivation, confidence, and anxiety reduction. Students felt more supported, more confident, and less anxious with instructor feedback, which provided both positive and negative feedback in a caring manner (p. 12). ChatGPT was appreciated for speed but considered less encouraging.

### RQ4: Benefits and Challenges of ChatGPT Feedback

**Benefits:** Instant turnaround, 24/7 accessibility, standardized suggestions, encouragement of autonomous revision.

**Challenges:** Lack of personalization, generic commentary, inability to address methodology and data analysis, overwhelming volume of suggestions that burdened prioritization. Students reported ChatGPT provided "a huge amount of feedback which creates a burden" (p. 12).

## Key Claims

1. **Complementary role:** ChatGPT is effective for immediacy, grammar, sentence organization, and preliminary revision — but not as a standalone replacement for instructor feedback (pp. 1, 13).
2. **Pedagogical depth & personalization:** Instructor feedback remains essential for content, organization, methodology, analysis, and motivational support (pp. 11–13).
3. **Affective scaffolding:** Motivation, confidence, and anxiety reduction in high-stakes writing depend fundamentally on perceived authenticity and empathy of human feedback (p. 12).
4. **Cognitive burden of AI overload:** Unfiltered AI feedback can overwhelm learners; instructor mediation is necessary to guide prioritization (pp. 10–12).
5. **Hybrid feedback model:** AI should complement, not replace, instructor feedback — AI for mechanical polishing, instructor for higher-order writing (pp. 1, 13).

## Discussion

The discussion interprets instructor feedback as more individualized and developmentally attuned, while ChatGPT feedback is valued for speed, accessibility, and neutrality (p. 11). The study maps feedback sources onto different writing dimensions: ChatGPT aligns with grammar, sentence organization, and basic coherence; the instructor aligns with organization, clarity, content, methodology, and analysis (pp. 10–12). The affective dimension is central: instructor feedback is linked to motivation, support, and confidence; ChatGPT is less encouraging despite its speed (p. 12). The study attributes AI's personalization gap to algorithmic limitations, limited contextual awareness, and constrained adaptability (p. 12), and proposes hybrid systems embedding instructor input (p. 12).

## Conclusion

AI tools offer clear advantages for writing instruction — speed, accessibility, and surface-level mechanical correction — but human feedback remains preferable for most students because it is more individualized, detailed, motivationally supportive, and aligned with higher-order academic writing needs (p. 13). ChatGPT is most defensible as a complementary support, not a substitute, for instructor feedback in advanced EFL academic writing.

## Unique Contributions

1. **Empirical direct comparison** in the Saudi EFL context, comparing instructor and ChatGPT-4 feedback within the same cohort and curriculum across multiple authentic assignment cycles (pp. 4–5).
2. **Dual-scenario design** separating concealed AI-assisted feedback (Scenario 1) from explicit independent AI consultation (Scenario 2), isolating awareness effects from tool efficacy (pp. 5–6).
3. **Affective & cognitive load insights:** identification of "AI feedback overload" as an emerging cognitive challenge, alongside empirical documentation of AI's limitations in alleviating writing anxiety (pp. 10–12).
4. **Pedagogical differentiation:** identification of which feedback functions students assign to AI (mechanical, speed, accessibility) and which they reserve for instructors (content, methodology, affect) — making the study actionable for feedback workflow design.

## Limitations

- **Sample specificity:** Single public university in Riyadh; senior advanced (C2) translation students only; limited generalizability to lower-proficiency learners, other institutions, or different national contexts (p. 13).
- **Single AI model:** Only ChatGPT-4 was evaluated, excluding specialized academic writing tools (Grammarly, Writefull, Elicit, Scite) and subsequent LLM versions (p. 13).
- **Rapid LLM evolution:** Findings from one model version may age quickly; the study's short-term design limits conclusions about evolving AI capabilities (p. 13).
- **Instructor confound:** The same instructor both taught and provided teacher feedback, making instructor identity and feedback source partly inseparable (p. 6).
- **Qualitative transparency:** Thematic analysis phases are described, but the paper does not provide intercoder reliability metrics, coding codebooks, or detailed examples of coding decisions (p. 7).

## Future Work

- **Longitudinal studies** tracking writing development and feedback literacy over time (pp. 13–14).
- **Comparative AI tool benchmarking** testing general-purpose LLMs against specialized academic writing platforms (p. 13).
- **Cross-context studies** across different educational settings, proficiency levels, and national contexts (p. 13).
- **Adaptive hybrid interface design** integrating instructor rubrics to filter and organize AI suggestions before student review (p. 12).
- **Objective writing-performance measures** alongside perceptual survey data (pp. 13–14).
- **Cross-version AI comparisons** to capture the evolution of AI feedback effectiveness over time (p. 13).

## Relevance to My Study

This paper is relevant for research on AI in language education, EFL writing pedagogy, feedback literacy, teacher agency, and human-AI instructional design. Its most useful insight is that student acceptance of AI feedback is **conditional**: learners welcome ChatGPT for speed and initial revision but still depend on teachers for interpretation, prioritization, academic judgment, and emotional assurance. For teacher-agency research, it supports the argument that AI redistributes but does not eliminate the teacher's mediating role. For learner autonomy, it shows that independent ChatGPT use can support self-directed revision only when students can judge which suggestions are relevant and how they compare with instructor feedback.
