# Quick Card

## Bibliographic Metadata

Aljasser, Amal. 2025. "Investigating EFL students' perceptions of feedback: a comparative study of instructor and ChatGPT-generated responses in academic writing." Education and Information Technologies. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-025-13649-8.

## One-sentence Summary

This mixed-methods study of 112 advanced Saudi EFL undergraduates finds that students valued ChatGPT feedback for speed, accessibility, and lower-level language support, but still preferred instructor feedback for personalization, task specificity, motivation, and higher-level academic-writing judgment (pp. 1, 8, 10-13).

## Keywords

ChatGPT feedback; instructor feedback; EFL academic writing; Saudi higher education; AI-generated feedback; mixed methods; learner perceptions; writing pedagogy.

# Structured Summary

## Purpose

The paper addresses a specific gap in AI-writing-feedback research: prior studies had often assessed AI feedback or instructor feedback separately, while this study compares students' perceptions of both sources within the same advanced EFL writing course. The introduction states that previous work had reported the two feedback types "in isolation" and that the current study examines how learners perceive the combination of instructor and AI feedback for writing performance, motivation, confidence, and feedback preference (p. 2). The empirical aim is therefore comparative rather than purely evaluative: ChatGPT is not tested as a stand-alone replacement for the instructor, but as a feedback source whose perceived usefulness is weighed against, and potentially integrated with, teacher feedback.

## Background (Theory & Literature Review)

The literature review frames feedback as central to academic writing development for EFL learners because academic writing requires command of linguistic, rhetorical, and organizational conventions. The author links this premise to Vygotskian sociocultural theory: feedback is treated as scaffolding within the Zone of Proximal Development, where learners can move beyond what they can do independently when guided by a more capable other (p. 2). The review also draws on constructivist accounts of feedback as an interactive process of revision, reflection, and knowledge-building rather than a one-way correction mechanism (p. 2).

Within L2 writing scholarship, the review distinguishes direct and indirect corrective feedback. Direct feedback supplies the corrected form and is described as more suitable for lower-proficiency learners; indirect feedback signals a problem and leaves learners to solve it, which may promote engagement and problem-solving for many students (p. 3). This matters because the study's participants are advanced C2-level learners, making them capable of evaluating and using higher-order feedback on structure, content, methodology, and argument, not just sentence-level correction (p. 5).

The AI-feedback literature is presented as ambivalent. ChatGPT, Grammarly, and related tools can provide immediate, accessible, objective, and repeatable feedback, enabling students to revise repeatedly and autonomously (pp. 2-3). But the same review foregrounds the key limitation that AI feedback may lack learner-specific adaptation, detailed personalization, and deeper engagement with content and structure (p. 3). The author then narrows the discussion to Saudi and Middle Eastern EFL contexts, where prior studies suggest AI tools can support grammar, organization, preliminary revision, and content organization, while teacher feedback remains preferred for assignments involving critical thinking, complex analysis, and contextual depth (p. 4).

## Method

The study used a mixed-methods design combining quantitative Likert-scale survey data with qualitative open-ended responses. The participants were 112 senior EFL students in an English Language and Translation program at a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia; the paper describes them as final-year undergraduates and C2-level English learners under the CEFR (p. 5). The course was an online advanced academic writing course in which students wrote a research paper through four assignments: introduction, literature review, methodology, and results/discussion (p. 5).

The procedure compared two scenarios. In Scenario 1, students submitted Assignments 1 and 2 to the instructor. The instructor used both her own evaluation and ChatGPT feedback, but students did not know ChatGPT had been consulted; ChatGPT-generated comments were labelled as overall comments, while instructor feedback appeared as in-text comments (pp. 5-6). In Scenario 2, students were explicitly asked to consult ChatGPT themselves for Assignments 3 and 4 before submitting to the instructor, who then provided her own feedback without consulting ChatGPT (p. 6). This design allowed the author to examine both concealed AI-assisted feedback and explicit learner-facing ChatGPT feedback.

Surveys A and B followed the two scenarios. Both used a forced-choice 4-point Likert scale from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree and included open-ended questions about helpfulness, weaknesses, and feedback preference (p. 6). Quantitative responses were analyzed with descriptive and inferential statistics; qualitative responses were coded thematically using a six-phase thematic-analysis procedure including familiarization, coding, theme searching, theme reviewing, theme naming, and report production (p. 7).

## Findings

Quantitatively, students generally agreed that ChatGPT feedback was clear, relevant, useful, timely, accessible, and fair, but agreement was weaker on personalization and emotional support. In Table 1, relevance of ChatGPT feedback received especially strong agreement in early tasks, with mean values of 1.03 and 1.14 for R1-A1 and R2-A2, while personalization showed weaker agreement with means between 1.50 and 1.93 across tasks (p. 9). Anxiety reduction and confidence support were also only moderate compared with the strongest clarity/relevance/accessibility indicators (p. 9).

The comparison items show a clearer preference for instructor feedback. Students rated in-text instructor comments as more helpful in later assignments, while ratings for ChatGPT as most helpful declined substantially in R3-A3 and R4-A4, reaching means of 2.44 and 2.53 respectively (p. 10). Table 2 reports total group means from 1.34 to 1.64 across the four assignment points and an ANOVA result of F = 2.40, Sig. = 0.072, which the author interprets as no statistically significant difference among group means at the 0.05 level (p. 8). The stronger message therefore comes less from between-group significance and more from item-level patterns plus qualitative explanations.

Qualitatively, students appreciated feedback that identified specific issues in sentence structure, tense, research questions, transitions, readability, and organization. When students believed the combined comments were teacher-generated, they attributed helpfulness to the teacher's personal understanding of their needs and found the feedback motivating (p. 10). Yet they also criticized some feedback for lacking examples or reasons, especially when comments were broad or ambiguous around areas such as research significance and delimitations (p. 10).

In Scenario 2, students liked being able to consult ChatGPT independently and compare its fast grammar and sentence-organization suggestions with teacher feedback. This encouraged some self-directed learning, but students still reported greater trust in the instructor's feedback, especially for content, organization, methodology, analysis, and explanation of specific academic mistakes (pp. 10-11). ChatGPT's volume of feedback sometimes became burdensome, making it difficult to decide what to revise first (pp. 10-12).

## Discussion & Conclusion

The discussion interprets instructor feedback as more individualized, detailed, comprehensive, and aligned with each student's learning profile, while ChatGPT feedback is valued for speed, accessibility, neutrality, and immediate practical suggestions (p. 11). The study does not dismiss AI feedback; rather, it positions AI as most useful for surface-level and preliminary writing support. For grammar and sentence organization, ChatGPT is treated as a helpful source of rapid suggestions; for higher-order content, structure, methodology, and analytical judgment, instructor feedback remains more trusted and pedagogically richer (pp. 11-13).

The conclusion repeats the central pedagogical position: human feedback remains preferable for many students because it is more individualized and detailed, but AI tools can still support writing development when used alongside teacher feedback (p. 13). The implication is a hybrid feedback model: AI can expand access and immediacy, while teachers provide prioritization, contextual judgment, affective support, and academic-rhetorical depth (pp. 12-13).

## Purpose

The paper's problem is not simply whether ChatGPT can give useful writing feedback. The narrower problem is how EFL students perceive instructor feedback and ChatGPT feedback when both operate within the same academic-writing course. The abstract establishes the rationale: while several studies had examined large language models for writing feedback, the author argues that it remained unclear how learners perceive AI feedback in combination with instructor feedback (p. 1). The introduction then makes the gap more precise by saying prior studies had generally examined each type separately and that the current study compares the effect of instructor feedback and AI-generated feedback on academic writing development, preferences, motivation, confidence, and performance (p. 2).

This framing is useful because it moves beyond the replacement question. ChatGPT is not treated as a possible substitute teacher in a vacuum. Instead, the study asks where ChatGPT is perceived to work well, where instructor feedback remains stronger, and how a hybrid arrangement might better support EFL academic writing. The practical research object is therefore feedback ecology: a course-level system in which learners encounter teacher comments, AI-generated comments, and their own choices about how to use both.

## Theory & Literature Review

The literature review builds its rationale in three layers.

First, it grounds feedback in theories of mediated learning. Vygotskian sociocultural theory is used to describe feedback as scaffolding in the learner's ZPD; the key premise is that students can complete more demanding writing tasks when guided by a teacher or more capable peer than they could through independent problem-solving alone (p. 2). This matters for a feedback study because it suggests feedback quality cannot be reduced to whether an error is flagged. Effective feedback must fit the learner's current developmental needs, task demands, and capacity for revision. The review's constructivist layer adds that learners actively revise and rebuild knowledge through reflection and instructor input (p. 2). Together, these frameworks support a view of feedback as dialogic, adaptive, and developmental.

Second, the review situates instructor feedback within L2 writing research. It discusses corrective feedback as a long-standing mechanism for improving accuracy over time and distinguishes direct from indirect feedback (p. 3). Direct feedback gives students the corrected form and is framed as especially useful for lower-proficiency learners, whereas indirect feedback requires learners to diagnose and repair errors themselves, making it potentially more engaging for students who can handle the cognitive demand (p. 3). For this paper, that distinction is important because the participants are advanced C2-level students (p. 5). They are not merely looking for grammar correction; they are positioned as capable of evaluating complex writing advice about structure, research components, methodology, and discussion.

Third, the review positions AI feedback as promising but incomplete. ChatGPT and similar systems can offer rapid, accessible feedback and support repeated revision (pp. 2-3). Prior studies in Saudi and broader EFL settings report benefits for grammar, organization, feedback access, preliminary drafting, and learner autonomy (pp. 3-4). At the same time, the review repeatedly identifies personalization, consistency, accuracy, academic integrity, and depth of content engagement as unresolved concerns (pp. 3-4). A concise statement of the core limitation is that AI feedback may show an "absence of personalization" (p. 3). The Saudi-context subsection strengthens this claim by reporting a recurring pattern: learners may welcome AI support for lower-level tasks but rely on instructor feedback for critical thinking, complex analysis, contextual depth, and rhetorical sophistication (p. 4).

The literature review therefore prepares the reader for a differentiated finding. It does not predict that AI feedback will simply be good or bad. It predicts that students may value ChatGPT for immediacy, accessibility, and lower-level revision, while continuing to need instructor feedback for individualized, context-sensitive, academically situated writing support.

## Gaps & Research Questions

The paper's gap is comparative and ecological: it asks about student perceptions of instructor and AI feedback concurrently, not each source in isolation. The author argues that prior international and Saudi studies show AI-feedback benefits but do not sufficiently address how students perceive the combined presence of AI and instructor feedback in the same writing process (p. 2).

The four research questions are:

1. How do EFL students perceive instructor feedback compared with ChatGPT feedback for improving academic writing?
2. What is the relative effectiveness of instructor versus AI-generated feedback for clarity, structure, and coherence?
3. How do the feedback types affect motivation and confidence in academic-paper writing?
4. What key benefits and challenges do students associate with ChatGPT feedback?

These questions are well aligned with the mixed-methods design because they require both scaled comparisons and qualitative interpretation of why students preferred one form of feedback for particular writing purposes (pp. 4, 6-7).

## Theoretical Framework

The explicit theoretical frame is not developed as a formal model, but three assumptions guide the study.

First, feedback is scaffolded assistance. The review's use of Vygotsky implies that feedback is most pedagogically valuable when it mediates students' movement from current performance to potential performance (p. 2). Second, feedback is constructive and revision-oriented; students are expected to interpret, revise, and build knowledge through feedback rather than merely receive corrections (p. 2). Third, feedback must be personalized enough to address learner needs. The review cites personalized feedback as student-centered information delivered in a motivationally supportive way (p. 3). These assumptions support the later interpretation that instructor feedback is more effective for higher-order and affective dimensions because the teacher can draw on course context, task expectations, and student profiles.

## Methodology

### Context

The study took place in an online advanced academic writing course in Saudi Arabia. Students wrote a research paper through four sequenced assignments: introduction, literature review, methodology, and results/discussion (p. 5). The sequence matters because feedback was not an isolated one-off event; it was embedded in a staged writing process across two semesters with repeated interactions between students, instructor, assignments, and feedback sources (pp. 5-6).

### Participants

The participants were 112 senior EFL students in an English Language and Translation program at a public university in Riyadh. They were final-year undergraduates and described as C2-level English learners under the CEFR (p. 5). The paper justifies this population by arguing that advanced learners can engage with complex linguistic structures and critique sophisticated academic feedback mechanisms (p. 5). This strengthens the study's relevance for advanced academic writing, but it also limits generalizability to lower-proficiency EFL learners.

### Data Sources

The study used two surveys and open-ended responses. Survey A followed the first scenario, in which students received instructor feedback plus ChatGPT-assisted overall comments without knowing that ChatGPT had been used. Survey B followed the second scenario, in which students knowingly consulted ChatGPT before receiving instructor feedback (pp. 6, 14-17). Both surveys used 4-point Likert items and open-ended questions. The forced-choice scale removed a neutral midpoint, pushing students toward agreement or disagreement (p. 6).

### Procedure

In Scenario 1, Assignments 1 and 2 were submitted to the instructor. The instructor provided both her own comments and ChatGPT-generated feedback, but students were unaware of ChatGPT's role; the AI-generated part was labelled as overall comments, while actual teacher feedback appeared as in-text comments (pp. 5-6). This scenario tested perceptions of feedback quality without explicit knowledge of AI authorship.

In Scenario 2, Assignments 3 and 4 required students to consult ChatGPT independently before submission. The instructor then gave her own feedback without using ChatGPT afterward (p. 6). This scenario tested perceptions when students knowingly used AI and then compared it with teacher comments.

The same instructor taught all 112 students, which likely helped keep course expectations, grading practice, and feedback style consistent (p. 6). However, the same-instructor design also means teacher effects are inseparable from the feedback-source comparison.

### Analysis Methods

Quantitative Likert-scale data were analyzed through descriptive statistics and inferential comparisons, including ANOVA for RQ1 (pp. 7-8). Qualitative open-ended data were analyzed thematically. The author reports a six-phase thematic-analysis process: familiarization, initial coding, theme searching, theme reviewing, defining/naming themes, and producing the report (p. 7). This method is appropriate for explaining why students preferred certain feedback sources, but the paper provides limited procedural detail on coding reliability, coder number, audit trail, or example codes, so qualitative rigor should be treated as partially documented rather than fully transparent.

## Findings

### RQ1: Perceived Usefulness of Instructor vs. ChatGPT Feedback

Students generally viewed ChatGPT feedback as clear, relevant, useful, timely, accessible, and fair, especially for identifying areas of improvement and offering understandable suggestions. Table 1 shows strong agreement on relevance in R1-A1 and R2-A2, with means of 1.03 and 1.14, respectively; usefulness also remained in the strong-agreement range, with means from 1.21 to 1.44 (pp. 8-9). This supports the claim that ChatGPT feedback was not rejected by students. It had real perceived value.

However, the same quantitative pattern shows weaker agreement on personalization and affective support. Personalization means ranged from 1.50 to 1.93, and anxiety reduction ranged from 1.65 to 2.00, suggesting that students were less convinced that ChatGPT feedback fit their writing style, needs, or emotional concerns (p. 9). Instructor feedback was preferred for depth and personal relevance, especially where students compared in-text teacher comments with ChatGPT feedback (pp. 8-10).

The qualitative data explain this pattern. In the concealed-use scenario, students found feedback useful because it identified concrete issues such as sentence structure, tense, research questions, transitions, readability, and organization; because they assumed all feedback came from the teacher, they attributed its helpfulness to teacher understanding of their needs (p. 10). When ChatGPT was explicitly involved, students still liked its speed and scope, but they trusted the instructor more for content and organization (pp. 10-11).

### RQ2: Relative Effectiveness for Clarity, Structure, and Coherence

The results differentiate feedback by writing level. ChatGPT was useful for clarity and lower-level improvement, especially grammar and sentence organization. The discussion states that AI feedback was effective for "grammar and sentence organization" (p. 11). Students also found ChatGPT fast and comprehensive for basic language and organization suggestions (p. 10).

Instructor feedback, by contrast, was perceived as stronger for global organization, clarity, task relevance, and higher-order writing concerns. The discussion says instructor feedback was most effective for "general organization and clarity" (p. 11). Qualitative comments indicate that teacher feedback was viewed as more specific and task-oriented, particularly for content, organization, methodology, and analytical aspects of the research paper (pp. 10-11). This distinction is central: AI feedback helps students notice and revise surface or mid-level language features, while instructor feedback helps students interpret the academic task itself.

### RQ3: Motivation and Confidence

Students associated instructor feedback more strongly with support, confidence, and reduced anxiety. The discussion states that the effect size of instructor feedback was greater than AI feedback for motivation and confidence, and that students felt more supported, confident, and less anxious when the instructor provided both positive and negative feedback (p. 12). ChatGPT feedback was valued for quickness but was less encouraging and less effective in decreasing assignment anxiety (p. 12).

This affective finding is important for academic-writing pedagogy. Feedback is not only information about errors; it shapes students' willingness to revise, confidence in their academic judgment, and tolerance for complex writing tasks. The study suggests that AI feedback may increase access to revision advice but does not automatically provide the relational reassurance students need for high-stakes academic writing.

### RQ4: Benefits and Challenges of ChatGPT Feedback

Students identified several benefits of ChatGPT: it is instant, accessible, standardized, and able to provide practical suggestions (p. 12). These benefits are especially relevant in courses where instructor workload limits the frequency of detailed feedback. ChatGPT can act as a preliminary revision partner, giving students something to work with before instructor review.

The challenges are more serious for advanced writing. Students described ChatGPT feedback as insufficiently personal, too general, less suitable for academic context, and weak for methodology and data analysis (p. 12). Some also found the volume of feedback overwhelming, because extensive AI comments did not necessarily help them prioritize revisions (pp. 10-12). The paper attributes this to algorithmic limitations, limited contextual awareness, lack of learner-specific data integration, and constrained adaptability to specific writing goals (p. 12).

## Key Claims

1. ChatGPT feedback is useful but domain-limited: it works best for immediacy, grammar, sentence organization, accessibility, and preliminary revision support (pp. 8-12).
2. Instructor feedback remains stronger for personalization, task interpretation, content development, organization, methodology, analysis, motivation, and confidence (pp. 10-13).
3. Students' trust is not determined only by feedback quantity or speed; it is tied to perceived pedagogical knowledge, awareness of the learner, and fit with assignment demands (pp. 10-12).
4. Hybrid feedback is the recommended pedagogical direction: AI should complement, not replace, instructor feedback (pp. 1, 12-13).
5. The study's evidence is strongest for advanced Saudi EFL learners in one online writing course and weaker for broader EFL populations, lower-proficiency students, other AI tools, or later LLM versions (p. 13).

## Discussion

The discussion's main contribution is its differentiated model of feedback value. Instructor and AI feedback are not treated as interchangeable. Instructor feedback is interpreted as more individualized because teachers can draw on a student's learning profile, assignment history, course rubric, and the local meaning of the task (p. 11). ChatGPT feedback is interpreted as useful because it is fast, accessible, and capable of giving standardized suggestions, but it is weaker when feedback requires contextual judgment, prioritization, and academic discourse expertise (pp. 11-12).

For RQ1, the paper argues that students see instructor feedback as more individualized and detailed, while appreciating ChatGPT for rapid access and helpfulness (p. 11). The key discussion move is that usefulness does not equal preference. Students can agree that ChatGPT feedback is useful while still preferring instructor feedback for serious academic-writing development.

For RQ2, the study maps the feedback sources onto different aspects of writing. ChatGPT is aligned with grammar, sentence organization, and basic coherence; the instructor is aligned with organization, clarity, content, methodology, and analysis (pp. 10-12). This is an important distinction for literature-review use because it suggests AI-feedback studies should disaggregate writing outcomes. A tool can be useful for linguistic accuracy but inadequate for research argumentation or academic genre development.

For RQ3, the paper emphasizes the emotional dimension of feedback. Instructor feedback is linked to motivation, support, confidence, and anxiety reduction, whereas ChatGPT is less encouraging even when students appreciate its speed (p. 12). This strengthens a sociocultural interpretation: feedback works not only because it provides information, but because it is socially situated and pedagogically recognized.

For RQ4, the paper frames ChatGPT's major limitation as a personalization problem. Students found AI feedback general, sometimes excessive, and weak for methodology and data analysis (p. 12). The discussion attributes this to the absence of learner-specific data integration, limited contextual awareness, and constrained adaptability (p. 12). The proposed remedy is not to discard AI, but to design hybrid systems that embed instructor input and preserve pedagogical judgment (p. 12).

## Conclusion

The conclusion states that AI feedback has clear advantages for writing instruction but that human feedback remains preferable for most students because it is more individualized, detailed, motivationally supportive, and aligned with higher-order academic-writing needs (p. 13). ChatGPT is most defensible as a complementary support for immediate and accessible revision, especially around grammar and sentence organization. It should not be positioned as a stand-alone substitute for instructor feedback in advanced EFL academic writing.

## Unique Contributions

The paper's first contribution is contextual: it focuses on Saudi advanced EFL undergraduates in an online academic-writing course, adding evidence from a setting where AI tools are increasingly used but where learners still value human instructional judgment (pp. 4-6).

The second contribution is methodological: the two-scenario design compares concealed AI-assisted feedback and explicit student use of ChatGPT. This distinction is useful because perceptions may shift depending on whether students know AI is involved and whether they use it independently (pp. 5-6, 10-11).

The third contribution is pedagogical: it does not simply ask whether ChatGPT feedback is effective. It identifies which feedback functions students assign to AI and which they reserve for instructors. This makes the paper more useful for designing feedback workflows than studies that report only global satisfaction with AI tools.

## Limitations

The paper identifies several limitations. The sample was restricted to senior advanced EFL students at one public university in Riyadh, so the findings may not generalize to lower-proficiency learners, other institutions, younger students, or different national contexts (p. 13). The study used ChatGPT-4 as the AI tool, which limits claims about other LLM versions or writing-specific tools such as Grammarly, Writefull, or Elicit (p. 13). The rapid development of LLMs also means findings from one model version may age quickly (p. 13).

Additional methodological limitations are implicit. The same instructor both taught and provided teacher feedback, so instructor identity and feedback source are partly confounded. The qualitative analysis is described through thematic-analysis phases, but the paper does not provide enough detail about coder triangulation, intercoder agreement, or examples of coding decisions (p. 7). The quantitative results are useful descriptively, but some inferential evidence is modest; for instance, Table 2 reports Sig. = 0.072, which is not significant at the 0.05 level (p. 8).

## Future Work

The paper recommends research across different educational settings, proficiency levels, AI systems, and time periods (p. 13). Longitudinal studies are especially important because writing development and feedback literacy unfold over time, and LLM capabilities change rapidly (pp. 13-14). Comparative studies should also test general-purpose LLMs against specialized academic-writing tools to determine whether personalization and precision improve when tools are designed specifically for scholarly writing support (p. 13). Future designs would benefit from direct writing-performance measures, clearer coding transparency, and experimental separation of feedback source, feedback format, and instructor identity.

## Relevance to My Study

This paper is relevant for research on AI in language education, EFL writing pedagogy, feedback literacy, teacher agency, and human-AI instructional design. Its most useful insight is that student acceptance of AI feedback is conditional: learners may welcome ChatGPT for speed and initial revision but still depend on teachers for interpretation, prioritization, academic judgment, and emotional assurance. For studies of teacher agency, the paper supports an argument that AI changes the distribution of feedback labor but does not eliminate the teacher's mediating role. For studies of learner autonomy, it shows that independent ChatGPT use can support self-directed revision, but only when students can judge which AI suggestions are relevant and how they compare with instructor feedback.

## Possible Use in Literature Review

This article can support three literature-review claims.

First, it can be used to argue that EFL students do not evaluate AI feedback globally; they differentiate feedback usefulness by writing dimension. ChatGPT is valued for grammar, sentence organization, speed, and accessibility, while instructor feedback is valued for personalization, content, organization, methodology, analysis, and motivation (pp. 8-13).

Second, it can support the claim that hybrid feedback models are pedagogically more defensible than replacement models. The study's conclusion and implications both recommend combining AI immediacy with instructor expertise, especially because students may be overwhelmed by AI feedback without teacher guidance (pp. 12-13).

Third, it can be cited as evidence that AI-feedback research should include affective outcomes. Motivation, confidence, anxiety, and trust are central to whether feedback is taken up, particularly in advanced academic-writing assignments where students must manage complex genre expectations and high cognitive load (pp. 9, 12).
