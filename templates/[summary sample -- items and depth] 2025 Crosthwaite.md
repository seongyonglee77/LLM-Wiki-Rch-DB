# Structured Summary

## Purpose:
To explore both affordances and issues related to the introduction and potential integration of generative AI within the English for Academic Purposes classroom, serving as a guide to achieving best practice for both learners and practitioners.

## Background (Theory & Literature Review):
Despite the ready availability of AI applications for academic writing, generative AI (GAI) has posed significant challenges for EAP given its ability to instantly produce academic text across any genre or discipline. While GAI use is difficult to detect and may produce hallucinated citations and references, it also offers potential for instant feedback, exemplar creation, assessment items, and increased learner motivation. The chapter reviews the limited but growing body of AI-related research in EAP and applied linguistics journals.

## Method:
This is a comprehensive review chapter. The author synthesizes literature across multiple domains including: (1) AI applications in EAP (AES, AWE, ASR, MT, plagiarism detection), (2) empirical studies on ChatGPT/GAI in academic writing, (3) corpus linguistic comparisons of AI- and human-produced texts, and (4) pedagogical implications and future directions.

## Findings:

### Affordances:
- **Feedback**: AI provides personalized, instant feedback. GPT feedback rated as more relevant and balanced across content, organization, and language than teacher feedback. AI+Teacher model shows students incorporate more co-produced feedback. However, ChatGPT WCF varies considerably even with same prompts.
- **Positive Motivation**: AI enhances language learning, facilitates collaboration, reduces writing anxiety, and significantly improves writing motivation, self-efficacy, and engagement.
- **Assessment**: GPT3.5/GPT4o can accurately predict essay scores and assess error rates, though reliability varies by prompt and task sequence.

### Weaknesses:
- **Academic Language/Register**: AI-produced academic texts show significant differences from human writing in lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, epistemic stance, and thematic development. ChatGPT uses narrower, more repetitive vocabulary bundles.
- **Plagiarism**: AI lowers barriers to cheating while making detection difficult. AI detection tools show significant false positive rates. Students and reviewers often cannot distinguish AI- from human-generated texts.
- **Hallucination**: GAI produces factually incorrect citations, references, and content. Students are developing critical skills to spot unreliable output.
- **Passive Learning**: Over-reliance on AI may reduce creativity, criticality, and independent skill development. However, students can self-regulate AI use when trained.

## Discussion & Conclusion:
EAP practitioners must move beyond debates about whether AI should be allowed to how to successfully integrate it. Future directions include embedded AI in writing applications (Microsoft CoPilot, smartphone integration) and professional development needs. The field requires tailored institutional support, peer support, and policy innovations. AI literacy frameworks spanning six constructs (benefits/drawbacks, GPT-assisted assessment, critical evaluation, ethics, prompt engineering) are needed for successful integration.

# Deep Summary

## Purpose:
This chapter provides a comprehensive, balanced review of artificial intelligence (particularly generative AI and large language models) in English for Academic Purposes. It aims to serve as a practical guide for EAP practitioners and learners, exploring both the transformative potential and significant challenges of AI integration in academic writing instruction, assessment, and feedback.

## Theory & Literature Review:

**AI in EAP Context**:
- AI describes computerized applications using NLP combined with rule-based or predictive models (machine/deep learning)
- Traditional AI applications in EAP: Automated Essay Scoring (AES), Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE), automated speech recognition (ASR), machine translation (MT), plagiarism detection
- Generative AI (ChatGPT, LLMs) represents transformative shift, offering immediate feedback on grammar, vocabulary, coherence, and citation suggestions (p. 562)

**GAI Capabilities and Concerns**:
- ChatGPT achieved first-class grade on UK physics essay (Yeadon et al., 2023)
- SWOT analysis highlights: information accessibility, workload reduction, personalized learning as strengths; limited topic comprehension, inability to critically assess information as weaknesses; academic integrity challenges and plagiarism as threats (Farrokhnia et al., 2024) (p. 563)

**Research Landscape in EAP Journals**:
- Surprisingly little direct AI activity in EAP before late 2022
- Journal of English for Academic Purposes: only 18 articles mentioning "artificial intelligence" since 2010, with only 2 dealing directly with AI
- Key studies: Grieve et al. (2024) on ethical AI-assisted MT; Kim & Lu (2024) on ChatGPT for Swalesian move-step analyses; Kong & Liu (2024) on GAI-generated vs. human abstracts; Flowerdew & Petrić (2024) on corpus-based pedagogy for ESP (p. 563-564)
- Major EAP journals slow to feature AI research, though frequency rapidly increasing

## Gaps & RQs:
- Limited direct AI research in major EAP journals before 2022
- Need for comprehensive synthesis of affordances and issues
- Uncertainty about best practices for AI integration in EAP
- Questions about future directions as technology evolves rapidly
- Need for practitioner guidance on navigating AI transformation

## Method (brief):
- context: Review chapter in The Routledge Handbook of English for Academic Purposes
- procedure: Comprehensive narrative review synthesizing literature across multiple domains
- data_collection: Analysis of empirical studies, corpus linguistic research, pedagogical reports, and theoretical discussions from EAP and applied linguistics literature
- analysis_methods: Thematic synthesis organized around affordances (feedback, motivation, assessment) and weaknesses (register, plagiarism, hallucination, passive learning)

## Findings:

### Affordances

**1. Feedback**:
- **Personalized Feedback**: Li et al. (2024) found GPT3.5 and GPT4o feedback rated by Chinese L2 students as more relevant to specific needs than teacher feedback, which focused mainly on accuracy (p. 565)
- **Balanced Coverage**: GPT feedback considered balanced across content, organization, and language domains, unlike teacher feedback (p. 565)
- **Volume**: Guo & Wang (2023) appreciated larger volume of ChatGPT feedback addressing content, organization, and language with equal quantity and quality (p. 565)
- **Consistency Issues**: Lin & Crosthwaite (2024) found ChatGPT WCF varied considerably even with same prompt on same text, making standardization across cohorts difficult (p. 565)
- **Editing/Proofreading**: Barrot (2023) suggests GAI significant role in editing if carefully implemented and supervised (p. 565)
- **Student Preferences**: Allen & Mizumoto (2024) found Japanese EAP students preferred GAI-assisted editing over student-led groups for targeted feedback (p. 565)
- **Advantage over Grammar Checkers**: Kohnke (2024) found students preferred GAI over automated grammar checkers because GAI "clearly explain[s] why [students] are wrong" (p. 565)
- **Human Touch Still Valued**: Teng (2024) found students missed "human touch" in teacher feedback over ChatGPT's overly formal nature (p. 566)
- **AI+Teacher Model**: Han & Li (2024) co-produced feedback (ChatGPT + teacher modification) resulted in students incorporating more feedback into revisions, "leveraging the analytic strengths of AI while maintaining essential teacher-student interactions" (p. 566)

**2. Positive Motivation**:
- **Enhanced Learning**: Yang & Kyun (2022) review of 25 studies found AI enhances language learning and collaboration, with teacher guidance leading to improved outcomes (p. 566)
- **Reduced Anxiety**: Chan & Hu (2023) observed GAI in L2 writing reduces time spent and alleviates frustration and anxiety (p. 566)
- **Motivation and Self-Efficacy**: Teng (2024) found ChatGPT had "significant effect" on writing motivation, self-efficacy, engagement, and intention to write collaboratively (p. 566)
- **Self-Regulated Learning**: Wang (2024) trained students in GAI affordances for brainstorming, source-seeking, word meaning exploration, significantly improving writing efficacy and perception of English education quality (p. 566)
- **Increased Engagement**: Research suggests AI incorporation has resulted in increased student engagement with academic writing as a discipline (p. 566)

**3. Assessment**:
- **Automated Essay Scoring**: Mizumoto & Eguchi (2023) used GPT3.5 to assess 12,500 ETS essays, finding accurate score prediction (p. 567)
- **Error Assessment**: Mizumoto et al. (2024a) found strong correlation between ChatGPT and human expert evaluations of error rates in Cambridge Learner Corpus (p. 567)
- **Limitations**: Pfau et al. (2023) found ChatGPT rarely misidentified errors but frequently underestimated overall error count (p. 567)
- **Prompt Sensitivity**: Poole & Coss (2024) found correlation between human and ChatGPT evaluations heavily influenced by prompts used, with task sequence impacting reliability (p. 567)
- **Model Improvement**: Li et al. (2024) reported GPT4o outperformed human raters, while Kim et al. (2024) found ChatGPT 4 reliability "moderate to low" (p. 567)
- **Assessment Future**: Higher education struggling with ethical AI use in assessment; potential shift from essays to non-written assessment forms (video reflections) may require EAP instruction rethink (p. 566-567)

### Weaknesses

**1. Academic Language and Register**:
- **Register Differences**: Berber-Sardinha (2024) used multidimensional analysis finding "AI's ability to capture the intricate patterns of natural language remains limited" (p. 567)
- **Linguistic Features**: Mizumoto et al. (2024b) found significant differences between AI- and human-produced essays across lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, modals, epistemic markers, and discourse markers (p. 567)
- **Thematic Development**: Yang et al. (2024) using SFL found ChatGPT lacks human-like thematic development, producing "redundant, list-like writing" (p. 567-568)
- **Lexical Bundles**: Jiang & Hyland (2024) found ChatGPT uses narrower, repetitive 3-word lexical bundles with less epistemic stance and authorial presence (p. 568)
- **Vocabulary Differences**: Zhang & Crosthwaite (2025) found ChatGPT excels at formal, complex vocabulary for academic themes while human L2 writers focus on personal/social issues with more varied, context-rich vocabulary (p. 568)
- **Stance and Engagement**: Mo & Crosthwaite (2025) found human writers use stance/engagement features more frequently with significantly narrower, more repetitive lexis in GAI essays (p. 568)
- **Conclusion**: "AI still has a long way to go in really capturing the essence of 'the human touch' in academic writing" (p. 568)

**2. Plagiarism**:
- **Detection Difficulties**: Lodge et al. (2023) suggest barriers to cheating lowered while detection became "significantly more difficult, if not impossible" (p. 568)
- **False Positives**: Liang et al. (2023), Kar et al. (2024), Ibrahim (2023) found significant false positive rates in AI detection software (p. 568)
- **Human Detection Failure**: Guo et al. (2023) found over half of participants unable to distinguish ChatGPT from human writing; Casal & Kessler (2023) found reviewers could not distinguish AI- from human-generated abstracts; Fleckenstein et al. (2024) found teachers unable to distinguish AI- from student-written texts (p. 568-569)
- **Equity and Ethics**: Foung et al. (2024) questions about equitable and ethical implementation (p. 569)
- **Acceptable Use Spectrum**: Rowland (2023) notes "different students and different academics 'draw the line' in different places" from proofreading to co-authoring to full AI adoption (p. 569)
- **Student Perspectives**: Almanea (2024) found Saudi EAP students acknowledged affordances but revealed temptation to over-use ChatGPT in absence of reliable detection (p. 569)
- **Academic Publishing**: Tang et al. (2024) discuss need for AI usage declarations in journals, though less than half of surveyed medical journals required such statements (p. 569)

**3. Hallucination**:
- **Definition**: Term describes inconsistencies/inaccuracies in GAI output; technically GAI always hallucinating through statistical approximations (p. 569)
- **Academic Writing Issues**: Hsu (2023) found when prompting ChatGPT 4.0 for full research paper: 12-hour process, omitted key variables, used wrong methods despite prompts, "correct sounding but logically incorrect" results (p. 569)
- **Citation Problems**: Well-known that GAI invents studies that don't exist (Glickman & Zhang, 2024) (p. 569)
- **Student Critical Skills**: Glahn (2024) notes EAP students developing critical skills to spot unreliable GAI production, particularly inaccurate citations (p. 569)

**4. Passive Approach to Learning**:
- **Critical Thinking Loss**: Fuchs (2023) warns uncritical acceptance of GAI may lead to failure in developing key academic skills including source evaluation and reliability assessment (p. 569)
- **Skill Reduction**: Barrot (2023) notes overreliance could reduce creativity, criticality, clarity, and precision (p. 569)
- **Self-Regulation**: Wang (2024) found students split on whether GAI hinders critical thinking, but learning journals showed students realized limitations, developed sophistication in tool selection, and self-regulated use "in response to the evolving dynamics of the learning environment" (p. 569-570)
- **Balance Challenge**: Difficulty in getting balance right between incorporating AI without sacrificing crucial EAP skills (p. 570)

## Discussion:

**Future Directions**:
- **Beyond Chatbots**: Next challenge is shift from relational chatbots to GAI embedded in writing applications (Microsoft CoPilot, smartphone integration), potentially rendering ChatGPT obsolete (p. 570)
- **Implications**: Major implications for academic writing processes including planning/brainstorming, research/citation/referencing, drafting/editing/revising, and assessment/feedback (p. 570)
- **Professional Development**: Kohnke et al. (2023) found Hong Kong EAP teachers familiar with AI tools but lacking pedagogical know-how for integration (p. 570)
- **AI Literacy Framework**: Ma et al. (2024) developed ChatGPT Literacy framework with six constructs: understanding benefits/drawbacks, GPT-assisted assessment, critical evaluation, ethics, prompt engineering (p. 570)
- **Curriculum Integration**: Tseng & Lin (2024) incorporated ChatGPT 3.5 into tertiary EAP course, finding "the application of AI can enhance the learning experience without compromising the individuality of student expression" (p. 570)
- **AI for Academic Purposes (AIAP)**: Ngo & Hastie (2025) embedded 10-week generative AI literacy module in Scottish university EAP program, resulting in improved critical evaluation, increased ethical understanding, and new affordances beyond proofreading (p. 570)

**Key Takeaways**:
- EAP field unprepared for paradigm shift but integration already underway
- Need for tailored institutional and disciplinary support
- Professional development must include self-paced targeted pedagogical training
- Policy innovations needed for forward-facing AI integration
- More research needed on curriculum-level incorporation of AI in EAP
- Technology will continue evolving, requiring ongoing adaptation
