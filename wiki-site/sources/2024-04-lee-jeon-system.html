Teacher agency and ICT affordances in classroom-based language assessment: The return to face-to-face classes after online teaching

Seongyong Lee

a , Jaeho Jeon b,*

A R T I C L E I N F O

1. Introduction

Teachers play agentic roles in classroom-based assessment (CBA), which focuses on the ongoing process of monitoring students ' learning progress, by implementing pedagogical decision-making based on a learning-oriented formative assessment (Davison & Leung, 2009; Rea-Dickins, 2004; Willis et al., 2019). Teacher agency, defined as their capacity to notice, choose, and act on peda­ gogical opportunities in a local educational environment (Gibson, 1986; Priestley et al., 2015; van Lier, 2004), illustrates the importance of their instructional practices in responding to critical situations in CBA (Ali & Hamid, 2022; Chong & Isaacs, 2023). The COVID-19 pandemic has enhanced our understanding of how teacher agency was enacted on CBA against such critical incidents in education (Chen, 2023a; Fazel & Ali, 2022; Yan et al., 2021). Research shows that the pandemic prompted a critical evaluation of traditional assessment methods in classrooms, enabling teachers to maintain learning-oriented assessment in virtual environments with limited opportunities for dialogic communication and dynamic interactions (Fazel & Ali, 2022; I. Lee, 2021).

A B S T R A C T

Recent research on classroom-based assessment (CBA) during the COVID-19 pandemic has examined language teachers ' strategic responses to challenges in online teaching. However, there is still a paucity of empirical research on their CBA practices supported by information and communication technology (ICT) during the return to face-to-face (f2f) classes. Moreover, most studies on CBA during and after the pandemic have focused on secondary or higher education, leaving young learner contexts underexplored. Thus, this study investigated how two primary school English teachers in South Korea exercised their agency to implement ICT-afforded CBA to address challenges in returning to f2f classes, grounded in an ecological perspective. It further explored mediating factors that influenced their agency in both temporal and relational aspects. Observations, interviews, and reflection papers revealed four main themes regarding their agency enactment in identifying ICT affordances for CBA, which were linked to individual factors in their agency enactment, including their pedagogical beliefs, ICT use experiences, identity, and pro­ fessional development. The cultural (e.g., national curriculum), structural (e.g., student wellness and parental voices), and material dimensions (e.g., institutional support of teacher training) also influenced their agency enactment. This study provides both theoretical and practical implica­ tions for ICT-afforded CBA in the post-pandemic era.

The ecological perspective effectively explains how teacher agency can be exercised in relation to ICT affordances as pedagogical opportunities offered by technological resources that influence teachers ' instructional decisions and practices (Chen, 2022; Kitade, 2015; Priestley et al., 2015). In fact, the dramatic shift to online L2 education during COVID-19 fostered dynamic interactions between teacher agency and ICT affordances, especially when both teachers and students experienced limited access to technological and pedagogical resources (Ashton, 2022; Jeon et al., 2022b; Chen, 2022). For instance, research indicates that teachers demonstrated their agency by embracing diverse ICT affordances in reaction to the changing ecology of L2 education during the pandemic, main­ taining authentic CBA to enhance student learning (Chen, 2023b; Moorhouse et al., 2023). Teachers ' purposeful reflection on tech­ nology use in virtual environments, influenced by a variety of personal, cultural, and structural factors, affected their CBA practices as they adapted to online instruction during COVID-19 (Ali & Hamid, 2022; Chen, 2022; Mansouri et al., 2021).

Recent studies have revealed that the ecology of L2 teachers ' instructional and CBA practices changed upon returning to in-person classrooms from remote online teaching (e.g., Moorhouse, 2023a, 2023b; Pathiranage & Karunaratne, 2023). This transition led teachers to reevaluate factors influencing the interaction between their agency and ICT affordances, including professional teacher development (Chen, 2023a, 2023b), teachers ' digital competence (Moorhouse, 2023a), institutional and social support (Chen et al., 2023; Pathiranage & Karunaratne, 2023), student mental health (Moorhouse, 2023b), and teacher well-being (Chen, 2023b; Pressley, 2021). However, while these diverse aspects of ICT-integrated L2 education in face-to-face (f2f) classes are well-documented, as shown in Chen (2022; 2023a, 2023b), the dynamics between teacher agency and ICT affordances can further warrant critical examination to understand ' how professional learnings developed in emergency remote classroom situations may apply to the evolution of face-to-face teaching and learning beyond the pandemic ' (Chen, 2023b, p. 12).

Therefore, drawing on the ecological view on teacher agency and ICT affordances (Chen, 2022, 2023a; Levine, 2020; Priestley et al., 2015), this study investigated how two primary school English teachers in South Korea enacted their agency in adopting ICT affordances to tackle challenges arising from the return to f2f teaching, with a focus on their CBA practices. Notably, few studies have concentrated on implementing classroom-based L2 assessment in f2f settings post-pandemic, considering the ecological dynamics between teacher agency and ICT affordances. Also, while the formative aspect of CBA is highly recommended for young learners (J. Lee & Butler, 2020), existing research on L2 teachers ' CBA during and post-pandemic has targeted secondary or tertiary education, neglecting young learner contexts except for a few studies (e.g., Chen, 2023b; Moorhouse, 2023a; Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023). To bridge these gaps, this study examined the interface between teacher agency and ICT affordances within the ecological perspective on classroom-based L2 assessment, using the context of primary English education in South Korea as a case.

2. Literature review

2.1. The ecological perspective on teacher agency and ICT affordances

The affordance theory, as suggested by Gibson (1986), associates human behaviors with natural environments that provide diverse opportunities for action, known as affordances (e.g., rocks in a forest offering seating for humans). Affordances are not the fixed, objective properties of environments, but rather elements of the human-environment system; thus, although an environment may present unlimited action possibilities, only those affordances perceived by humans can be transformed into actual behaviors (Stof­ fregen, 2003). This ecological approach views affordances as actualizable, contingent upon an individual ' s intentions and capacities, which together constitute agency, a socioculturally-mediated capacity for potential action that is realized ' through the interplay of personal capacities and the resources, affordances and constraints of the environment ' (Priestley et al., 2015, p. 19).

The ecological perspective effectively explains the relationship between L2 teacher agency and affordances. This view hinges on the dynamic interaction between a teacher as an agent and the educational environment, emphasizing that teachers perceive and selectively exploit certain pedagogical affordances in their teaching contexts for L2 education. The ecological view thus focuses on how teachers evaluate and reflect on their practices considering these affordances and constraints particularly when they respond to challenges such as curriculum changes, where they actively use their agency for desired educational outcomes. In computer-assisted language learning (CALL), the pedagogical opportunities provided by technological resources are regarded as ICT affordances for language learning (Chen, 2022; Kitade, 2015). Chen (2022) underscores the dynamic interplay between teacher agency and ICT affordances in specific educational contexts, highlighting that CALL teachers ' reactions to diverse resources may materialize into pedagogical actions through their agency in identifying and employing ICT affordances, particularly ' in relation to various types of social, cultural, and historical change ' (Kitade, 2015, p. 418).

The sudden transition to emergency remote teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic required L2 teachers to address critical in­ cidents in online teaching by evaluating and enhancing their technological and pedagogical skills (Ashton, 2022; Chen, 2022; Teng & Wu, 2021). Chen (2022) recognized digital environments as a contextual factor influencing Chinese language teachers ' agency enactment based on their reflective teaching regarding the social impact of the pandemic. Drawing on Levine ' s (2020) human ecological language pedagogy, which connects L2 learning to the interaction between human agents, language socialization, and diverse contextual backgrounds, Chen (2023a) examined how L2 teachers adapted to remote online teaching and attained their professional development (PD) through continuous self-reflection on their instruction by reassessing their agency in daily instruction, social and emotional well-being, and teaching-learning dynamics. The findings conceptualized context-specific, transformative PD as a result of the mutual dependence of teacher agency and digital affordances within the ecology of L2 pedagogy.

In CBA contexts, the COVID-19 pandemic has facilitated research on teacher agency in implementing assessment within remote online teaching settings (e.g., Ali & Hamid, 2022; Yan et al., 2021). While many studies have focused on language teachers ' strategic responses to technological challenges to support accurate interpretation and feedback on student learning (Yan et al., 2021), there S. Lee and J. Jeon remains a scarcity of research exploring how teacher agency is actualized in relation to specific CBA cases in technology-rich classroom environments during and after the pandemic. In other words, research on the interplay between teacher agency and digital affordances in the post-COVID-19 era underscores the importance of integrating our knowledge on teacher agency and ICT affordances to enhance L2 teaching, aligning pandemic-era remote CBA practices with broader digital education trends for constructing adaptable educational communities (Chen, 2023a). This then highlights the need to reconsider an ecological perspective on teacher agency and ICT-based CBA within the during- and post-pandemic contexts (Chen, 2022; Tian et al., 2021).

The ecological perspective, as described by Priestley et al. (2015), asserts that teacher agency in local teaching contexts is mediated by temporal-relational resources, encompassing three dimensions: the iterational dimension (past experiences), the practical-evaluative dimension (present experiences shaped by current affordances and constraints), and the projective dimension (short-term and long-term goals). Fig. 1 illustrates the ecological perspective on teacher agency based on these dimensions: the iterational responses to current teaching conditions, the projective shaping responses based on future pedagogical goals, and the practical-evaluative factors that impact teacher agency in the present, such as cultural, structural, and material ones. Priestley et al. (2015) emphasize that ' agency is achieved in the present and is shaped by the nature of those present-day contexts ' (p. 131), high­ lighting the importance of current social and material factors for teacher agency. In other words, teacher agency is notably demon­ strated in present contexts by evaluating available resources and challenges for instructional decision-making, and influenced by previous experiences and future goals (Ashton, 2022; Priestley et al., 2015). Also, the enactment of teacher agency is mediated by cultural, structural, and material conditions in the present contexts. Fig. 1 illustrates the dynamics between the three dimensions with the practical-evaluative dimension positioned as a central hub for the enactment of teacher agency.

The COVID-19 pandemic has encouraged scholars to revisit the ecological perspective on teacher agency and affordances within CALL contexts: emergency remote teaching has presented unprecedented opportunities and challenges in enacting teacher agency in relation to ICT affordances, allowing for teachers ' PD through reflective teaching practices (Chen, 2022, 2023a, 2023b; Tian et al., 2021). Recognizing the need for a revised framework to better understand the factors influencing teacher agency in ICT-based CBA, a modified model of teacher agency and ICT affordances for CBA was developed (Fig. 2). It integrates new constructs from online teaching, such as pedagogical beliefs (Ashton, 2022; Jeon et al., 2022a; Chen, 2022), ICT affordances (Chen, 2022; Kitade, 2015), and online teaching experiences (Jeon et al., 2022a) within the CBA context (Tian et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021). It further adds remote online teaching experiences to the iterational dimension and f2f teaching contexts to the practical-evaluative dimension (Ashton, 2022; Chen, 2022, 2023a; Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023). In addition, the modified model indicates that cultural, structural, and material dimensions function as mediating factors influencing teacher agency in the practical-evaluative dimension, which may then lead to actualized benefits for CBA (Chen, 2022, 2023a; Jeon et al., 2022a; G. Kim et al., 2022).

2.2. Teacher agency in CBA

CBA has emerged in L2 education over the past two decades to align pedagogical goals and assessment practices (Davison & Leung, 2009; Leung & Mohan, 2004; Yan et al., 2021). This shift toward learning-oriented classroom assessment emphasizes the formative potential of teacher assessment (Fazel & Ali, 2022), with CBA sharing a conceptual foundation with assessment for learning (AfL) (Davison & Leung, 2009). This AfL aspect of CBA includes three important principles to promote student learning: supportive feedback, multiple sources of formative feedback, and student-centered assessment (Chong & Isaacs, 2023; Davison & Leung, 2009).

Research on L2 teacher roles in CBA has centered on their agency in addressing assessment opportunities and challenges in classrooms, with teachers exhibiting agency by designing and implementing learning-oriented assessments in critical educational situations (Ali & Hamid, 2022; Rea-Dickins, 2004). The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the significant impact of both individual and structural factors on teacher agency enactment in language education (Chen, 2022, 2023a; Jeon et al., 2022a; Zou et al., 2021). Research during the COVID-19 pandemic highlights the critical role of structural factors in shaping teacher agency in the sudden shift to remote teaching, which influenced teachers ' access to assessment resources (Ali and Hamid, 2022; Chen, 2023b; Zou et al., 2021). Specifically, language assessment policies (Fazel & Ali, 2022), institutional support (Chen et al., 2023), and exam-oriented cultures (Yan et al., 2021) significantly influenced teacher agency during this period. For example, Fazel and Ali (2022) found that while Malaysian university instructors faced limited control over assessment practices and systematic constraints due to authoritarian policies and an exam-oriented culture, the Canadian instructors enjoyed greater autonomy and agency in learning-oriented assessment conditions. Ashton (2022) also identified the significant impact of social structural factors, such as teacher-student relationships and cultural expectations, on teacher agency during the pandemic. Consequently, L2 teachers exercised their agency in modifying CBA designs and methods in line with changing educational policies (Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023; Yan et al., 2021).

In young learner contexts, there is a consensus that AfL should prioritize communicative skills over grammatical knowledge (Butler, 2009; Yan et al., 2021). However, CBA has not been extensively investigated in this context. In fact, CBA is accepted as a policy-supported practice in primary school education in Asian countries (Davison & Leung, 2009). For example, South Korea has promoted formative assessment as part of communicative language teaching through teachers ' autonomous decisions (Butler, 2009); however, clear guidelines for implementing CBA were not offered until the 2022 national English curriculum by the Ministry of Education (MOE) (B. C. Lee, 2021). In this context, some publications suggest reexamining CBA as a tool to advance student learning and reflect the shift in assessment trends towards learner-centered practices (Chong & Isaacs, 2023; J. Lee & Butler, 2020).

2.3. Teacher agency in ICT-afforded CBA

Recent studies on CBA have explored ICT affordances for formative assessment in both f2f and online teaching. Lam (2021) summarized the diverse benefits of e-portfolio for formative assessment, including task authenticity, assessment integration, the complement of students ' multiple artifacts, and the promotion of students ' media and technology literacy. A. Kim et al. (2022) noticed the enhanced student engagement, motivation, and interaction through app-based quizzes. Overall, using appropriate technologies as assessment tools may facilitate the effectiveness of CBA practices (Lam, 2021; I. Lee, 2021; Zou et al., 2021).

The full-scale shift to online teaching during the pandemic forced L2 teachers to explore ICT tools to implement CBA (Chen, 2023a; Fazel & Ali, 2022; I. Lee, 2021; Zou et al., 2021). Although this critical situation increased the demand for advanced skills for ICT use, teachers experienced constraints such as a lack of technological knowledge for CBA due to limited institutional support (I. Lee, 2021; Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023). Regarding contextual and cultural aspects, the absence of guidelines and support (Yan et al., 2021), S. Lee and J. Jeon exam-oriented policies (Fazel & Ali, 2022), limited interactions with students (Chen, 2023a), teacher burnout in remote teaching contexts (Pressley, 2021), and parents ' burden in digital labor (Chen, 2023b; Chen et al., 2023) were identified as perceived challenges against CBA practices during this period. Chen (2023a) adopted a human ecological language pedagogy (Levine, 2020; Swanson & Levine, 2020) as a theoretical framework to explore diverse in-class and social factors influencing L2 teachers ' PD at three levels. Micro-level classroom activities related to students ' raised social justice and real-world L2 application, while at the meso-levels teachers integrated evolving social contexts into L2 curriculum, and at the macro-levels the promotion of cosmopolitan citizenship transformed educational practices and built resilient learning communities.

I. Lee (2021) observed that to tackle such contextual, cultural, and professional challenges in online CBA during the pandemic, L2 teachers decided to change their CBA practices in two different aspects: behavioral changes (e.g., the use of technology or alternative methods for CBA) and cognitive changes (e.g., decision-making regarding the CBA methods). Teacher agency was addressed as a driving force for such responses to educational demands in both online and in-person contexts (Chen, 2023a, 2023b; Zou et al., 2021). For example, in the context of primary school L2 education, Moorhouse (2023a, 2023b) found that teachers exercised their profes­ sional digital competence in using interactive digital technologies to enhance young students ' engagement in self-correction during online CBA. In primary school math teaching, Chen (2023b) reported that teachers adapted their assessment practices to meet the evolving demands from emergency remote teaching by pursuing professional learning in their daily activities while serving as pedagogical, social, managerial, and technical roles. They demonstrated agency by evolving their instructional methods in virtual contexts, thereby fostering both their own autonomy and that of their students through remote classroom activities. Similarly, Cooper et al. (2022) advocated for addressing the equity gap in implementing authentic formative assessments, emphasizing a focus on in­ dividual student progress and well-being instead of making comparisons across larger groups of students.

In sum, recent studies on CBA practices during the pandemic highlight L2 teachers ' strategic responses to personal, contextual, and cultural challenges in promoting student learning. However, research on teacher agency in the context of ICT-afforded formative language assessment during the return to f2f classes is still limited, calling for the reconceptualization of the ecology of teacher agency and ICT affordances in CBA contexts after the pandemic (Chen, 2022; 2023a). Furthermore, the majority of studies on classroom-based language assessment during the pandemic focused on secondary or higher education, neglecting young learner contexts (Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023; Yan et al., 2021). To address these gaps, this study investigated how two primary school English teachers exerted their agency to implement ICT-afforded CBA in response to challenges during the return to f2f classes from online teaching. The investi­ gation of teacher agency enactment in ICT-afforded CBA concentrated on their experiences within the practical-evaluative dimensions, recognizing it as the pivotal hub for teacher agency. In other words, we explored how the teachers evaluated the opportunities and challenges of ICT affordances in the present f2f CBA contexts by considering relational and contextual factors. Finally, we explored how temporal elements within the iterational and projective dimensions influenced the teachers ' agency enactment (Ashton, 2022; Jeon et al., 2022b; Priestley et al., 2015). The study is guided by the following research questions (RQs).

1. How did two primary school English teachers exert their agency to notice and utilize ICT affordances for CBA in f2f teaching contexts? 2. What factors mediated their agency achievement?

3. Method

This study adopts a case study approach to explore the agency achievement of two primary school English teachers in South Korea, consistent with the majority of prior studies on teacher agency (e.g., Ashton, 2022; Jeon et al., 2022a; Chen, 2022; Kitade, 2015). Case studies have been a dominant approach to examining teacher agency as they can provide a detailed understanding of complex phe­ nomena in real-life educational contexts (Ashton, 2022). Ethnographic case studies have been considered necessary to investigate how teachers and learners exert their agency in CALL contexts (Lafford, 2009), as this approach is useful for providing a thick description of individual teachers ' pedagogical decisions regarding ICT affordances (Liu & Chao, 2018).

3.1. Context and participants

The seventh national education curriculum of South Korea, implemented in 2001, aimed to promote daily conversation skills and develop students ' communicative competence through ongoing formative assessment, not imposing any exam-related pressure (Butler, 2009). However, the actual implementation of this policy was left to teachers without clear guidelines for its practice (B. C. Lee, 2021). The discussion of teacher agency is situated in this particular context.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic outbreak in 2020, South Korea shifted all classes to emergency remote teaching, with students accessing learning resources from home. The MOE provided teachers with Edunet, learning management system (LMS) for uploading teaching materials and assessing student achievement. However, schools resumed f2f classes in the fall of 2021, with over 70% of students fully vaccinated against COVID-19. Therefore, teachers who conducted remote teaching were encouraged to reexamine their CBA practices in f2f situations.

Against this backdrop, we contacted nine candidates in Seoul based on their qualifications and experiences. We first conducted interviews with these candidates to gather their background information and assess their suitability for this study. Subsequently, we selected two teachers from the pool based on their expressed willingness to participate in the research, including on-site observations and interviews, as well as their ability to offer both similar and contrasting perspectives (see Table 1). Specifically, they shared commonalities in their professional backgrounds, as both taught English to elementary school students in Seoul and accumulated over S. Lee and J. Jeon

400 contact hours of online and face-to-face teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic. Their class sizes were comparable, and all students were native Korean speakers. However, differences in their teaching experiences were also notable. Sue, pursuing a doctoral degree in English education, had seven years of teaching experience and four years of implementing ICT-based CBA, both before and during the pandemic. In contrast, Jimin, holding a bachelor ' s degree in elementary education, had four years of teaching experience in primary education and two years of engaging in ICT-based CBA exclusively during the pandemic. The diversity in their backgrounds was further reflected in the characteristics and socioeconomic statuses of their students. Sue taught in School District A in Seoul, where students ranged from beginner to lower intermediate English proficiency levels and came from low to mid-income families on average. Meanwhile, Jimin taught in School District B, with students of high English proficiency levels from high-income families. Table 1 provides detailed background information for each participant.

3.2. Data collection

Data collection was carried out over a five-month period in 2022. To address our RQs, we employed multiple sources of data and triangulation to ensure the validity of our qualitative research through data convergence (D ¨ ornyei, 2007; Glesne, 2021). First, we conducted initial interviews with the participants to gain an understanding of their backgrounds, including their teaching experiences, philosophy on teaching, CBA, and PD. Then, we conducted two on-site non-participant observations to examine how the two EFL teachers exercised their agency with technology to implement CBA, tackle challenges from in-class language activities, and foster student learning with feedback (Davison & Leung, 2009; Fazel & Ali, 2022; Tian et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021). Our observations followed Lyon et al. ' s (2020) High-Impact Classroom Assessment Practices protocol (HI-CAP), which provided validated observation items based on a review of classroom-based assessment (see Appendix A for details). This protocol covers three dimensions of CBA-related observation: Structures that Facilitate Teaching and Learning, Iterative Content Development, and Activating Students. The first domain observes teacher preparation for CBA, including Preparation and Planning, and Learning Targets. The second domain observes a specific content area of CBA practices, encompassing Content Development, Effective Questioning, and Use of Evidence. The last domain observes teacher-initiated opportunities for student participation in CBA, with Student Involvement and Student Engagement. Notably, we integrated Technology Enhanced Instruction across all dimensions, focusing on how teachers employed technology in CBA practices. During observations, one author took descriptive field notes documenting observed actions, concen­ trating on how teachers implemented CBA with technology by exercising their agency.

The two participants were asked to write a reflection paper in Korean or English, focusing on their agency achievement regarding CBA practices, specifically their use of technology in online and f2f teaching during the pandemic. Following the completion of the reflection papers, two subsequent interviews (56 - 72 minutes each) were conducted. The second interview involved follow-up queries about the classroom observations, focusing on their intentions and effectiveness in using technology for CBA. This interview also explored their reflections on the three dimensions of teacher agency related to ICT tool usage for CBA during the pandemic (Priestley et al., 2015; Tian et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021). The third interview explored diverse factors mediating their agency (Zou et al., 2021). See Appendix B for sample interview questions. All interviews were conducted, transcribed in Korean, and then translated into English.

3.3. Data analysis

We used reflexive thematic analysis (TA) to identify themes from the qualitative data, acknowledging the reflexive and subjective nature of qualitative research (Braun & Clarke, 2021). To maintain rigor in TA, we combined ' the deductive a prior ' codebook method with ' the data-driven inductive approach ' across three main steps of coding (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006, pp. 82 - 83). First, we S. Lee and J. Jeon applied Codebook TA as a deductive method, involving a structured coding approach (Braun & Clarke, 2021): based on the two RQs and theoretical concepts on the ecological view on teacher agency and ICT affordances for CBA, which encompass three dimensions of teacher agency, ICT use in different teaching contexts, and influencing factors on agency enactment, we developed a code template that features four broad themes and ten codes (see Appendix C). Following this, we summarized the transcribed data and selected representative excerpts that highlight the key points brought up by the participants in relation to interview questions. These inductive codes were then aligned and merged with the four broad themes to refine and expand the preliminary codes (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). In the second step, we delved into data extracts and memos linked to these codes, identifying four main themes pertinent to the two RQs, such as participants ' agency enactment using ICT tools in CBA implementation within f2f contexts and the mediating factors influencing their agency. Finally, we employed the constant comparative method to finalize the themes and codes (Glaser & Strauss, 1999), iteratively comparing and interpreting all relevant excerpts and memos for each code, and integrating these into the final thematic and coding structure. We integrated TA results into the presentation of a multiple case study, as TA can present salient patterns across data from two different participants (Braun & Clarke, 2021). We identified and described similarities and differences in agency enactment by comparing codes across the two cases (Ashton, 2022).

In ensuring the trustworthiness of this study, we used a few rigorous methods. First, to control for extraneous variables such as interview dates, different observers, and environmental factors, a standard protocol was adhered to across all data collection points. Specifically, interviews were conducted in similar settings (see Fig. 3), and observation guidelines, along with reflection journal protocols, were strictly followed to maintain consistency (see Appendix A). Second, member checking was utilized as a crucial component to ensure participant validation (Braun & Clarke, 2021): each participant was given two weeks to review and comment on the data, including observations, interview transcripts, and reflection papers. Finally, to ensure inter-rater reliability, the two authors independently coded the data content and then compared their coding results, achieving an initial Cohen ' s Kappa coefficient of 0.82 for all three data sources, which indicates substantial agreement. Discrepancies in their coding were meticulously resolved through consensus meetings, where differing opinions were discussed and reconciled by referring back to the original data and the established coding schemes.

4. Findings

Corresponding to the two RQs, we first identified diverse ways that the two participant teachers exercised their agency in the present, known as the practical-evaluative dimension, when responding to CBA opportunities and challenges in f2f contexts. In addition, to address the second RQ, we elaborated on different factors influencing teacher agency enactment, including relational and contextual factors in current contexts and two dimensions - the iterational and projective dimensions. 4.1. Focus of CBA in f2f contexts

When English teachers transitioned back to in-person teaching from remote online instruction in mid-2021, they faced diverse challenges and opportunities in implementing CBA for young students, who were already used to online L2 tasks but lacked experience in physical classroom environments. A primary concern for teachers was how to shift the focus of CBA to suit f2f contexts upon returning from an online L2 assessment mode. In adapting CBA to fit f2f teaching methods, the participant teachers considered the English teaching curriculum prescribed by MOE. Sue noted the MOE ' s guidelines on CBA in primary education as presenting both opportunities and limitations. 5. Discussion and implications

The study explored the ways in which two primary school English teachers exerted their agency to notice and utilize ICT affor­ dances for CBA in f2f contexts. It further investigated diverse factors for teacher agency from two temporal aspects, namely the iterational and projective dimensions, as well as relational/contextual factors in the current contexts of teacher agency. Grounded in the ecological perspective on teacher agency (Ashton, 2022; Priestley et al., 2015), the findings presented similarities and differences between the two primary school English teachers in terms of how they responded to challenges identified during the return to f2f classes by exercising their agency. In particular, the ecological approach revealed the multidimensional aspects of their agency in specific temporal-relational teaching contexts (Chen, 2022; Priestley et al., 2015; Tao & Gao, 2021).

First, for RQ1, the study shows that teacher agency is manifested in recognizing and adopting ICT affordances in unique f2f CBA contexts (Chen, 2023a; Moorhouse & Kohnke, 2023). Unlike previous studies that focused on phenomenological strategies of L2 teachers in classroom-based language assessment during pedagogical disruptions (e.g., Fazel & Ali, 2022; Yan et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021), this study offers a ' concept-oriented analysis ' of teachers ' creative and resilient adaptation to CBA opportunities and challenges when returning to f2f classes (Chen, 2022, p. 5). Specifically, it identified four ways in which the participant teachers exerted their professional capacity within the ecology of f2f CBA settings, including ICT affordances, CBA goals, school attitudes toward formative assessment, teacher-student interactions, students ' emotional experiences, parental influences on CBA, and institutional support for teacher PD. This ecological perspective highlights teacher agency as not just an individual, functional capacity, but a contextual and social capability facilitating interactions between L2 teaching agents and their social environments (Levine, 2020; Swanson & Levine, 2020).

In addition, this study highlights the dynamic nature of the practical-evaluative dimension, where teacher agency is actively exercised in relation to CBA affordances and constraints in current contexts (Ashton, 2022; Jeon et al., 2022b; Priestley et al., 2015). In this study, facing a focus on summative assessment, Sue chose to enhance student learning by leveraging the practical benefits of AI apps for formative assessment. In contrast, Jimin, aligning with his school ' s exam-centric approach, restricted his ICT use to a basic application of the LMS, giving precedence to objective evaluation over formative assessment. This reflects the dynamic interplay between teacher agency and ICT affordances in CBA, shaped by teachers ' ongoing reflections on the use of ICT for L2 instruction in f2f settings (Ashton, 2022; Chen, 2022, 2023a).

Regarding RQ2, the two participants ' different approaches to utilizing ICT affordances for CBA were found to be influenced by diverse mediating factors. First, their individual differences were linked to the iterational and the projective dimensions in a temporal aspect of their teacher agency, as well as their investment and identity (Priestley et al., 2015; Tao & Gao, 2021; White, 2018). Although both teachers were prompted by the COVID-19 pandemic to leverage ICT affordances for CBA in online teaching, upon returning to f2f classes, their approaches diverged: Sue continued using technologies for CBA as a tech-savvy teacher, while Jimin reverted to traditional methods or ' old school ' , using technologies minimally to increase students ' hands-on experiences in CBA tasks. This suggests that increased technology reliance for CBA during the pandemic might not persist post-pandemic, with the outcomes influenced by individual teachers ' past experiences and future goals regarding technology use. Therefore, the temporal aspect of their teacher agency enactment revealed how past teaching experiences, pedagogical beliefs, and future goals shaped their agency enact­ ment in f2f classes (Ashton, 2022; Chen, 2022, 2023b).

The findings further illustrated the relational, contextual factors in the present that influence teaching agency enactment in relation to ICT affordances in f2f CBA (Priestley et al., 2015; Tao & Gao, 2021; White, 2018). Concerning the cultural dimension, the findings indicate the significant impact of institutional expectation on teacher agency on CBA in two different CBA contexts (Ashton, 2022; Fazel & Ali, 2022; Tian et al., 2021). Their reflections suggest that their agency enactments against the disruptions to CBA during the COVID-19 pandemic were manifested in a different manner. For example, Sue adopted diverse technologies for formative assessment to fulfill the MOE curriculum emphasizing communication skills, while Jimin used LMS minimally for test-oriented evaluation. This divergence seems tied to the different levels of control allowed in their respective districts. Sue ' s school in School District A encouraged greater autonomy in CBA, while Jimin ' s School District B explicitly required digital data collection for objective evaluation. This shows that the specific CBA culture of local school districts allows language teachers to reflect on the complexity of school education beyond the classroom, enabling them to explore diverse CBA affordances at the institutional level (Chen, 2023a; Levine, 2020).

As for the structural dimension, the participants ' reflections specified the noticeable influence of parental voices over their agentic behaviors in two different CBA contexts. Parents in School District A were reported to support Sue ' s CBA practices oriented toward formative assessment, which encouraged her to digitize students ' English skills using ICT tools for assessment-related decision-making. In contrast, parental voices in Jimin ' s school context were interpreted as great pressure on his evaluation policy and ICT use in f2f classes. This resulted in his limited use of ICT tools for formative assessment and his return to traditional methods to increase student rapport and interaction. It is suggested that teachers ' consciousness of social pressure from parents, particularly in terms of CBA accountability, may operate as either a facilitating or a constraining factor on their teacher agency (Butler, 2009; Fazel & Ali, 2022). Therefore, the power dynamics between teachers, learners, and parents in different school contexts mediate teacher agency as one of the structural factors in diverse CBA situations (Ashton, 2022).

S. Lee and J. Jeon

In relation to the material dimension, the findings indicate that a lack of teacher training from the MOE may discourage teachers from developing their technological skills despite its support for ICT (Fazel & Ali, 2022; Lan & Fan, 2019). Sue acknowledged that her initial low self-efficacy in technological knowledge was partly due to a lack of teacher-training support. However, she responded to this challenge in a constructive way by investing in her PD. She reflected that the school supported her pursuit of this PD when she served as a director of the technology and information department of the school. This indicates that institutional support for teacher training programs and PD is a necessary condition for teachers to invest in developing their ICT skills for PD (Moorhouse, 2023b).

Finally, the way both teachers applied their agency in ICT-afforded CBA was influenced by young students ' emotional experiences with f2f CBA practices. Teachers ' use of ICT in CBA often hinges on their managerial and pedagogical interactions with students (Chen, 2023b). Facing students ' wellness issues upon returning to in-person classes, Sue aimed to boost their motivation and engagement in assessment tasks by fostering a collaborative, immersive learning environment. In contrast, Jimin prioritized enhancing physical interaction to strengthen their rapport. This indicates that focusing on students ' individual progress and emotional well-being is crucial for authentic CBA implementation, as it promotes students ' optimal performance (Cooper et al., 2022).

Some theoretical and practical implications can be suggested from the ecological view on teacher agency. Theoretically, given that the relationship between teacher agency and ICT affordance is one of the emerging topics in CALL, this study contributes to expanding the ecological perspective in research on ICT-afforded CBA. This integration can enhance our understanding of ' their joint contri­ butions to L2 classroom instruction ' in the post COVID-19 pandemic era, as technology normalization becomes increasingly relevant (Chen, 2022, p. 2). Second, the study emphasizes the need to address diverse contextual, cultural, and relational factors influencing teacher agency in evaluating ICT affordances in CBA. Future research should thus investigate the mediation of teacher agency by individual and social factors, especially the dynamic interplay between teacher agency and ICT affordances for CBA post-pandemic (Ashton, 2022; Fazel & Ali, 2022). Practically, technology acceptance for CBA transcends individual level and necessitates exploring the relationship between structural factors and teacher agency, particularly within PD centered on ICT skills (Ashton, 2022; Chen, 2022; Kitade, 2015). Therefore, we advocate for institutional support in teacher training to foster collective agency within local schools. Finally, particularly in primary English education, where learning-oriented CBA is crucial for cognitive and linguistic development, supporting teachers ' agentic decision-making is crucial (Butler, 2009; Yan et al., 2021). Implementing AfL with young learners can narrow the equity gap and support student well-being in what are often complex and challenging assessment settings (Cooper et al., 2022).

6. Conclusion and limitations

This study examined how two primary school English teachers enacted their agency in implementing CBA through ICT affordances upon returning to f2f classes. It further delved into diverse factors influencing teacher agency, drawing on the ecological view of teacher agency and ICT-based CBA. The study reported four unique themes regarding the interplay between teacher agency and ICT affordances for CBA, as well as diverse temporal and relational/contextual factors mediating teacher agency. It further showed that individual differences emerged regarding their agency enactment in adopting ICT affordances for CBA upon returning to face-to-face classes. These variations were interpreted through individual factors (e.g., pedagogical beliefs, investment, identity, and future goals) and contextual ones (e.g., cultural, structural, and material dimensions), demonstrating the multidimensionality of teacher agency in specific temporal-relational contexts of ICT-afforded CBA (Ashton, 2022; Priestley et al., 2015).

There are several limitations to this study. First, the case-study approach may limit the generalizability of the results. Hence, future research should include participants from diverse contexts to broaden the findings. Next, this study did not investigate the direct causal relationship between mediating factors, the exercise of teacher agency, and the realization of ICT affordances for CBA, as depicted in Fig. 2. To address this issue, future research could adopt an inferential approach to explore these sequential paths.
