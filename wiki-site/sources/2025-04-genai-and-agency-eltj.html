Introduction

Generative AI (GenAI) and preservice teacher agency in ELT

Seongyong Lee, Jaeho Jeon, and Hohsung Choe

Despite abundant research on the pedagogical benefits of generative AI (GenAI) in ELT, teachers' agentive use of GenAI in an instructional context has not been fully explored. How pre-service teachers utilize GenAI in teacher-training courses also remains unclear. Thus, grounded in the framework of teacher agency and professional development, this study explored pre-service English teachers' perceptions of engaging in a GenAI-enhanced lesson design project. Thematic analysis of interviews and reflection papers from eighteen pre-service English teachers who participated in lesson design with diverse GenAI tools revealed their agentive use of GenAI in two aspects: (1) agentive role in teacher-GenAI collaboration; (2) responsible GenAI use to address its constraints. The findings highlight the importance of pre-service teacher agency in leveraging GenAI for ELT. Pedagogical implications for training pre-service teachers in GenAI literacy are discussed.

Key words: generative AI (GenAI), ChatGPT, pre-service teacher, teacher agency

Recent research on artificial intelligence (AI) tools has examined the pedagogical benefits of using generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in ELT, highlighting both its benefits and challenges as perceived by L2 teachers. One core advantage is its capacity to provide immediate, customized feedback on students' work, thereby complementing teacher feedback and supporting language development (Guo and Wang 2024). GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude, can also facilitate personalized learning by adapting to individual student needs and creating engaging activities (Ahn, Lee, and Son 2024; Moorhouse and Kohnke 2024). However, pedagogical challenges of GenAI usage remain, such as concerns about content quality and over-reliance on GenAI tools (Jeon and Lee 2023; Mohamed 2024).

Research on teachers' perceptions has displayed their mixed attitudes toward integrating GenAI tools into L2 education. On the one hand, GenAI is seen as supportive in reducing teachers' workload, designing lesson plans, developing teaching materials, and implementing interactive L2 activities (Bower et al. 2024; Choi et al. 2024). On the other hand, teachers express concerns about ethical issues, including plagiarism, academic dishonesty, privacy violations, and assessment disruption (Jeon and Lee 2023; Zhu et al. 2023). Given these promising yet skeptical views from teachers, clear guidelines should be implemented to protect personal data, promote responsible use, and support their critical decision-making regarding GenAI use in ELT (Guo and Wang 2024).

Several studies have focused on L2 teachers' agentive roles in using GenAI for ELT. Jeon and Lee (2023) explored the collaborative relationships between English teachers and ChatGPT and emphasized their active involvement in maximizing its pedagogical benefits. Rather than relying solely on ChatGPT, teachers' crafting and decision-making on its outputs, informed by their pedagogical knowledge, are crucial to achieving optimal educational outcomes. Guo and Wang (2024), in their study on EFL teachers' perceptions of ChatGPT as a writing tutor, highlight that ChatGPT's feedback should be complemented by teacher feedback to facilitate comprehensive L2 learning. Therefore, it is essential that teachers be equipped with GenAI literacy, focusing on how to orchestrate diverse GenAI capabilities while addressing its issues (Zhu et al. 2023; Ahn, Lee, and Son 2024).

However, several gaps remain regarding GenAI usage for ELT from teachers' perspectives. First, despite abundant attention to GenAI benefits and constraints, previous research has not fully investigated teacher agency in exploiting GenAI affordances. Teacher agency, defined as the teacher capacity to navigate educational chances and constraints in teaching practices (White 2018), is crucial for ELT, as educational situations constantly evolve (Chen 2022). This requires teachers to enact their agency by utilizing locally available affordances-social, relational, and technological-to promote student learning (Jeon, Lee, and Choe 2022). Second, although several studies have noted the usefulness of GenAI in teacher training (Guo and Wang 2024; Moorhouse 2024), preservice teachers' professional development has not been a primary focus, particularly regarding how GenAI can support their instructional practices. Research indicates that junior teachers often lack the full knowledge to use GenAI tools effectively in specific teaching contexts (Moorhouse 2024). To address this concern, it is vital to develop pre-service teachers' digital competence through continuous learning, including hands-on training in lesson planning, collaborative task design, and practical workshops (Crosthwaite, Luciana, and Wijaya 2023; Moorhouse and Kohnke 2024).

Therefore, inspired by the importance of teacher agency and pre-service teacher training regarding GenAI, this study explored pre-service English teachers' perceptions of using GenAI tools in lesson design projects. The following research question guided this study:

How did pre-service teachers exercise agency in utilizing GenAI tools for lesson design?

Eighteen pre-service teachers in a Korean university participated in this study (seven males and eleven females, aged 21-24). We used purposeful sampling to select participants with similar backgrounds. All participants were Year 3 students from the English Education department, had similar university entrance exam scores, and reported intermediate to advanced English proficiency. They also gained a similar level of pedagogical and content knowledge of English from their teacher-training program. The participants reported that they had not previously used GenAI tools for academic tasks, including lesson design. We advertised the current study to Year 3 students who had taken diverse teacher-training courses, and finally eighteen students voluntarily joined the study. With research ethics approval, we followed the ethics guidelines that address participant consent for participation and publication, information sharing, and data management.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups and conducted a fourweek GenAI-based ELT lesson design project in three stages: introduction, lesson design, and final outputs (Figure 1). In Week 1, they learned how to use GenAI tools, including ChatGPT, Copilot, and DALL-E3, and discussed the use of GenAI in ELT. From Week 2 to Week 4, they designed lesson plans, developed ELT materials, and created teacher-student scenarios using the tools. In the final stage, they refined their outputs, engaged in group discussions, and shared peer feedback. After all stages were over, we interviewed all participants in Korean (42-55 minutes for each group), addressing their perceptions of using GenAI tools. Interviews were audiorecorded, transcribed, and translated into English. Then, they wrote a reflection paper in English ( n = 18, a total of 9,096 words, mean = 505.3 words, standard deviation [SD] = 191.3 words) regarding their experiences of utilizing GenAI tools for lesson design. Appendix 1 provides sample interview questions and reflection prompts.

The participants reflected that their iterative interactions with GenAI tools through repeated prompting and editing work enhanced their GenAI literacy; that is, their perceptions and effective use of GenAI affordances developed through their collaboration with GenAI tools. This ongoing engagement with GenAI not only developed their technical skills but also boosted their self-efficacy. Their reflections further showed that their pedagogical knowledge, as well as their improved technological skills, contributed to refining GenAI outputs for use in lesson planning:

As I worked more with ChatGPT, I found that my skills get better and better over time. It's like the more I refined prompts to ChatGPT outputs, the more likely I'm to create useful lesson content, which in turn made me feel more confident in my tech skills. (Interview, Participant 5)

My skills to use ChatGPT have been improved, but I believe the ELT knowledge is also so important to get refined results, because it can make the outputs well-suited for my lessons. (Interview, Participant 15)

In addition, many participants ( n = 11) recognized their agentive roles in adapting GenAI outputs through final decision-making. They often found that these outputs were not sufficiently tailored to meet students' needs, primarily due to GenAI's limitations in generating highly personalized and contextualized materials. As demonstrated in the following example, the participants consistently adapted the generated materials in three aspects-selection, supplementation, and revision-drawing on their pedagogical and content knowledge. They found this final stage necessary, because even when using their own data as a reference, the GenAI outputs often failed to meet their specific expectations:

I always adapted the materials made with things like ChatGPT and DALL-E3 for my lesson plan, because I couldn't believe these things were directly usable for students. For this, I often felt that I needed to use more of my teaching knowledge from ELT courses to select, supplement, and revise the content. (Interview, Participant 12)

Finally, some participants ( n = 6) identified group work as providing peer scaffolding in developing the technological skills needed for GenAIenhanced lesson planning. In fact, personal innovativeness-referring to individual differences in GenAI literacy in this study-was identified as an important factor in obtaining quality outputs from GenAI. Some participants experienced challenges in composing effective prompts for GenAI, which made it difficult to achieve their desired results. To compensate for this personal lack of technological skills, they chose to utilize group collaboration as scaffolding to generate quality outputs from GenAI tools:

While working on lesson plans with GenAI, I sometimes struggled to get the exact results I wanted because I believe I wasn't as familiar with GenAI as my classmates were. Then, I chose to collaborate with other classmates to form a group. I could depend on them to refine the prompts and improve the outputs. (Interview, Participant 8)

The participants noted both technological (for example, content quality, lack of contextual or personalized output, misinformation) and pedagogical constraints (for example, academic dishonesty, hindrance of critical and creative thinking, over-reliance) while engaging in GenAI-enhanced lesson design. To address GenAI's technological limitations, the participants first ( n = 6) sought external sources of information to validate GenAI outputs. Specifically, when they were not satisfied with the lesson materials crafted with GenAI assistance, including teacher talks, lesson objectives, in-class activities, and assessment tasks, they decided to adopt multiple sources beyond GenAI tools for comparison and validation, as described in the following extract:

When I wasn't satisfied with the lesson plans, I cross-checked the content with other reliable sources for accuracy. For example, when I created an essay evaluation tool using My GPT, I couldn't fully trust the results due to previous irrelevant outputs, so I'd verify them with ELT textbooks or Google. (Reflection paper, Participant 3)

To obtain high-quality outputs from GenAI, some participants ( n = 5) pursued informal professional learning to develop their GenAI literacy beyond formal teacher-training courses. In lesson planning, they looked to social media, internet sources, and human sources to learn to operate GenAI tools more effectively and obtain better outcomes. Furthermore, they highlighted the need for teacher-training programs at the institutional level to focus on GenAI use for instructional design. The following extracts illustrate this aspect:

To improve my skills to use GenAI tools, I often found online forums and social media groups where other teachers shared tips and strategies. For example, I found a tutorial on YouTube that helped me refine my prompts, and then I could create better lesson plans. (Interview, Participant 13)

Despite my personal efforts (to seek informal learning sources), I realized they weren't enough to fully improve my GenAI usage skills. So, I believe that GenAI training courses are necessary at the university to equip student teachers with these skills. (Interview, Participant 1).

Regarding the pedagogical constraints of GenAI, a few participants ( n = 3) developed guidelines for students' ethical use of GenAI tools in their future classrooms. They believed that it is crucial to promote students' GenAI literacy that focuses on the responsible and mindful use of the tools. In addition, the participants ( n = 4) enhanced creative and hands-on in-class activities to foster students' creative and critical thinking, and helped them reduce over-reliance on the tools, as evident in the following extracts:

I made some guidelines to help my students use GenAI responsibly. In the guidelines, I emphasized the importance of using these tools as a support. (Reflection paper, Participant 6)

I designed some in-class activities in which students brainstormed ideas on their own before using GenAI for their final work. I think these activities could help them develop critical thinking skills. It would be also useful for them not to heavily depend on GenAI tools. (Reflection paper, Participant 5)

Unlike previous studies that have primarily focused on GenAI's functions and benefits, this study centered on pre-service English teachers' agentive roles, specifically their exercise of agency in utilizing GenAI affordances and addressing its constraints for ELT in lesson design. The findings showed that the pre-service teachers evaluated, selected, and orchestrated the affordances of GenAI tools for ELT, rather than simply accepting their functionalities (Chen 2022; Jeon and Lee 2023). For pre-service teachers, who lack actual teaching opportunities and have limited experience with technology-enhanced teaching, GenAI may create new educational situations by presenting both opportunities and challenges (Moorhouse 2024). Such changes in the educational landscape may necessitate teachers' active involvement in securing its pedagogical benefits to facilitate student learning (Jeon and Lee 2023). In this process, teacher agency is manifested in identifying and adopting diverse affordancesenvironmental, social, and technological-to achieve their pedagogical goals in these new educational contexts (White 2018; Jeon, Lee, and Choe 2022). The participants in this study exercised their agency by adopting the different affordances of GenAI tools (for example, content generation, feedback provision, and assessment) by constantly fine-tuning their prompts for GenAI, evaluating and adapting GenAI outputs, and ultimately crafting their own teaching materials using those outputs. This finding suggests that research on GenAI in ELT should address teachers' agentive roles in collaborating with GenAI tools, rather than focusing solely on GenAI capabilities (White 2018; Jeon and Lee 2023).

In addition, the findings indicate that pre-service teachers' agency enactment in harnessing GenAI tools for enhanced educational outcomes requires an ongoing negotiation of roles and responsibilities between humans and GenAI (Ahn, Lee, and Son 2024). This evolving collaboration between pre-service teachers and GenAI represents a shift toward a co-dependent relationship, where teachers increasingly integrate GenAI into their pedagogical decision-making processes, such as designing lessons, crafting objectives, and assessing student work (Choi et al. 2024). Participants in this study demonstrated a strategic and active use of GenAI, taking on the role of co-creators in the educational process. Therefore, the interplay between pre-service teacher agency and teacher-GenAI collaboration is multifaceted, as it involves not only the evolving dynamics of teacher-GenAI partnerships but also the empowerment of teachers to effectively use the tools (Jeon and Lee 2023). To facilitate successful teacher-GenAI collaboration, pre-service teachers must be equipped with the necessary skills and knowledge to exercise control over how GenAI is incorporated into their pedagogical strategies (Moorhouse 2024).

The findings further highlight the importance of pre-service teachers' subject-specific pedagogical knowledge for the successful exercise of agency in orchestrating GenAI affordances for ELT (Jeon and Lee 2023; Ahn, Lee, and Son 2024). The literature confirms that teachers' pedagogical knowledge alongside technological knowledge is of primary basis for the association between teacher agency and ICT affordances (Chen 2022; Jeon, Lee, and Choe 2022). Specifically, in the GenAI use context, the enactment of teacher agency is based on their evaluation of whether GenAI affordances are aligned with their pedagogical goals and beliefs,

Finally, the findings indicate that teacher education programs should offer appropriate training courses that empower pre-service teachers to critically and responsibly use GenAI tools (Jeon, Lee, and Choe 2022). Specifically, pre-service teachers need to be educated in ways that enable them to take the initiative in exploiting diverse GenAI affordances and addressing its constraints to fulfill their pedagogical goals in future classrooms (Crosthwaite, Luciana, and Wijaya 2023; Jeon and Lee 2023; Choi et al. 2024). Some participants in this study emphasized the importance of their knowledge gained from ELT courses in effectively integrating GenAI tools in lesson design, while others pursued informal professional development by developing their GenAI literacy beyond formal teacher-training courses. Therefore, future research should explore how teacher-training programs can support pre-service teachers in developing GenAI literacy that includes handling GenAI affordances and constraints in align with subject-specific pedagogical knowledge (Moorhouse 2024).

This study explored the agency of pre-service English teachers in utilizing GenAI tools for lesson design. The findings highlight the participants' active roles in teacher-GenAI collaboration and their responsible use of GenAI to address its limitations. The study has some limitations. Due to its qualitative approach, the findings cannot be generalized across different contexts. In particular, the potential association between participants' ELT knowledge and their agency enactment requires further investigation through quasiexperimental methods. Second, since the study focused on pre-service teachers' perceptions, it did not explicitly capture their behavioral and descriptive interaction with GenAI. Future studies could adopt diverse data sources, such as lesson design artifacts, discussions, field notes, and video recordings, to better document their agentive use of GenAI.
