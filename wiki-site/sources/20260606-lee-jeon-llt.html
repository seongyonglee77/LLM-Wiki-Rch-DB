A RTICLE

Abstract

Although essential for teachers' professional development, developing content knowledge (CK) remains a challenge in pre-service language teacher education, where applied linguistics concepts are often perceived as disconnected from practice. While research on technology integration emphasizes pedagogical or technological knowledge (TK), little is known about how emerging tools can mediate CK development. Thus, this study implemented a design-based learning project: 23 pre-service English teachers used generative AI (GenAI) and the metaverse to design their brands and 3D showrooms, applying applied linguistics concepts. Reflective journals and semi-structured interviews were analyzed through thematic analysis to explore how CK developed through TK and learner agency. The findings show that their CK was enhanced when applied linguistics knowledge was used to craft brands and showrooms through iterative design cycles. Agency mediated this process, as they calibrated the outputs and collaborated through distributed agency, using GenAI and metaverse affordances. Finally, reflective inquiry enhanced critical AI literacy, which supported CK development by prompting participants to work on GenAI-mediated decisions (e.g., reliability, bias, authorship, and inclusion) while designing their artefacts. The study discusses theoretical implications for affordance-agency mechanisms in technologymediated learning and pedagogical implications for integrating GenAI-metaverse design and critical AI literacy in teacher education.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, professional development in teacher education, particularly the integration of technology, has been a central topic in educational research (Celik, 2023; Tondeur et al., 2025). More recently, with the introduction of Generative AI (GenAI), 3D metaverse platforms, and virtual reality (VR), design-based active learning has gained increasing emphasis in teacher preparation programs (Hwang et al., 2025; Lee & Kim, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Yet, while these innovations have broadened opportunities for experiential learning, they also foreground the notable challenge of developing disciplinary content knowledge (CK) in language teacher education, where bridging theory and practice remains particularly difficult (Bartels, 2005). Although advanced technologies are now widely adopted, much of the previous work has focused on teachers' technological and pedagogical learning through lesson planning, materials design, and TPACK-oriented training (e.g., Lee & Jeon, 2024; Lee & Kim, 2025). At the same time, some L2 teacher education research has examined how teachers' CK can be strengthened through practice-based pedagogies, including reflective second language acquisition (SLA) coursework and problem-based design tasks (Bartels, 2005; Beshah & Anshu, 2024). However, the extent

Gaining CK is a fundamental requirement for pre-service teachers' professional growth, as it underpins the subject-specific knowledge essential for effective pedagogy (Beshah & Anshu, 2024). In L2 education, CK spans applied linguistics domains such as educational linguistics, translanguaging, discourse analysis, and L2 acquisition (Bartels, 2005). Thus, L2 teacher training programs, especially those preparing language teaching majors, should prioritize CK development from the outset (Ton-Nu & Nguyen, 2024). Yet research shows that pre-service teachers struggle with CK due to limited engagement in teaching, perceived irrelevance to practice, and theory-centered lectures (Bartels, 2005; Beshah & Anshu, 2024).

This study addresses this challenge by examining how GenAI and metaverse technologies can be integrated to support pre-service English teachers' CK development. Although AI and immersive platforms are increasingly used in teacher education for their pedagogical affordances (Moorhouse et al., 2024), only some studies have addressed CK development as a research focus, typically by examining how pre-service teachers gain disciplinary knowledge through experiential learning (e.g., Beshah & Anshu, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Even in these CK-oriented studies, emerging technologies are positioned as support tools rather than as a mediating means through which learners develop applied linguistics knowledge through agency enactment in design work (Lee & Jeon, 2024). Thus, this study investigates pre-service teachers' CK development as enacted in design work, specifically asking how GenAI and metaverse affordances and agency work on the internalization of applied-linguistics knowledge.

Literature Review

Pre-service Language Teacher Training with Technology

Professional development in teacher education, particularly technology integration, has been a central issue in educational research (Tondeur et al., 2012, 2025). Over the past decades, teacher education has been reconceptualized by technological innovations, prompting new approaches to preparing pre-service teachers (Jeon et al., 2022; Hwang et al., 2025). Early studies emphasized training methods to strengthen teachers' technological pedagogical knowledge (TPK) and classroom readiness through tasks, such as lesson planning and materials development (Chen, 2023; Jeon et al., 2022).

Previous research on technology integration in language teaching reveals a tendency to be technocentric (Al-Furaih, 2017; Campbell et al., 2022). Such approaches emphasize the benefits of technology for language teaching (e.g., Moorhouse, 2024; Hwang et al., 2025), while paying insufficient attention to the dynamic interplay among technology, disciplinary content, pedagogy, and context (cf. Bower, 2019; Chen, 2022; Tondeur et al., 2025). This orientation has posed pedagogical challenges by prioritizing technology over its synergistic potential with broader educational objectives in aligning it with pedagogy and content to meet specific language learning goals (Lee & Jeon, 2024). Accordingly, this underscores the need for comprehensive frameworks, such as Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), in pre-service teacher training to guide contextualized technology adoption into subjectspecific pedagogy (Celik, 2023; Tseng, 2018).

Teacher-training models have been developed to integrate technology with pedagogy and subject content in preparing pre-service teachers for technology-enhanced instruction (Al-Furaih, 2017). Specifically, the TPACK framework (Koehler & Mishra, 2009) links technological tools, pedagogical approaches, and disciplinary content, and the Synthesis of Qualitative Evidence (SQD) model offers empirically validated strategies such as modelling, collaborative design, scaffolded practice, and reflection (Tondeur et al., 2012, 2025). Building on these models, design-based active learning engages pre-service teachers in iterative cycles of exploration, design, implementation, and reflection (Campbell et al., 2022; Hwang et al., 2025), strengthening skills in multimodal lesson design, prompt engineering, and AI-assisted feedback, while enhancing pedagogical judgment and the capacity to adapt technology to disciplinary contexts (Jeon et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2022). Thus, these models provide a foundation for cultivating integrated CK, PK, and TK competency for effective technology use in teacher education (Lee & Jeon, 2024; Tai, 2015; Tseng, 2018).

Emerging Technologies for Pre-service Teacher Education

Emerging technologies such as AI and immersive platforms, including VR, are increasingly recognized for their potential to enhance pre-service teachers' technological and pedagogical skills (Lee et al., 2025, 2026; Wu & Chun, 2025). Research shows that AI-supported lesson planning can develop authentic learning environments and strengthen teachers' readiness to integrate technology into classroom practice (Jeon et al., 2022). Similarly, metaverse-based simulations provide situated practice spaces that simulate real teaching contexts, supporting collaborative learning, multimodal task design, TPACK development, and digital literacy (Lee & Wu, 2024; Wu & Chun, 2025). Combined with AI chatbots, immersive environments enable interactive practice and provide feedback while allowing teachers to develop taskoriented design and material adaptation skills (Hwang et al., 2025).

The release of ChatGPT in late 2022 marked a turning point, moving AI from niche innovation to a mainstream pedagogical tool in language education (Jeon & Lee, 2023; Moorhouse et al., 2024). Since then, AI integration has spread across domains, from writing to all language skills and multimodal task design, indicating a shift toward technology normalization (Celik, 2023; Moorhouse, 2024). Thus, both pre-service and in-service teachers increasingly recognize the pedagogical value of AI, thereby adopting it for lesson planning, materials development, and learner engagement (Moorhouse et al., 2024).

Research emphasizes that critical AI literacy should be embedded within pedagogical knowledge that connects AI usage to teaching practice (Lee et al., 2025, 2026; Pei et al., 2025). That is, while GenAI can help design instructional plans, teachers should critically evaluate GenAI affordances and constraints, aligning them with local teaching contexts (Choi et al., 2024). Similarly, GenAI-supported lesson study may enhance pre-service teacher agency, strengthening critical AI literacy and pedagogical knowledge through evaluation and revision of AI-generated materials (Lee et al., 2025). Extending this perspective, Pei et al. (2025) argue that critical AI literacy involving GenAI should include critical evaluation, ethical awareness, and adaptability to diverse scenarios, ensuring that AI use supports intended learning outcomes. These studies caution against viewing GenAI as an autonomous content generator and instead present it as a co-design partner orchestrated by teachers to meet curricular goals and learner needs (Jeon & Lee, 2023).

Research also notes that teacher training needs to cultivate reflective practitioners who address GenAI critically, ethically, and creatively to attain professional growth (Moorhouse et al., 2024; Pei et al., 2025). Accordingly, teacher education programs should integrate critical AI literacy by emphasizing sociotechnical awareness, such as power relations, labor conditions, and environmental impacts, alongside epistemic and ethical evaluation of AI usage (Veldhuis et al., 2025). Regarding the association between teacher agency and GenAI affordances, teachers should be able to critically evaluate both affordances and constraints of GenAI tools at the individual, institutional, and societal levels as they 'recognize the power of GenAI to position them in particular ways' within their teaching contexts (Darvin, 2025, p. 21). Practically, teacher training needs to help cultivate teachers' reflective capacity to assess GenAI outputs for reliability, bias, authorship, and equity (Lee et al., 2025, 2026).

Developing Pre-service Teachers' CK

Teachers should be equipped with subject-specific CK for effective teaching (Beshah & Anshu, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). In applied linguistics, CK includes diverse areas, such as SLA, sociolinguistics, pragmatics, and discourse analysis, among others, that equip pre-service teachers with conceptual and analytical tools for language instruction (Bartels, 2005). Acquiring CK is not only about learning disciplinary knowledge but about building a network of conceptual understanding, professional beliefs, and instructional decision-making (LaFond & Dogancay-Aktuna, 2009). Such integrated CK allows teachers to interpret, adapt, and use subject-specific insights in pedagogically responsive, adaptive ways that address learners' diverse needs (Beshah & Anshu, 2024).

Despite its importance for effective language teaching, developing CK in applied linguistics remains a challenge for pre-service teachers (Bartels, 2005; Zhang et al., 2025). Many struggle to gain theoretical concepts due to low intrinsic interest, and they often question its relevance to classroom practice, perceiving it as abstract or disconnected from their future teaching contexts (Bartels, 2005). Moreover, teacher-training courses frequently adopt theory-oriented lectures rather than hands-on practice, making it difficult to bridge the gap between CK and pedagogical application (Beshah & Anshu, 2024). These barriers can lower pre-service teachers' self-confidence and motivation, reinforcing the sense that disciplinary knowledge is detached from the practical expertise needed for classroom instruction (LaFond & Dogancay-Aktuna, 2009).

To address these challenges, research has explored diverse strategies for developing language teachers' CK. Traditional methods emphasize direct instruction through lectures, textual analysis, essay writing, and small-scale research projects (LaFond & Dogancay-Aktuna, 2009; Ton-Nu & Nguyen, 2024). Participatory approaches, including collaborative activities such as online discussion forums via mobile platforms, support the negotiation of meaning, reflection, and co-construction of CK beyond the classroom (Sandoval-Cruz et al., 2022). Problem-based learning in online or VR environments also shows promise by engaging students in authentic problem-solving that integrates linguistic knowledge with pedagogical strategies (Lee & Kim, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

This review shows that research has explored diverse strategies for developing pre-service teachers' CK; however, it has centered on strengthening applied linguistics CK through experiential coursework and problem-based inquiry in which student teachers are trained to apply CK to authentic tasks (SandovalCruz et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, recent studies highlight the increasing integration of AI and immersive platforms into teacher education, examining teachers' TK and TPACK growth, ICT readiness, and its pedagogical usage (Choi et al., 2024; Moorhouse et al., 2024). The remaining gap is therefore examining how GenAI and metaverse environments together mediate CK through multimodal design work, as well as exploring learner agency enactment in relation to technological affordance (cf., Jeon et al., 2022).

Technology-mediated Learning Theory and its Relevance to CK Development

Technology-mediated learning theory (TMLT) frames technology not as a passive medium but as a mediating tool for learning through affordances, learner agency, and design principles (Bower, 2019). It emphasizes that the interplay of agency and affordances inform students' engagement in disciplinary learning, while design-based active learning projects enable the development of both TK and CK (Chen, 2023; Jeon et al., 2022). Therefore, TMLT provides an appropriate lens for examining technologymediated learning through affordances and learner agency (Bower, 2019). Regarding the use of AI for learning, the student-AI collaboration (SAC) model (Kim et al., 2022) may be suggested as an interpretive, supplementary lens to describe how participants' collaboration with GenAI evolved across the project: moving from learning about AI, to learning from AI, and learning with AI. SAC is often adopted as a method to tag a pedagogical sequencing heuristic when designing reflection and interview prompts about student-AI collaboration.

Three TMLT premises among seven are particularly relevant to this study: learner agency, technology affordances, and design-based active learning. First, learner agency, defined as learners' capacity to act purposefully and take ownership of their learning process (Biesta & Tedder, 2007), manifests in technology-mediated environments when they make decisions, adapt resources, and regulate their engagement with tools for their learning goals (Liu & Chao, 2018). These agentive practices resonate with Tondeur's (2012, 2025) active learning principles for pre-service teachers, where pre-service teachers as learners are positioned as co-designers rather than passive recipients of content, thereby reinforcing CK development as an active, situated process (Jeon et al., 2022). The SAC model further explicates the actualization of learner agency, illustrating progression from learning about AI, to learning from it, and ultimately to learning with it while each stage entails higher levels of agency and decisionmaking (Kim et al., 2022).

From the TMLT perspective, agency is a critical capacity that enables learners to recognize and purposefully enact technological affordances within a pedagogical context (Liu & Chao, 2018). By exercising agency, learners pursue disciplinary goals-for instance, in GenAI-supported lesson design, pre-service teachers may notice and leverage GenAI functions such as generating multimodal materials, providing feedback, or simulating communication tasks (Lee et al., 2025). In doing so, they can develop both TK and CK for the pedagogically informed GenAI usage (Moorhouse, 2024).

Design-based learning operationalizes TMLT principles in teacher education through iterative cycles of planning, creating, evaluating, and revising, during which learners integrate CK, PK, and TK in authentic design tasks (Bower, 2019; Tondeur et al., 2025; Wu & Chun, 2025). In this process, learner agency mediates the use of technological affordances, while technology acts as both resource and co-participant. For example, Lee and Jeon (2024) demonstrated that AI-integrated lesson design enhanced pre-service teachers' TK, supported CK-PK integration, and improved adaptive lesson planning. Such technologyenhanced design activities further help cultivate professional digital competencies that encompass both CK and PK (Lee et al., 2025; Moorhouse et al., 2024).

Therefore, TMLT offers a robust framework for examining how pre-service teachers' applied linguistics knowledge develops through agency, affordance recognition, and iterative design processes. The integration of the SAC model further captures the evolving dynamics of human-AI collaboration in mediating this learning. Guided by these perspectives, this study addresses two complementary research questions (RQs):

  • RQ1. How do pre-service teachers develop content knowledge through their engagement in iterative design processes using GenAI and the metaverse?
  • RQ2. In what ways do their technological knowledge and learner agency mediate content knowledge development in a technology-mediated learning environment?

Methods

The study adopted a qualitative approach to explore two RQs, as this methodology is particularly wellsuited for an in-depth exploration of participants' experiences and voices within their design works (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Dörnyei, 2007).

Context and Participants

This study was conducted in Spring 2025 as part of an undergraduate applied linguistics course at a university in Eastern China. The course, a compulsory component of the Applied Linguistics program, introduces concepts such as SLA, genre and discourse analysis, translanguaging, and technology use in applied linguistics, with applications to real-world contexts. Of the 39 Year 3 students enrolled in the course, we collected data from 23 students (6 males, 17 females, aged 21-23) who volunteered to share their activities for this study. They prepared to pursue careers as secondary- or university-level English teachers, consistent with the program's curriculum, which qualifies students for a teaching certificate in China.

The first author served as the course instructor, positioning the researcher as an insider to the instructional context. To address ethical considerations and potential power imbalances, participation in the study was voluntary, research data were not used for course assessment, and all reflective journals and interview data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Research Procedure: Brand and Showroom Design Project

Drawing on SQD and TMLT principles, we implemented a design-based learning project within an applied linguistics course that engaged pre-service teachers in authentic activities using GenAI and the metaverse. Following SQD, the project incorporated strategies such as linking theory and practice, instructor modeling, design tasks, collaboration, authentic practice, reflection, and feedback (Tondeur et al., 2012, 2025). From the TMLT perspective (Bower, 2019), the project was designed to (1) make key tool affordances explicit through modelling and guided experimentation with ChatGLM, a GenAI tool, and Spot as a metaverse platform, (2) promote learner agency via learner choice (brand topic and identity), goal-setting, and adjustment for accepting, rejecting, and modifying AI outputs, and (3) operationalize design-based active learning through repeated plan-create-evaluate-revise cycles culminating in a public showcase. Figure 1 summarizes how SQD strategies and focal TMLT premises (affordances, agency, design-based active learning) were enacted across the six stages of the project. To make student-AI collaboration effective for participants during the project, we also used the SAC viewpoint (Kim et al., 2022) as a pedagogical heuristic when sequencing reflective tasks especially during Stages 2 and 3. After being introduced to different concepts in applied linguistics, students developed a brand using ChatGLM and created a virtual showroom in the metaverse to showcase their designs. This project was selected because we expected it to cultivate students' creativity and provide opportunities to utilize applied linguistics concepts, such as genre and discourse analysis, to a branding project. Students worked in 16 groups of 2-3 members.

Data Collection

To address the two RQs, we used multiple data sources for triangulation (Dörnyei, 2007): reflective journals and semi-structured interviews. As part of their final portfolio, students submitted reflective journals in Week 11, designed to elicit critical reflections on the project and their learning experiences (Appendix A). After submission, they participated in 30-40 minute semi-structured interviews in English, which were recorded and transcribed verbatim (Appendix B). The interview protocol with four thematic sections was designed to elicit more in-depth description of (1) participants' CK application and internalization and (2) the ways they utilized GenAI and the metaverse platform as mediating resources (see Appendix C for this mapping). For this, TMLT constructs (Bower, 2019) informed the primary probes on affordance noticing, agentive decision-making, and design-based learning, whereas SAC (Kim et al., 2022) was used as a heuristic to probe student-AI collaboration (Kim & Cho, 2025; Lee et al., 2025). Importantly, the TMLT principles informed our analytic focus and structured prompts for eliciting reflective accounts: (1) CK development, including how participants applied linguistics concepts in their GenAI and metaverse designs and how their theoretical understanding evolved (Bartels, 2005; Beshah & Anshu, 2024); (2) technological affordances, focusing on how they identified and adopted GenAI and metaverse features for project goals (Bower, 2019; Jeon et al., 2022); (3) agency and TK, examining decision-making, initiative, and growth of TK through iterative engagement (Lee et al., 2025; Moorhouse, 2024); and (4) agency and CK, analyzing how agency influenced the internalization of CK and the link between theory and practice (Liu & Chao, 2018; Zhang et al., 2025).

Data Analysis

Qualitative data from the reflective journals and interviews were analyzed in parallel using thematic analysis, with the two data sources treated as complementary for interpretation, to identify emerging themes and patterns related to RQs (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Data analysis was conducted using a hybrid approach that encompasses both inductive and deductive analyses (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). Specifically, in the first cycle of inductive analysis, the authors repeatedly read the data for familiarization, then generated initial open codes inductively, guided by the two RQs, focusing on CK development and technology-mediated processes including affordances, learner agency, and TK. The authors took analytic memos to document emerging codes and interpretations. They then reviewed code labels, definitions, and illustrative excerpts to categorize codes into specific themes.

After inductive themes were determined, a second cycle of deductive analysis was conducted based on the two theories in this study (Bower, 2019; Kim & Cho, 2025; Kim et al., 2022). Themes and extracts were mapped to theoretical constructs from TMLT and to interpretive orientations from SAC; that is, the data were analyzed in relation to TMLT principles as the primary lens and interpreted using SAC orientations to enhance analytic depth (see Appendix C). This mapping allowed for specifying which TMLT principles were implicated in each theme and to characterize salient SAC orientations reflected in the data.

Finally, reflective journals and interviews were integrated into data analysis to support triangulation. Journals were primarily used to trace students' learning moments and design decisions, while interviews provided retrospective evidence such as elaboration, confirmation, or qualification. Evidence from both sources was then synthesized at the theme level, while themes were refined through complementary comparison rather than source-specific separation.

Inter-coder agreement for the double-coded subset reached Cohen's κ = .82. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the refined codebook was applied to the remaining data.

Findings

The analysis shows how pre-service teachers developed CK through a design-based learning project with GenAI and a metaverse platform. The findings show that CK development unfolded as an iterative process, supported by the affordances of the two tools and mediated by growth in TK and the enactment of learner agency. Appendix D presents an overview of how eight themes were coded, categorized, linked to interpretive lenses (TMLT and, where applicable, SAC), supported by evidence from the two data sources.

RQ1: CK Development through Iterative, Affordance-driven Design-based Learning

Regarding RQ1, the data showed that participants' CK development was influenced by their active engagement in iterative design tasks that leveraged GenAI and metaverse affordances. In contrast to lecture-based or case-study approaches typical of applied linguistics classes (Bartels, 2005), this project required students to embed disciplinary concepts into authentic, technology-mediated design work. Four themes emerged, reflecting how participants applied, refined, and expanded their CK through cultureoriented design, iterative artifact development, and strategic adoption of GenAI and metaverse affordances.

Applying CK in Culturally and Contextually Relevant Designs

Participants (n = 14) emphasized the importance of embedding applied linguistics knowledge, such as genre and discourse analysis, corpus linguistics, and SLA, into brand and showroom designs that they believe were culturally and contextually appropriate. This required bridging communicative goals with disciplinary concepts from the course and adapting language, visuals, and interactional framing to the sociocultural context of target audiences. For example, two students noted:

I designed my metaverse booth for a Korean cosmetics brand, making sure the slogans fit the cultural context. (Student 7, Reflective Journal)

When designing the slogans for our metaverse booth, I realized that simply translating persuasive expressions was not enough. I considered cultural expectations and how certain words might be interpreted differently by international audiences. (Student 11, Reflective Journal)

Similarly, 11 participants reported framing their metaverse design decisions around the expectations of audiences from different linguistic and cultural backgrounds. This often involved adjusting tone, register, and visual layout to promote inclusivity and accessibility, thereby strengthening the persuasive appeal of their projects, as one student explained:

I tailored the showroom and presentation style to match the expectations of international students who would potentially visit our virtual booth. (Student 3, Interview)

Refining CK through Iterative, Collaborative Design Processes

Many participants (n = 16) emphasized the value of iterative design cycles in refining the application of CK to their artifacts. Rather than producing a single draft, they engaged in repeated cycles of ideation, prototyping, feedback, and revision, which deepened their engagement with principles such as discourse structuring, audience accommodation, and multimodal alignment. Peer and tutor feedback often prompted them to revisit earlier decisions, leading to the restructuring of discourse moves or multimodal arrangements. Regarding this, two students reflected:

The process of drafting, feedback, and revision helped me connect the theoretical principles of the course with practical design choices more clearly. (Student 8, Reflective Journal)

Peer feedback showed me that my initial discourse moves did not match the audience's expectations, so I revised my strategy to create a more persuasive flow. (S14, Reflective Journal)

In addition, 12 participants reported trying multiple discourse and genre strategies to operationalize CK in their brands and showrooms. By testing variations in tone, design structure, and sequencing of showroom elements, they compared alternative GenAI outputs and selected the most persuasive versions:

I used ChatGLM to generate brand taglines and messages, then refined them to match the tone and persuasive styles we learned in the course, such as puns and translanguaging. (Student 15, Interview)

I created two alternative opening sections to see which would engage the audience more effectively. (Student 5, Reflective Journal)

GenAI affordances: Expanding CK through the Strategic Use of GenAI Affordances

Participants (n = 18) reported using diverse GenAI affordances in their brand and showroom designs, from content generation to cognitive support in brainstorming, expanding their discourse strategies, stylistic choices, and genre structures. They relied on prompt engineering techniques, selective adoption, and repeated refinement to align GenAI outputs with the persuasive and genre-specific goals of the project. One student explained:

I used ChatGLM to generate alternative introductions and then refined them to match genre principles. For example, I revised a slogan to add my own style with translanguaging and Chinese-English puns. (Student 21, Reflective Journal)

Eleven participants utilized GenAI as a feedback provider, selectively adopting its suggestions to improve their work. Sometimes they accepted recommendations; at other times they rejected them but refined their drafts using the insights gained, as two students explained:

ChatGLM suggested a more concise headline than my first draft, which I revised to fit the persuasive structure of our genre. It worked well in the end. (Student 6, Interview)

ChatGLM helped me make multiple versions of introductions very quickly. However, by looking at several alternatives side by side, I rejected some of them, acknowledging genre differences, such as how much background information was appropriate for this type of promotional discourse. (Student 13, Interview)

These processes show how GenAI's affordances, when critically employed, broadened the range and depth of participants' CK application in their design projects.

Metaverse Affordances: Applying CK for Crafting Immersive, Multimodal Showrooms

Participants (n = 15) emphasized the constructive role of metaverse affordances in applying their CK to design immersive, multimodal 3D environments. The spatial, designable, and interactive features of the platform enabled them to translate linguistic and genre-based decisions into embodied, navigable experiences. This required integrating discourse planning and advertising strategies with spatial design, multimodal embedding, and interactive sequencing for actualizing applied linguistics knowledge. For example, one student noted:

I embedded an interactive quiz into the metaverse booth to check visitors' understanding of our campaign message. (Student 9, Reflective Journal)

Others (n = 13) explained that Spot allowed them to embed multimedia resources, such as text, images, and video, to appeal to target audiences. This involved identifying the communicative function of each resource and selecting the most suitable modality to convey the message. With these affordances, students created showrooms that engaged visitors through multiple sensory and cognitive channels. This multimodal embedding made showrooms visually engaging and enhanced comprehensibility by offering multiple pathways to meaning. One participant explained:

We placed an embedded video in the welcome zone, so visitors could quickly grasp the brand story, applying what we learned about delivering messages through multimodal methods. (Student 2, Interview)

RQ2: Mediating roles of TK and Learner Agency in CK Development

While RQ1 examined how CK was applied through design activities and technological affordances, RQ2

focuses on the psychological processes that enabled such development (Liu & Chao, 2018). Specifically, participants' growth in TK and enactment of agency mediated the relationship between technology use and CK internalization (Lee, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). These mediating processes involved recontextualizing GenAI outputs, aligning TK with subject knowledge, co-constructing CK through distributed agency, and reflecting on ethical and critical language awareness (Godwin-Jones, 2024; Kim et al., 2022).

Agency-driven Recontextualization of GenAI Outputs

Some participants (n = 12) described moments in which they exercised agency to adapt, reject, or reframe GenAI-generated outputs to align with CK principles in applied linguistics. These decisions were not passive responses to technology but deliberate acts of control over the design process to meet genre conventions (e.g., business communication discourse in this context) and audience expectations. One participant highlighted the decision to reject GenAI-generated text that lacked cultural and audience engagement and to rewrite the section:

I edited ChatGLM's slogan because although grammatically correct, it missed our target audience and persuasive tone. I kept the structure but aligned the wording with our brand identity. (Student 11, Interview)

Another group (n = 7) emphasized agency in sequencing communicative elements within the metaverse booth. Rather than treating it as a static display, they considered how visitors moved through space and encountered persuasive elements in real time. For example, one student repositioned the call-to-action earlier in the design:

I redesigned the layout to position the call-to-action more prominently. Moving it from the end to an earlier point grabs visitors' attention faster and makes the campaign's objective clear from the start. (Student 4, Reflective Journal)

Mediating Role of TK Development

The development of TK, such as prompt engineering, GenAI feature integration, and multimodal editing, emerged as a key mediator between participants' use of technological affordances and CK internalization. Many (n = 21) reported that growing TK enabled them to better exploit GenAI and metaverse affordances, producing outputs aligned with the linguistic and genre principles taught in the course. One participant highlighted the role of prompt techniques in producing genre-appropriate outputs:

At first, the AI outputs were too generic and did not reflect the genre conventions we had studied. By refining my prompts with constraints, specifying tone, audience, and rhetorical purpose, I generated output much closer to our target genre. This showed me how prompt engineering itself applied discourse and genre principles in practice. (S10, Reflective Journal)

Sixteen participants also stressed the importance of TK in integrating different modes of communication into brand and showroom designs. Their accounts show that TK not only enabled tool use but mediated how multimodal outputs aligned with applied linguistics knowledge, ensuring accuracy, discourse structuring, and persuasive intent across modes. As one student reported, producing visuals and aligning them with the text required knowledge of genre-sensitive coherence in linguistic aspects:

Working with visuals from ChatGLM was not just about inserting images. I had to merge them with my text, checking that colors, imagery, and slogans reinforced rather than distracted from the message. This required thinking about genre conventions in multimodal terms, where visuals and language had to work together for a coherent and convincing campaign. (S06, Reflective Journal)

Distributed Agency and Collaborative CK Reconstruction

CK reconstruction often occurred through distributed agency, where design decisions were co-shaped by students, peers, and GenAI rather than individuals alone. Participants (n = 8) described how GenAI

outputs, peer suggestions, and their own CK knowledge were negotiated to reach effective design choices. Instead of treating AI outputs as final, they valued group discussion and integration. One participant noted how peer critique directly informed CK adjustments:

I generated several images with ChatGLM, but my partner said they didn't fully match our message. After discussion, we combined my revised visuals with her text to create a more balanced design. (Student 20, Interview)

Similarly, six participants described GenAI outputs as flexible starting points for collaborative remixing. Rather than accepting slogans verbatim, groups reworded drafts through critique and disciplinary knowledge to better resonate with audiences. As one explained how group discussion influenced their communicative awareness through content remixing:

My partner suggested adapting our GenAI slogan to be more contextually suitable. Through discussion we remixed it into a version that better reflected our target audience. (Student 5, Interview)

Reflective Inquiry and Critical Language Awareness

Some participants ( n = 6) highlighted reflective inquiry as a way to question and revise linguistic choices, integrating critical language awareness into their design processes. Reflection was treated as active metacognitive inquiry into whether outputs aligned with genre conventions, ethical standards, and inclusive communication. This led to revisions that addressed audience diversity and the social implications of language use. One student noted this by reconsidering the inclusivity of GenAI-generated content:

When I looked back at the GenAI text, I saw it was too generalized and could even stereotype. After reflecting, I rewrote it to better respect the cultural backgrounds of our audience. (Student 8, Reflective Journal)

Others (n = 4) focused on ethical aspects of communication, revising AI-generated slogans that they found too forceful. Through reflective inquiry, two students in the following extracts reconsidered the ethical stance of the message and rephrased it to allow their audience to form their own interpretation:

At first, the slogan sounded pushy, like we were forcing people to act. After reflecting, I revised it to stay persuasive but respectful, so the audience felt invited rather than pressured. (Student 16, Interview)

After reviewing the AI-generated description, I felt it was too general and relied on exaggerated claims, such as '50 million images,' which could damage credibility. So, I revised the text to include more culturally appropriate information and realized that responsible authorship requires critical evaluation. (Student 23, Reflective Journal)

Discussion and implications

Given that the integrated use of GenAI and metaverse technologies has been unexplored in teacher training, particularly for enhancing pre-service teachers' CK, this study demonstrated that a technologyenhanced, design-based approach can develop CK. The findings indicate that student-AI collaboration in design projects activates learner agency in reflecting on theoretical concepts and technology use in realworld applications (Kim et al., 2023; Jeon et al., 2022; Tondeur et al., 2025).

Regarding RQ1, which explored how iterative GenAI-metaverse design fostered pre-service teachers' CK, the findings indicate that CK developed when abstract applied linguistics concepts were enacted in audience-specific artefacts through authentic design rather than taught as declarative knowledge (Bartels, 2005; Zhang et al., 2025). Within iterative cycles of planning, creating, evaluating, and revising, GenAI and the metaverse served as mediating tools that turned perceived affordances into concrete design actions (Bower, 2019; Jeon et al., 2022). GenAI required students to make explicit choices about register, stance, and cohesion, while metaverse affordances such as spatial layout and interactive displays enabled them to stage and experiment with discourse and genre meanings in immersive contexts (Hwang et al., 2025; Lee & Kim, 2025). Reflections further showed that this design-based active learning was scaffolded by SQD-informed strategies (e.g., modelling, authentic tasks, collaboration, and reflection) that supported CK growth beyond technocentric training (Tondeur et al., 2025; Wu & Chun, 2025). Consistent with the SAC perspective, participants also perceived GenAI as a co-designer, moving from learning about or from AI to learning with AI as they critiqued and adapted its outputs (Kim et al., 2022; Moorhouse et al., 2024). Overall, these findings extend previous research by demonstrating how affordance-driven design tasks with GenAI and the metaverse can support CK development in teacher education (Lee & Kim, 2025; Moorhouse et al., 2024).

Our data indicate that the effectiveness of design-based teacher training hinged on iteration: repeated plan-create-evaluate-revise cycles contributed to CK internalization (Choi et al., 2024). In these cycles, participants compared GenAI outputs, evaluated register and audience fit, and revised discourse moves and multimodal layouts to meet communicative goals. Using GenAI to refine drafts and the metaverse to adjust spatial narratives, they enacted applied linguistics knowledge in ways that were open to team discussion and negotiation. Aligned with TMLT-informed teacher education studies, our findings suggest that scaffolded, iterative practice-where learners agentively orchestrated affordances-fostered CK internalization effectively (Bower, 2019; Tondeur et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025). While prior studies credited iteration in GenAI and metaverse design separately (Lee et al., 2025), our study extends this by showing how their combined affordances-the designability of GenAI for draft generation and the constructibility of metaverse platforms for spatial reconfiguration-offer student teachers additional theory-to-practice opportunities for CK application (Lee & Wu, 2024; Wu & Chun, 2025).

As to RQ2, which examined how learner agency mediates the relation between TK and CK in a GenAImetaverse design context, our analysis shows that it was agentive orchestration rather than the tools themselves that transformed affordances into CK growth (Bower, 2019; Chen, 2022; Jeon & Lee, 2023). Participants enacted agency by strategically adapting GenAI outputs and engaging in design-based revision, purposefully accepting, rejecting, or customizing text and visuals against diverse criteria of genre, audience, and ethics, thereby mediating TK into CK. Viewed through the SAC lens (Kim et al., 2022), this shift from learning from AI to learning with AI positioned GenAI as a co-designer whose drafts invited participants' critique and refinement, turning potentially passive knowledge into active practice (Lee & Kim, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Extending prior work on the role of agency in actualizing affordances (Bower, 2019; Lee et al., 2025), our findings locate agency within strategic iterations that operationalized CK criteria in multimodal artefacts, linking applied linguistics theory to genre-related discourse decisions (Bartels, 2005; Lee & Kim, 2025).

The findings further show that participants' collaborative design with GenAI and the metaverse can be framed through the lens of distributed agency, where action is shared across students, GenAI, and the platform, and emerges relationally among human and non-human actors (Godwin-Jones, 2024). In this study, AI outputs functioned as shared objects around which peers exercised co-reflexivity, interrogating, revising, and recontextualizing the outputs to meet genre and audience expectations (Godwin-Jones, 2024; Kahn, 2014). Group showrooms exemplified this process, as the participants modified and embedded multimodal artefacts in 3D spaces, a dynamic activity consistent with resistanceaccommodation cycles in human-technology interactions (Högström et al., 2024). Notably, in these cycles of interacting with GenAI and the metaverse as non-human actors in the given network, students divided responsibilities: some generated prompts and screened AI outputs, while others refined language, adapted visuals, or aligned content with audience expectations, taking complementary roles such as prompt initiator, editor, or presenter. Unlike earlier studies that explored distributed agency in online forums (Sandoval-Cruz et al., 2022) or PBL-based contexts (Zhang et al., 2025), our research operationalizes it in L2 teacher education with GenAI and the metaverse, showing how team-level critique and re-authoring of AI outputs enabled collaborative CK reconstruction. This moves beyond technocentric accounts of tools (Godwin-Jones, 2024) or individual-level views of engagement (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Kahn, 2014). This suggests that cultivating distributed agency in this way can contribute to pre-service teachers' professional development by fostering critical design skills, collaborative authorship, and adaptive expertise in technology-mediated environments (Godwin-Jones, 2024; Högström et al., 2024).

Finally, the data also highlights how reflective inquiry enhanced critical language awareness, allowing students to examine GenAI outputs for ethical stance, audience appropriateness, and genre fit. Reflection was used as a metacognitive strategy through which participants critiqued and revised their work in light of communicative appropriateness and cultural inclusivity (Chen, 2023; Jeon et al., 2022; Tondeur et al., 2025). These practices corroborate prior research that frames critical digital and AI literacy as essential teacher-education competencies, highlighting not only technical proficiency but also the ability to evaluate, critique, and redesign digital texts for equity and inclusion (Lee et al., 2026; Moorhouse et al., 2024). By embedding reflective inquiry in authentic design tasks, this study thus linked CK development with professional growth through cultivating ethical judgment and critical literacy to interrogate how digital tools shape discourse and its implications for fairness, authorship, and cultural inclusivity (Moorhouse et al., 2024; Pei et al., 2025). This discussion is associated with concerns about over-reliance and metacognitive laziness in GenAI-mediated learning (Fan et al., 2025). Rather than treating GenAI as an answer tool, in this study, the participants kept engaging in the calibration of outputs, justification against CK-based criteria, and collaborative critique, which required them to externalize reasoning and sustain metacognitive monitoring. This suggests that design-based active learning with critical inquiries can mitigate unreflective dependence by positioning GenAI as a draft-producing co-designer that invites scrutiny and revision, rather than as a substitute for disciplinary thinking (Lee & Jeon, 2023; Moorhouse et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This study explored how a design-based project integrating GenAI and a metaverse platform supported the development of CK in applied linguistics among pre-service English teachers. The combined affordances of the two technologies engaged participants in iterative design cycles, giving them opportunities to apply their applied linguistics knowledge in practice by testing, justifying, and refining design decisions in relation to genre, audience, and ethical considerations. The findings show that learner agency, which is enacted through goal setting, selective uptake, and revision, mediated how technological affordances were translated into design actions guided by disciplinary knowledge (e.g., discourse and genre analysis for business communication), supporting the internalization of CK beyond declarative knowledge (Bartels, 2005; Bower, 2019; Lee & Wu, 2024).

The findings provide both theoretical and pedagogical implications. Theoretically, the study specifies how TK can inform CK through agentive mediation in design-based projects, clarifying an affordance-andagency process of learning in teacher education (Lee et al., 2025; Bower, 2019). Building on this account, future research could examine this TK-CK pathway by testing scaffolds for agency, such as goal setting, calibration, and critique protocols (Jeon et al., 2022), and by modelling the impact of technology mediation on performance-based CK (Tondeur et al., 2025). The findings also underscore iteration as a key mechanism of CK internalization: the plan-create-evaluate-revise cycle can bind design decisions to genre, audience, and ethics criteria, stabilizing conceptual uptake (Campbell et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2024). Pedagogically, technology-enhanced teacher education should embed scaffolded, design-based iteration (e.g., modelling, authentic tasks, collaboration, reflection) to cultivate CK as well as TK, thereby supporting disciplinary expertise and professional readiness (Lee & Kim, 2025; Moorhouse et al., 2024; Tondeur et al., 2025). Also, teacher education curricula should incorporate critical AI literacy as an explicit objective, ensuring that pre-service teachers can evaluate whether AI outputs align with disciplinary demands. Discipline-specific reflective tasks and rubrics that prompt students to justify AIassisted designs with CK may further support the transformation of subject-matter knowledge into principled classroom practice (Pei et al., 2025).

This study has several limitations. First, its findings have limited generalizability, as they derive from a single course in one semester and rely on qualitative perception-based data. Future research could incorporate performance-based CK measures (e.g., rubric-based evaluations of artefacts), pre/post comparisons of instructional effectiveness, and longitudinal approaches tracking novice teachers' practices to assess both immediate and lasting impacts on teacher learning. Second, the results are tied to the specific affordances of the GenAI and metaverse platforms, since students designed advertising brands and structured activities around particular theoretical constructs. This may restrict the applicability of this study's findings to other tasks or subject areas. Future studies could address this by testing the approach with different project types, disciplinary domains, and technological platforms.
