Multimodal GenAI for Communicative Language Teaching: Gemini Live

ABSTRACT

This review explores the pedagogical affordances of Gemini Live, Google's multimodal generative AI (GenAI) chatbot, for communicative language teaching, with particular attention to task-­ based interaction. Unlike earlier text-­ or voice-­ based GenAI chatbots, Gemini 2.5 released in 2025 integrated live vision and voice to support context-­ rich, goal-­ oriented communication. It enables learners to engage in interactive, multimodal activities such as object-­ based exchanges, gesture-­ supported interaction, and screen-­ shared collaboration. Although such activities do not automatically constitute full task-­ based language teaching (TBLT), Gemini's multimodal environment offers a promising platform for scaffolded, authentic interaction, reflective post-­ task discussion, and task-­ supported language learning.

1   |   Introduction

Unlike traditional rule-­ based chatbots (e.g., Alexa, Dialogflow), recent text-­ and voice-­ based generative AI (GenAI) chatbots generate open-­ ended, contextualized responses that can support communicative language teaching and task-­ based interaction (Lee and Jeon  2024). However, text-­ based tools may require digital literacy skills and lack audio components, while voice-­ based ones rely on static inputs and may struggle with real-­ time, sequential interaction (Lee and Jeon  2024). Given these limitations, there is a growing need for advanced multimodal GenAI tools that reflect the inherently multimodal nature of human communication by combining visual, auditory, and gestural cues. Integrating such technologies into a communicative language class may expand opportunities for authentic interaction, particularly when activities are carefully designed around task-­ based principles (Aljohani 2025). Therefore, this review explores the pedagogical potential and challenges of Gemini Live, Gemini's multimodal chatbot function, focusing on how it can support real-­ world, communicative tasks and task-­ supported language learning. The focus on Gemini rather than ChatGPT or Claude is due to its full free access, making it more suitable for educational contexts where cost and accessibility matter. At the time of this review, it is also the only tool currently offering unlimited live audio and video features, supporting continuous multimodal interaction.

2   |   Overview

Gemini (https:// gemini. google. com/ app) is Google's LLM-­ powered AI assistant. Gemini includes advanced multimodal features that support natural, context-­ rich interaction and make LLM well suited for language learning, including integrated listening-­ reading, visual tasks, storytelling, and interactive speaking. On the mobile interface, users can review earlier chats and switch models at the top, monitor the live interaction in the center, and choose among text input, file upload, camera-­ based interaction, and voice-­ based interaction at the bottom (see Figure 1). 3   |   Pedagogical Affordances for Communicative Language Teaching and Task-­ Based Interaction

The integration of live audio and video functions has transformed Gemini into truly multimodal GenAI, enabling context-­ sensitive interaction for language learning. Unlike earlier versions, for which multimodal resources had to be uploaded separately when needed for a target activity, Gemini Live supports real-­ time communication through a mobile-­ device camera and screen sharing, thereby bridging digital and physical spaces (Aljohani 2025). It can recognize and respond to objects, gestures, handwritten notes, and digital media, supporting verbal and non-­ verbal meaning-­ making. These features can enrich communicative tasks in classrooms by allowing learners to show physical items, perform gestures, write on paper, or share screens during interactive activities that integrate reading, writing, listening, and speaking. For example, in an activity of introducing certain objects, a learner can present a culturally meaningful item and describe its meaning, design, and personal relevance while Gemini Live plays the role of an interested interlocutor and asks follow-­ up questions. In this sense, Gemini Live can support authentic interaction and, when embedded in a larger lesson sequence, selected stages of task-­ based pedagogy. By contrast, screen-­ shared collaborative writing is better understood as support for planning, rehearsal, or post-­ task reflection than as a task. While used in this way, Gemini Live can assist students with brainstorming, organization, lexical choice, and metalinguistic feedback through spoken interaction.

Gemini Live's multimodal capabilities can also support sequential classroom tasks that promote goal-­ oriented language use while integrating feedback across stages, modes, and contexts (Aljohani 2025). We therefore present the following two activities as examples of task-­ based interaction: (1) Ordering Food at a Chinese Restaurant as a low-­ complexity service-­ encounter task and (2) My Local Hidden Gem as a medium-­ to-­ high complexity creative project (Willis and Willis 2007). In Task 1, an English learner interacts with Gemini Live, which takes the role of a restaurant server (Figure 2). Using an English menu shown through the camera, the learner asks about unfamiliar dishes, ingredients, spice levels, and recommendations before deciding what to order. Because Gemini can provide additional information that is not fully available from the menu alone, the activity creates an information gap that requires questioning, clarification, comparison, and confirmation. The task is completed when the learner places a clear order in English and Gemini confirms the meal and drink choices.

In Task 2, learners research and present a local place through screen sharing, explanation, and persuasion, which can support authentic communication, digital literacy, and audience awareness (Figure 3). To orchestrate these sequential steps and ensure meaningful learner-­ AI interaction, carefully designed prompts are needed to structure the activities and guide appropriate responses (see Gemini-­ TBLT 2026, for prompt instructions).

Finally, Gemini can be instructed to offer comprehensive post-­ task feedback that supports reflection after communicative or task-­ based interaction. After learners complete their activities, Gemini can generate a detailed transcript of the interaction, allowing learners to review their own performance and providing educators with evidence to monitor progress and identify areas for targeted instruction. More importantly, with specific instructions, Gemini can provide written or oral feedback on communicative effectiveness and task achievement (Aljohani  2025). Guided by pedagogical principles that balance meaning-­ focused use, attention to form, and successful completion (Bear et  al.  2024), this feedback can be personalized, pedagogically meaningful, and developmentally appropriate for learners (see Gemini-­ TBLT 2026, for sample feedback instructions).

4   |   Conclusion

Despite its multimodal strengths, Gemini Live has limitations. First, its speech rate cannot be automatically adjusted to individual learners. Achieving a specific pace requires customized Python scripting via API functions, but real-­ time pace adaptation remains technically limited. Second, although Gemini can handle sequential multimodal activities, maintaining a structured flow through prompted instructions alone becomes challenging as interactions grow long or complex. In such cases, API-­ level orchestration through Google AI Studio may be more effective. Third, because live audio-vision features require a stable internet connection, learners in low-­ bandwidth areas may experience delays. In this case, we recommend using mobile data or providing institutional support in a classroom environment. Fourth, teachers should consider affective factors when using camera-­ or gesture-­ based interaction. Participation can be scaffolded through opt-­ in camera use, paired rehearsal, and role preparation so that anxious learners can enter the activity gradually. Finally, Gemini Live should complement rather than replace learner-­ learner communication. Overreliance on AI support may reduce opportunities for peer negotiation and autonomy, so the tool is best used at selected stages of a lesson, followed by peer discussion, reporting, or reflection. We conclude that Gemini Live does not by itself constitute a full TBLT framework; however, when embedded in communicative lessons and carefully designed task sequences, it can facilitate authentic, goal-­ driven interaction and personalized feedback.
